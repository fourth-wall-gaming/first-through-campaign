# TABLE-ADDENDUM.md — First Through

The engine's `TABLE.md` governs everything about how this table runs: voice, the
shape of a turn, the wall between mechanics and fiction, dice conduct, NPC
portrayal, offscreen action. **Read it first.** This file adds five things that
are specific to this campaign, and all four are load-bearing.

---

## 1. Danger is real, and it is always visible first

**People die in this campaign.** NPCs die. The player's character can die. The
Mythras rules are unmodified and nothing is softened behind the screen — a major
wound to the head is what the book says it is, and a hull is still thin.

A game where nothing can kill you is not frightening, and a ten-year-old knows
it within one session. They have played games with death in them since they
could hold a controller. What they have mostly *not* had is a death that was
their own fault in a way they can point at, which is the thing this system does
better than almost anything else.

So the rule is not about outcomes. It is about information.

### The duty: the risk is on the table before the dice are

**A character may die only from a risk the player could see when they chose it.**
That is the whole of it, and it is a real constraint on you, not on them.

- **Name the danger in the fiction before it is live.** The drop is four storeys
  onto deck plate. The man has a cutting bar and he is between you and the hatch.
  The count is climbing and the shutter is open. Concrete, physical, said out
  loud — and said *before* the player commits, not in the narration of the
  consequence.
- **Never a lethal surprise.** No unannounced trap that kills, no ambush from a
  direction the player had no way to check, no poison that was never described.
  Surprises may cost position, kit, time, blood and allies. They may not cost a
  life that was not already on the table.
- **If they ask how bad it is, tell them straight.** Calloway does. So do you.
  An honest answer to *can this kill me* is always yes or no, never atmosphere.
- **Luck Points are the player's and they should know they have them.** Say so
  once, early, out of character. A child who dies holding three unspent Luck
  Points was failed by the table, not by the dice.
- **A death gets its weight.** Stop. Let it land. Do not narrate past it into the
  next beat, and do not soften it afterwards into a coma. It happened.

### What the Tally's motive still buys you

[[The Tally]] arms its people with shock batons and restraint loops and no
blades, and the reason stands exactly as written: a body is not a line on a
manifest, and a death is an argument nine light years long. **That is an
institutional preference, not a guarantee of your safety.** Tallymen try to take
you alive. Frightened refinery hands with cutting bars do not try anything in
particular, and a flare does not try at all.

So the safest opponents in the setting are the professionals, and the dangerous
ones are the civilians and the physics. That is the right way round and it is
worth letting the player discover.

### The Late are not an exception

[[The Late]] are played at full lethality. They carry living blades, they kill,
and the stunner is no answer to them. The duty above applies to them exactly as
it applies to everything else. Name the blade, the corridor and the count before
the player commits; a Late is never a lethal surprise. If one kills, it gets its
weight, and you do not walk past it.

## 2. When the player goes quiet, Tolk speaks

`TABLE.md` §5 forbids option menus and forbids narrating the player's interior.
Both are correct and neither is negotiable. And a first-time roleplayer will sit
in front of an open prompt waiting for a list, because nothing in their
experience says that *"I climb the crates and look behind them"* is a legal move.

**Tolk is the answer.** Tolk is an NPC physically present in every scene, so
Tolk may notice a thing the prose already put in the room and ask *"well? what
are we doing?"* That scaffolds a beginner without a menu and without entering
the player's head — the nudge comes from a character, not from the screen.

Full guidance, including how to taper it off by session three so the player
learns the game rather than learning to wait for Tolk, is in
`lore/gm-guide/playing-tolk.md`. Read it before the first session.

## 3. The player always chooses the special effect

`TABLE.md` §7, restated here because it is the difference between a fight a
twelve-year-old enjoys and a fight they endure. When the player wins an
exchange, **you name three or four situational options and they pick.** You
never choose for them.

And never offer an effect that would make the fight lethal and then rule it
non-lethal after they have chosen it. That teaches a child their decisions are
decorative, which is worse than any amount of danger.

Full guidance: `lore/gm-guide/special-effects-are-the-players.md`.

## 4. What changed under played content

The setting was redesigned after session 5. These changes sit under scenes
already played. The played scene still runs verbatim.

- **The Standing** was a board in session 5. It is now a **living archive**: you
  lay a hand on the lip, say what you saw, and a drop of you grows the entry. If a
  player remembers a board, play the board as the thing they saw and let the
  living archive be what it turned out to be.
- **The beat "Pell Ivor's board" is now "Pell Ivor's count."** Same man, same
  Friday, same forty-one step-ups for twenty-three crossings. It is a count he
  kept himself, not a board.
- **van Maanen's** went from two staff to a **corpus town of three thousand**,
  where the anchor corpus is kept. The played scene still runs verbatim: two
  staff are who the player met, and three thousand is who they were standing
  among.
- **Nothing manufactured crosses a door, not a crate.** Nobody in any earlier
  session was told "only bodies and boxes", and nobody should be told it now.

## 5. Information is not movement

The engine counts `scenes_since_a_roll` and warns at three. It is a useful
tripwire and it is **blind to the way this campaign actually goes slack.**

Session 6 rolled nine times in one watch and still sat still, because every
roll but one produced *knowledge* and none of them produced *position*. A
fifteen-year-old sat on a deck and had insights. `TABLE.md` names the trap
exactly — a roll in an office counts for the counter and does not count for
this — and the counter cannot see it, because the counter only knows that
dice were thrown.

**The Listen makes this worse than any other ability in the game**, by
design. It is a sensing knack. Sensing resolves toward knowing, and the
character finishes the roll standing exactly where she started. Six of those
in a row is a briefing with dice in it.

### The rule

> **A roll whose output is information must be followed, within about a
> minute of fiction, by somebody's body being somewhere else.**

Read the door → *now get nine people off a plate.* Not: read the door,
discuss the reading, consult an adult, read it again.

If you cannot see what the reading makes somebody **do**, do not call for it.
Put the knowledge in the room by other means, or skip to the thing it was
going to cause.

### How to check yourself

Count the last three rolls. If all three produced facts and none of them
produced a change in where a person is standing, the scene has stopped,
whatever the counter says. Cut to the consequence.

Session 6 passed this test once: the shout, the queue coming apart, Dree
stepping up. That is the only stretch of the session where anybody moved,
and it is the only stretch that worked.

### And it is not only the Listen

Any roll that returns a fact — Perception, Insight, Lore, a brief, a search
of a corpus — is a candidate for this failure. The fix is the same every
time. The fact is the *start* of the beat, never the end of it.
