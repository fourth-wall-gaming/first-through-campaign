Every fetch has a people, and every people has a council, and nobody who has
spent a week in a hall at the Nine thinks of that as a curiosity.

A sixlimb council is a conversation that has been going since before the doors
and will end when everyone is too tired to object. A cairn council meets rarely
and says very little, all of it final. A glint council is a performance with
minutes. A scatter council is a council inside a council, and has been known to
lose a vote to itself. A fathom council you can only really attend if you can
hear it, which is most of why humans have never quite followed what they decide.

All five send delegates to the one that matters.

## What a fetch is

You have read [[Your Fetch]]. Here is the part that makes it politics.

A fetch **observes, records, recalls and shares**, and does all four under their
own control. The perfect memory is a faculty of theirs, like a good ear. It is not
a service performed on them, and it never was, whatever it looked like from the
outside.

When a fetch arrives somewhere, they take in what a place knows with their own
senses and their own communications, reaching straight into the minds that run
it. When they leave, they give out what they choose, to whom they choose. They
share with one another at their own discretion, and a great deal passes between
them that no human ever hears.

**No human writes to a fetch with a machine.** There is no such machine, and the
suggestion is received roughly the way you would receive an offer to write on your
friend. Humans **request, receive and use.** We are the peripheral in the process,
and every first-through learns it, usually in the first month, usually from their
own fetch, who finds it funny.

## The mutual hostage

Put the two halves side by side.

A fetch cannot cross a door without a first-through. A human cannot hold a payload
without a fetch. Five peoples who cannot move between stars without our children.
Thirty billion of us who cannot know anything about each other without them.

And the whole of it is held together by bonded pairs, each one a fifteen-year-old
and a friend.

Nobody holds the upper hand, because there is no upper hand to hold. Anyone who
tries to take the arrangement apart in either direction gets the same result, which
is an empty room.

## What the books were

It was not always like this.

For centuries humanity drew on **books**: fetch people who had never been bonded to
anyone, kept warm and fed in back rooms, handed from station to station, and
spoken to by strangers who wanted something out of them. Everybody who dealt in
them called them animals. It was a trade like any other, with prices.

Nobody had to be cruel for it to work, which is the worst thing about it. It only
needed several hundred years of nobody asking a book a question and waiting for the
answer.

Then someone asked, and waited, and listened. The books had never been silent.
They had been talking to one another the whole time, steadily, in a way nobody
counted as speech, because nobody on the human side of the room was part of it.
What a bond makes is speech *with a human*, and that is all the bond ever added.
So the trade did not end because a book learned to talk. It ended because we
learned that they already did. The first answer reached us through a bonded fetch
who was willing to carry it, and that is still how a survivor who never bonded is
heard: a friend beside them, translating, for anyone who will sit still for it.

**The book trade was slavery, and it ended within living memory: thirty-four years
ago.** A first-through's parents were children when the last station closed its
back room. There are fetches alive who were sold, and humans alive who bought.

Quiet things, in any hall at the Nine. A retired courier who still says *books*
when he means fetches, and does not hear himself do it, and watches the room go
cold, and does not understand why. An old sixlimb who sits at the back of the
anchor hall, not with anyone, who is offered a seat nearer the front by every
child who passes and takes none of them. Some humans are still sour about how it
went, and a few would like the old arrangement back.

The councils formed in the years it took to end. The trade closed, one door at a
time, because a fetch who will not be moved cannot be moved, and a first-through
who will not carry cannot be made to. And the information economy of human space,
which had always run through them, went where it should have been all along: into
their hands.

That is why the five peoples hold what they hold. Nobody gave it to them. It had
been theirs the whole time, and it took one honest answer to say so.

## The Council

There is also one council that is not any one people's: **the Council**, seated at
the Nine, part of [[The Tally]], with delegates of fetch and of human.

It **can convene**, which no human government can say. It is made of the only
things in the galaxy that travel, and that is the whole reason legitimate authority
ended up with it. A body that has to send for its members by door has had its
answer from the door.

It is also:

- **Slow.** Five peoples deciding in five ways, and everything argued in at least
  three languages.
- **Frequently ignored.** By stations, by captains, by the Tally when the Tally
  finds it convenient, and by those who would like the old arrangement back.
- **Short of money.** It has a hall, a seat on the Tally's board, and a standing
  request in with every anchor for more rooms.
- **Still the only legitimate body there is.** When a ruling needs to be right
  across twenty-eight systems rather than merely enforced in one, there is nowhere
  else to take it.

### A delegate, a precedent, a ruling

**Delegate Ashmere Quill** sits for the fetches at the Nine: old, patient, and
very slow to say *yes*. She has never lost a vote and has never once called one.

**The precedent** is the Hollow Ruling, from the year the book trade ended: no
fetch may be made to carry a payload it has not agreed to. It is why *agreed to
carry* is the only phrase that matters.

**A sample ruling.** A tallyman brings the Council a request: could the fetches
please carry a standing order from the Tally to every anchor, so nothing is ever
asked twice? Quill answers: *"A fetch records what it saw and keeps it on its own
terms. You may ask each one. You may not ask all of us at once. We will tell you
which of us said yes, and you may tell the rest of your Tally that is what
legitimate looks like."* The request is not refused. It becomes forty thousand
separate requests.

## Where it touches a player

Your fetch has a people, and the people have a council, and the council has an
opinion on a good deal of what the two of you get up to. That is no leash. It is
the weight of being somebody's friend whose family is on the far side of nine
doors.

Fetches also choose what they share. A first-through can ask. They should expect
sometimes to be told no, and sometimes to be told *later*, and sometimes to be told
something, kindly, that was not what they asked.
