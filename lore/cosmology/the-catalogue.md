Every station has a telescope and every station has an argument about it.

The argument is nine hundred years old and it is about **the Catalogue**: the
list of worlds that are the right size, at the right distance from the right
kind of star, to have weather and water and a sky. Two hundred and ninety
planets sit in the habitable zone by the broad reckoning. Forty-five of them
are rocky.

Not one of them has a door.

## Why the Reach could not see them

This is the part that is hard to read and it is nobody's fault.

The instruments that chose where three thousand ships were aimed could see
**big planets, close in, around bright stars, going round fast.** That is what
the methods of the day were good at. A small temperate world at a comfortable
distance from a sun like ours is the single hardest thing to detect, because it
is faint, it passes rarely, and it barely tugs its star at all.

So the First Reach aimed at what it could resolve, which was not the same as
what was worth having, and nobody in that century could have known the
difference. They were not careless. They were working at the edge of their
sight and the edge of their sight had a shape, and nine hundred years later you
can lay the Causeway over that shape and see it.

That is why a traveller steps out of a door into a flare star's shelter, a slag
terrace, four scorched pebbles, a dead white dwarf with a records office on a
rock, and a dim red dwarf nobody can farm. Not because the galaxy is poor.
Because those were the ones that showed up.

## Where the argument actually is

Nobody agrees where the edges of the zone are, and they never have.

Take a world a little too close to its star and you can argue it is a Venus
that never happened. Take one a little too far and you can argue for a thicker
atmosphere. The conservative reckoning gives you a short list. The broad one --
anything from a tenth of Earth's sunlight to five times it -- gives you a list
several times longer, and about a third of the Causeway's observatories use it.

So a station will tell you the Catalogue has forty-five worlds on it, and the
station through the next door will tell you it has a hundred and sixty, and
both of them are reading the same sky and neither of them is lying. People have
made careers out of moving one world from one list to the other.

**Nothing turns on it.** That is what makes it bearable enough to argue about
over a galley table for nine centuries. There is no road to any of them either
way.

## The number nobody says out loud

One light year takes twenty years. See [[Twenty Years to the Light Year]].

A wet world thirty light years out is a six-hundred-year voyage. You would have
to spend a civilisation to go, the way the First Reach spent one, and everybody
who left would be dead before it arrived, and the people who arrived would be
strangers who had inherited the bill.

It has been proposed, formally, eleven times. It has never once been funded.

## And one of them is about to have a door

[[LHS 1140]] is on the Catalogue and on every chart, drawn in, with a date
against it.

It is the only world on that list that anybody has ever aimed a ship at, and
only because the ship was aimed nine hundred and seventy-five years ago at a
star, by people who had no idea what was orbiting it.

The planet is a rocky super-Earth at a temperate distance from a small quiet
sun, and the current reading of its light says there may be an atmosphere on
it. *May.* Every observatory in the Causeway has looked and the argument about
what they are looking at is the loudest argument in the sciences.

Next year it stops being an argument, because somebody is going to walk through
a door and stand on it.

Which is why every faction in human space has an opinion about who that
somebody should be, and why a fifteen-year-old with a knack is worth more to
certain people than they have yet worked out.
