Grown into the collar of every weave, about the size of a thumbnail, slightly
warmer than the cloth around it.

You speak to it under your breath and the mind that runs the hull answers you,
in the voice it uses for you and nobody else. Everybody has one from about six
years old. It is not kit, it is not a privilege, and nobody thinks about it any
more than you think about a light switch.

*Ask your ear* is what an adult says to a child who has asked them something
they could have found out themselves.

## What it is not

It is not a device you own and it does not hold anything.

There is nothing in it. No store, no record, no mind of its own, nothing you
could read if you cut it open and nothing anybody could steal. Take it out of a
hull with a mind in it and it is a warm lump of collar. Everything it ever
seemed to know belonged to something enormous that was listening.

Which means the thing a first-through has to learn in week one is that **your
ear is not yours.** It is the near end of somebody else, and the somebody else
is a person with nine centuries of manners and no authority to lie to you.

## What it is for

Ordinary life, which is most of what anybody uses it for. Where is my sister.
What is the trim on nine. Who has the bay after me. What did Calloway say the
rota was. Read me the hall traffic. Wake me at third.

And **attestation**, which is the part that matters and which nobody performs
deliberately. A mind can hear you because you are wearing its ear, so anything
you say out loud anywhere on a hull is said — permanently, publicly, in your own
voice, askable by anyone aboard. See [[Lading and Attestation]].

People who grew up elsewhere never quite believe this and keep looking for the
switch. There is no switch. There is only the collar of the garment, and taking
your weave off to have a conversation is the most conspicuous thing a person can
do on a station.

## And it dies at a door

It crosses, because it is alive and part of the weave, and that is the cruellest
engineering in the setting.

You step onto the plate talking. You step off it into silence — not static,
*silence*, the way a room goes when the thing you were leaning on is not there.
There is no mind on the far side, or there is one and it has never met you, or
there is nothing on the far side at all. No signal crosses a door and no signal
ever will.

A first-through's whole working life is bracketed by that: the moment it goes,
and the moment it comes back on the way home, which happens before you have
finished stepping off the plate and which nobody has ever got used to.

Couriers talk to a dead ear anyway. All of them. It is the single most
universal habit in the service, everybody does it, everybody knows everybody
does it, and it is never once mentioned in a hall.
