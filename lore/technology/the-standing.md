Over the anchor hall at the Nine, on the gallery wall, is a board four metres
wide with every door in human space written up on it and, against each one,
where it currently goes.

It is the single most important object in the Causeway. It is also entirely
hand-kept, and that is not a quaintness — it is the only arrangement the physics
allows.

## Why it cannot be anything else

Nothing crosses a door except a person who walks it. So the far side of a door is
not data anybody can fetch; it is **testimony**, carried back in somebody's head
by a child who was standing there.

Which gives three hard consequences:

- **A line can be corrected in eleven seconds** by somebody standing on a stool,
  the moment a courier walks up the stair with better information. No system in
  human space can beat that, because the information arrives on foot.
- **[[Old Man Sirius]] is not permitted to write on it.** A mind may not enter
  testimony it does not have, and he does not have any — he cannot cross. He
  agrees with the rule and will defend it if challenged.
- **Somebody has to keep it.** Not write it, which the tallymen do: keep it.
  Square the lines, chase the ones nobody has updated, notice when one has gone
  stale. At the Nine that is [[Sennet]], who is eleven.

## How to read it, which almost nobody does

Four things on every line:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Door** | which one |
| **Where it goes** | the far side, as last reported |
| **Who last stood it** | the courier whose testimony that is |
| **Which watch** | *when* they stood it |

People read the first two and walk off. The fourth is the only one that matters.

A line tells you where a door went **when somebody last looked**. That is all it
has ever told you. And a door can stray in the time it takes to walk down to the
hall.

> *A line isn't true. A line is when. If you don't read the watch you're just
> reading a rumour somebody wrote up neatly.*

A line eleven days old was read as current by the whole station until the keeper
noticed and nobody else did. That is the ordinary failure mode and it happens
constantly. It is also, at scale and over centuries, how the Proper Order
happened: not suppression, just everybody reading what was in front of them and
not checking when it was written.

## Kapteyn's

Far over to the left, in a hand nothing like any working tallyman's, is one line
where the writing has gone into the surface and the surface has gone grey around
it.

> **KAPTEYN'S — NO ANSWER THIS WATCH**

*This watch* is what you write when a door does not answer and you expect it to
answer later. It is the ordinary notation. The keeper at the Nine writes it four
or five times a year and rubs it out again by the next watch.

Somebody wrote it in the spring of six hundred and two because they thought they
would be rubbing it out by the afternoon.

It is the stalest line on the board by six centuries, it is the only line the
keeper is not allowed to square up, and she has asked twice. See
[[The Nine Hundred Years]].
