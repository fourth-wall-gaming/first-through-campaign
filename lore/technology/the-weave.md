Nothing manufactured crosses a door. So the garment a first-through crosses in
is not made at all. It is **grown**, it is alive, and it is the one thing that is
yours and goes everywhere you do.

## What it is

A weave is a garment. You pull it on, you pull it off, you hang it up. It is
close-fitting, long-sleeved and long-legged, about the weight of a good shirt,
and it comes out of the vat a plain off-white.

The only strange thing about it is that it was never woven, whatever it is
called. It is **grown**, at [[61 Cygni, and the Yards]], from a line nobody has
improved on in four centuries -- and grown to a measure, so somebody else's
hangs wrong on you and everybody can tell at a glance.

It is not armour and it is not a pressure suit. What it does is hold your heat,
keep you decent, seal a small cut, and -- the part that matters -- **be alive**,
which is the only reason it can go through. The pressure-skin, suit and hood are
grown from the same stock, but those are the Threshold's and are issued; the
weave is the one you keep. See [[A First-Through's Kit]].

## The flash, and everything else

There is exactly one regulation part of a weave and it is the **Threshold flash
at the left shoulder**: a small plain mark, grown into the cloth rather than
sewn on. The cloth is alive, so it takes the mark the way skin does, and it
cannot be faked because it cannot be attached. It is the thing a
tallyman looks at. It says this person is allowed through a door, and it is
the same on a twelve-year-old at the Yards and on a courier of twenty-one with
two thousand crossings.

**Everything else is yours.** A living weave takes dye the way nothing dead
does -- deep, even, and permanent until the cloth itself gives it up -- and a
first-through who has been paid for three months will have done something about
the off-white.

What people do with that is the closest thing the service has to a culture.
Deck colours, so you can be found in a hall. The colours of wherever you grew
up, which is how you start an argument with somebody from Barnard's. A band at
the cuff for every door you have stood. Something somebody's mother dyed and
sent through, badly, that the wearer will not replace. Plain black, from the
ones who think the whole business is childish, which is itself a statement and
everybody reads it as one.

The Threshold has tried four times in six hundred years to standardise the
colour and has lost four times, most recently to a work stoppage over it at
Alpha Centauri that nobody involved is embarrassed about.

## Keeping one

It is laundry that happens to be alive, and it is about as much trouble as a
houseplant.

Off you, it lives in a shallow tray by your bunk with an inch of feed in it.
Every bunkroom at the Nine has a rack of them and the whole row smells faintly
of warm bread, which first-throughs find comforting and visitors find
unnerving.

It sulks in dry air. It closes a tear by itself overnight and takes a week over
a bad one. Neglect it properly and it goes patchy, and somebody at the hall
will say so in front of people.

A weave lasts two or three years and then is retired and replaced, and a
first-through's first weave is kept by their family roughly as often as not.

## And it frays

It takes the crossing the way you do, and it takes it worse, because it is not
mending anything else at the same time.

So a weave goes **mottled** -- faint, cloudy, pale across the shoulders and
forearms first -- and the pattern is a straightforward record of how many times
its wearer has been through a door.

**And the dye is what makes it legible.** Fraying bleaches. On a plain
off-white weave the mottling is there if you look; on a dyed one it is loud,
and it reads across a hall. So dyeing your weave announces two things at once,
and you only get to choose the first of them.

Everybody works this out in their second year. A few stop dyeing, and let the
colour grow out, and go back to off-white. Nobody says anything about that
either, and everybody notices.

Which means you cannot lie about your number. Not to anybody who has seen a
weave before, and everyone has.

A clean new one on a twelve-year-old is the most hopeful thing at the Nine. A
weave gone white to the elbow on somebody of twenty-one is a thing people see
and do not mention, and the person wearing it knows exactly how long they have
been looking at it in the glass.
