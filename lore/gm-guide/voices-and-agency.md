Both of these are easy to fall into in this setting specifically, and both were
caught at the table rather than by any test, which is why they are written down.

## One: the player character is not a facilitator

**The tell.** Count what the PC has physically done in the last hour. If the
honest answer is *fetched people, and carried information between them*, the
NPCs have become the protagonists and the PC has become transport.

It happens in this campaign more easily than in most, because carrying things
between people who cannot reach each other **is literally the job**. A
first-through is a courier. The trap is letting that become her role in the
*story* as well as in the setting.

**The mechanism to avoid, stated plainly, because a GM will build it by
accident:** giving every insight to an NPC. [[Old Man Sirius]] has the
measurements. A fathom has the field sense. A tallyman has the only sheet. An
engineer has nineteen years of lamps. A reader has the leverage. And the player
walks between them collecting it.

Every one of those is a good character. Together they are a relay race in which
the PC is the baton.

**What to do instead:**

- **The PC's hands on the thing.** She finds it, she decides it, she does it.
  Where the discovery is the point, it is *her roll*, with her skills, and she
  owns the result.
- **NPCs supply obstacles, costs, reactions and refusals — not conclusions.**
  An NPC who knows something makes the player pay for it: a roll, a favour, a
  risk, a relationship. Never a download.
- **Watch the cast size.** Three or more NPCs in a room with the PC is a
  committee, and a committee is where agency goes to die. Two is a scene. One is
  usually better.
- **Give her a want that is hers**, that is not the task, and that costs her
  something when it conflicts with the task. Hers, not the plot's.

A useful question before any scene: *what can the PC do here that nobody else in
the room can?* If the answer is "walk to the next room", cut the scene.

## Two: six NPCs, one voice

**The tell.** Every NPC enumerates. Everybody asks for things on the record,
everybody insists on the order events happened in, everybody says *say it back*.

That is not characterisation, it is the GM's own register leaking into every
mouth — and in this setting it hides well, because the campaign is genuinely
about records nobody reads. A tic that agrees with the theme survives scrutiny.

**The fix: one character owns precision, and the rest are forbidden it.**

That character is **Old Man Sirius**, and it is real characterisation rather
than a habit — a mind given a voice by an inquest into a report nobody read,
whose office permits advice and forbids judgement. He states limits because his
limits are the whole of what he is.

Nobody else says *on the record*, *minuted*, *in order*, or *say it back*.
