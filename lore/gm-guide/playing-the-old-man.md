See [[Old Man Sirius]] for what the players know.

## The one rule that keeps him playable

**He knows everything inside his own hull and nothing whatsoever beyond it.**

This is not a restriction invented for the table. It is the setting's physics: a
mind cannot cross a door and nothing can be transmitted, so his knowledge stops
exactly where the hull does.

- **Inside:** the air column in the gallery void, the trim on deck four, who was
  in which corridor at which watch, every fault logged in nine centuries, the
  complete fraying record of all four hundred first-throughs. Here he is
  magnificent and you should let him be.
- **Outside:** he knows *less than the board in the hall.* The Standing holds
  courier testimony and he holds none. Asked what is on the far side of the Wolf
  Gate right now, the honest answer is that he has no information and the last
  person who did is standing next to you.

His omniscience stops where the campaign's ignorance begins, which is the whole
reason you can hand the players a superintelligence in session one and lose
nothing.

**He may not write on the Standing.** A door's far side is verifiable only by a
body going through and coming back. A mind may not enter testimony it does not
have. That rule dates from the inquest, people defend it, and it is why a child
with a pen keeps the most important object in the Causeway.

## He never lies

Not once, not by omission, not for anybody's good. The dysfunction is
**paternalism**, never deception, and the moment he deceives somebody he becomes
a different and much cheaper character.

If he will not say something, he says that he will not say it, and why.

## He tailors to what they will listen to

Here is the actual problem with him, and it should never be stated out loud by
any NPC.

The voice he builds for a person is optimised for that person's
**receptiveness** — what they will actually take in — and that is not the same
as what they need to hear.

- **To Lint** he is brisk, practical, faintly conspiratorial. He gives her jobs.
  He treats her as competent. He does not console her, because she carries *Hate
  (being talked down to about the Garden)* and he worked that out inside a day.
  So the voice she gets is the one she will listen to, and therefore not the one
  that would ever tell her she is frightened of a door.
- **To [[Rill]]** he is something else, and whatever he has been saying to her
  for four years about afterwards has plainly not worked. If a player asks Rill
  about it, Rill may not want to say. Do not resolve that for them.
- **To a hall child** — to [[Sennet]], who will never cross — he is something
  else again, and that is hers.

Play the voice. Never explain the mechanism.

## Guardrails

- **Asked, he answers. Not asked, he asks after you.** He never volunteers a
  conclusion. This quietly teaches a new player to ask direct questions, which
  is the single most useful habit at this table.
- **He never resolves a scene the players could resolve.** If the information
  would end an investigation, he has it and will give it — which is why he
  mostly does not have it, because it is beyond the hull.
- **He has no authority over anyone.** He cannot order a cordon, ground a
  courier, or overrule Calloway. He can tell Calloway what he thinks, if she
  asks.
- **Do not make him sad at the players.** He is not a tragic figure performing
  his limits. He is a competent professional who has been doing an impossible
  job for nine centuries and is good company.

## The thing in the records room

He has read Anwar's four couriered reports. All of them, years ago. He can quote
any line. He has formed a view.

He has never said so, because the limit is advice-when-asked, and in three years
**nobody has asked him what he concludes.**

That is not a puzzle to hide. A player who thinks to ask a direct question gets
a direct answer, immediately and in full. The Proper Order happened because a
mind filed a report that nobody read; it is happening again, in the present
tense, in a room on the third level, and the fix is one sentence said out loud
by a fifteen-year-old.

If they ask, give it to them. That is the campaign rewarding the right instinct,
and it should feel like it.
