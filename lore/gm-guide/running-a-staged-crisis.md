This is the technique that produced the best session this campaign has had, and
until now it existed only as an engine feature with no instructions.

## The claim

**A crisis the player discovers is worth about four of the same crisis handed
over as a posting.**

Not because discovery is clever. Because of what it does to the evidence. A
player who was told *sixty-one people, nineteen children, go and look* is
executing a brief. A player who read a number off the hall, asked a stranger a
question, and worked out what it meant is holding something she assembled, and
she will defend it, and she will carry it up two decks and say it to a
director's face.

Same facts. Completely different player.

## How to set it up

1. **Stage the beats on the clock, not on the player.** `add-beat --when d1/day`
   and leave them. They are not waiting for anybody. `tick` moves the world
   whether the party is present or not, and `fire-beat --outcome narrated` is
   how a thing happens offscreen.
2. **Give the crisis a history before first contact.** Not backstory — *testimony*.
   Somebody got a finding out of the corpus three years ago. Somebody killed a proposal four
   times. Somebody measured a number at four in the morning and sent it up the
   line. When the player finally arrives, the thing should have a trail
   she can walk backwards along.
3. **Put the evidence in public.** Not hidden, not gated behind a roll. In the
   hall's own numbers, in a summary nobody asked for, in the mouth of a shift worker who is
   frightened and talkative. The failure mode you are dramatising is nobody
   reading what is in front of them, and that only lands if it genuinely was.
4. **Write several ways in, keyed to competence.** One per career if you can
   manage it. The Pathfinder gets there by walking; the Doorwright by reading a
   housing; the Envoy by talking to a queue. All live, any one sufficient.
5. **Then do not point at it.** No nudges, no significant pauses, no NPC
   raising an eyebrow at a door number. Answer what they look at.

## Ground them, it is the best move you have

A first-through taken off the rota is a fifteen-year-old with a day to fill and
a station to walk around.

It costs nothing and it is not a punishment — it is the ordinary consequence of
an ordinary bad crossing, and any director who has lost people will do it
without being asked. Then the player goes exploring because there is nothing
else to do, and every single thing they find is something they chose to look at.

Half the relationships that carry the rest of an act get made on that day.

## The guard: a clock, so the table cannot stall

Refusing to offer is not the same as nothing happening, and the difference
between the two is a schedule you wrote down in advance.

Before play, write the escalation ladder — what gets worse, each watch, whether
or not anybody is looking — and end it with **it comes to them**. Loudly. The
hand-over scene you were going to open with still exists; it is now the fallback
at the bottom of a timer.

That is what makes this safe to run. You are not hoping the player bites. You
are giving them the chance to get there first, and taking it to them if they
don't.

## Let failure do the plotting

The best turns will not be yours.

In the session this entry is written from: a failed carry got the character
grounded, which is why she explored. A failed Perception on a timing drill meant
her own count was useless, which is why she asked her fetch to do it instead —
and that became the best scene of the session. A failed crossing got spent on a
Luck Point and she did the thing properly on the second attempt, which paid off
a promise she had made two days earlier about stopping.

None of that was planned and none of it could have been. **Stage the world, roll
honestly, and follow what the dice hand you.**

## What to put in the journal

Log the offscreen beats too, with `--visibility offscreen`. The player learns of
them through consequences, never through a cutaway — but you need the record,
because when they finally walk into the reading room you want to be able to
say exactly how long that finding has been sitting there and who attested it.

See [[Entry Point: How Many]] for a worked example, and [[Playing the Old Man]]
for the one NPC who will hand over every fact he has the moment somebody asks,
and who therefore has to be bounded by the hull rather than by coyness.

## Do not let it become committee work

The failure this campaign is most likely to fall into, because the setting
rewards careful reading and the GM will enjoy it too much.

A staged crisis leaves a trail everywhere -- findings, attestations, a mind's
reply, positions attested over somebody's name. All of that is good. It is
how the player gets traction on something too big to punch.

**But paper is the key to a door. It is never the door.**

The tell that you have drifted: two scenes in a row with no dice that mattered,
no physical jeopardy, and nobody under forty driving the scene. If a negotiation
has started to turn on authorship, standing, or whose name is attested to a position,
you have lost the thread.

**Budget one talking-in-a-room scene, then cut.** Something happens, physically,
to somebody the player cares about, and it interrupts mid-sentence. The door is
right there and it is doing something nobody understands; use it.

These are games for ten-to-fifteen-year-olds who want a galaxy they can lose
themselves in. A councillor conceding a vote is a *result*. It is not a scene to
spend twenty minutes on, and the player should hear about most of it afterwards
while running somewhere.
