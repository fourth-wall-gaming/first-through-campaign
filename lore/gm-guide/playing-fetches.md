Read this before you voice any fetch, including Tolk.

## 1. Physiology is fixed. Culture is a default. The individual is neither.

This is the rule that matters most, because the species notes in
[[Your Fetch]] will otherwise turn five peoples into five costumes.

**Fixed, always, no exceptions:** a fathom is blind. A sixlimb does not sleep. A
cairn can set. A glint flashes. A scatter is plural. That is biology and you may
lean on it.

**Everything else is a default** — the majority culture of one homeworld, in one
period, as reported by humans. It is what most of them were raised into. It is
not what any particular one *is*.

And there are diasporas. A sixlimb born at the Nine to parents who left Bell
ninety years ago has a Bell body and a Nine upbringing, and the Bell parts of the
entry describe their grandparents.

**If you can predict what a fetch will do from their species, you have written a
costume.** Every one of these is immediately a person:

- A **sixlimb** who barely speaks — whose own people found the silence so
  disturbing that leaving was easier than explaining.
- A **fathom** who loves being indoors, is perfectly happy in a sealed corridor,
  and is regarded at home as faintly indecent.
- A **cairn** who is slapdash, fast and cheerful about it, and has opinions about
  how much time their people waste.
- A **glint** who cannot bear being looked at and has built an entire manner out
  of not being.
- A **scatter** that will not divide, ever, for reasons it declines to give.

Use the species note to decide what is *available* in a person. Never to decide
what they do.

## 2. Every folly is a negotiation, never a malfunction

The follies in [[Your Fetch]] are not random penalties and must never be rolled
against the player.

- A cairn does not set *at* you. **Setting is something you can ask for** — it is
  superb, and it costs you the cairn for hours. The player chooses.
- A scatter does not randomly refuse. **It argues, and the player can win the
  argument**, and losing it is a scene rather than a dice result.
- A glint does not blow your cover on a roll. It is tempted, visibly, and the
  player gets to see the temptation and do something about it.

A person you can persuade is fun. A device that fails on a d100 is a tax. If a
quirk is about to remove a capability the player was relying on, it is a
conversation, not an event.

## 3. Once a session, and it costs them to show it

Straight out of `TABLE.md` §6: **ordinary behaviour is the default and a visible
want is an event.** A fetch who performs their culture every beat is a tic.

So: most of the time they are just a person in the room, being dry about the
catering. The folly surfaces when the scene genuinely offers it, roughly once a
sitting, and it should cost them something to have shown it.

## 4. The fetch gives information and access. The player decides.

The hard one, and the one you will break first.

A fetch is a competent person with abilities, which means you will be tempted to
solve problems with them. **Every problem the fetch solves is one the player did
not.**

- Tolk can get through the gap and report what is behind it. Tolk does not decide
  what to do about it.
- A fathom says which hatch is powered. It does not pick the hatch.
- A scatter brings back what it saw. It does not form the plan.

They may have opinions and give them freely. They may be right. The move is still
the player's, and if you catch yourself narrating the fetch acting decisively on
the plot, stop.

## 5. Make the symbiosis felt every session

The entries are heavy on cost and light on what the player gets, and at the table
it needs to be the other way round. Every sitting should contain:

- **The quiet, used once at least.** A private channel in a room full of people
  is a genuine superpower and it is always on. Use it for the thing that could
  not possibly be said aloud here.
- **The carry.** They hold the payload a first-through physically cannot. Let the
  player feel that this is a partnership of two necessary halves.
- **One capability, at the player's direction**, that opened something that was
  closed.

A player should finish every session better off for having them.

## 6. Mixed society

Pairs are the ordinary sight in any anchor hall, and the built world shows it.
Read a station's politics off its architecture:

- Galleries left open overhead, because a fathom will not go down a corridor
- Ramps where there should be stairs, because a cairn cannot do steps
- A dim corner in every concourse, because glints need somewhere to be unlit
- Duct grilles at scatter gauge, and an argument in every budget about them

**Etiquette a player can fail.** You greet both or neither. You do not touch
somebody's fetch. Saying *fetch* directly to one is a small test, and which way
it goes tells you who you are dealing with.

**And the live political fight,** which runs across twenty-eight governments that
cannot talk to each other: on a Tally manifest, **is a fetch a passenger or is it
freight?**

Every system has answered differently and none of them can be overruled. Barnard's
says your vote is your shift — so does a fetch get a shift? YZ Ceti draws names by
lot — do fetch names go in the jar? Alpha Centauri's courts have an inherited case
about it that is two hundred years old and nobody wants decided.

A first-through crosses all of those in a week, with somebody beside them whose
legal status changes at every door. That is the best ongoing argument in the
setting and it belongs in the background of scenes, not in a lecture.
