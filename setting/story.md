# First Through — the plan

**This file is the plan, not the script.** What order things go in, what each act
is *for*, and what each act **takes**. No scene is described here; descriptions
live in [`beats/`](../beats/), which is the catalog. If a description is in both,
one of them is already wrong.

Rewrite this freely.

## The three rules

**Every act takes something** — a loss that does not come back. An act in which
the player only gains has not happened.

**Ordinary world first.** The player has to love the Nine before it is
threatened. The hardest scene in Act I is the fourth beat, not the first.

**Conditions accelerate; time backstops.** A beat's `when` is the latest it can
happen.

## What the story is actually about

A first-through is the most important person in human space for about seven
years, and then it stops, and nobody has ever worked out what happens next.

Three people answer that question, and the campaign is the player choosing
between them.

| | |
|---|---|
| **[[Rill]]** | the warning. Twenty-one, frayed out, taking money from the only man who offered her a plan. |
| **[[Calloway]]** | the answer. Aged out at forty-one, runs a desk, and is the happiest and most loved person in the setting. |
| **[[The Quorum]]** | the refusal. The best first-through who ever lived, who would not accept being landlocked and paid for it with everything that made him a person. |

In Act III the Quorum offers the player exactly what he took. By then they will
have wanted it since session one, and they will have watched what it did to Rill,
and they will know what Calloway chose instead.

# ACT I — `d0 to d4` · THE NINE

**For:** making the player love this place and these people, and learning the job
in their own hands — and then finding out what happens to a fifteen-year-old who
is right about something in a world where being right is not the same as being
believed.

**Takes:** his anonymity, chosen, the first hour he acts; a first-through he liked, walked through a door by a stranger and left alive on the far side where nothing can ever reach them; and then either Oluf Varre or Mereth Vane, one of whom does not come out of this act — all of it caused by the player being right. WHAT CAN KILL HIM: twice the setting itself, a plate that will not take while he stands on the wrong side of the only door out of a system, no signal, no rescue possible from anywhere, the only creature who could come for him another child who does not know he is there — rolled, and if the dice strand him the campaign becomes getting him home; and once a person, Mereth Vane, nineteen, living blade at 79 against his drill at 42. AND THE SIZE OF IT: the maintenance figure is a doorknob. A door driven past what it was built for does not recover, nobody can build or pair or re-point or restart one, and Luhman 16 has ONE door and ninety thousand people behind it who cannot be evacuated — not slowly, not at all, because only the young cross, one at a time, one passenger each, and the sum does not work and never did. Seven of the Nine's own nine doors show the same shape.

**What can kill him, and when it gets its chance.** Twice by the setting itself:
a plate that will not take while he is standing on the wrong side of the only
door out of a system, with no signal, no rescue possible from anywhere, and the
only creature who could come for him being another child who does not know he
is there — rolled, and if the dice strand him, the campaign becomes getting him
home. And once by a person: Mereth Vane, nineteen, living blade at 79 against
his drill at 42, and she has four years of doing this.

**And the size of it, which is the part that was got wrong first time.** The
maintenance figure is a doorknob. What it is a clue about: a door driven past
what it was built for does not recover, nobody can build, pair, re-point or
restart one, and **Luhman 16 has one door and ninety thousand people behind
it.** They cannot be evacuated — not slowly, not at all. Only the young cross,
one at a time, each carrying one person by the sleeve, and the sum does not
work and never did. Seven of the Nine's own nine doors show the same shape.

In detail:

1. **His anonymity**, chosen, in the first hour he acts. He says a true thing out
   loud in a drum with forty people in it, because that is the only way anything
   gets said here, and by dusk it has reached two people who would rather it had
   not.
2. **An innocent first-through**, not chosen. Brace believes him, and Brace is
   the one adult on the station whose whole life is a count that closes, and he
   will strip a child's kit in a hall in front of people to make it close.
   Calloway cannot stop it and does not pretend she can.
3. **Oluf Varre, or Mereth Vane.** Varre is sixty-two, has stood the same plate
   for thirty-one years, and is the only human being who holds the figure
   first-hand. Vane is nineteen, has the player's own knack, and has eleven
   months before a door tells her no. One of them does not come out of this act.

**The antagonists, and why these two for this character.** Sigurd's engine is
that he is always right and nobody listens to him, and his one asset is
testimony. So: **Marek Sollen**, who never has to refute him and only has to be
kind about him in public, and who would legislate his fetch out of being a
witness; and **Mereth Vane**, who has the Read as well, which cancels the only
advantage on his sheet. The first attacks what he is. The second attacks whether
he continues.

Full scenario, plotted in sequences:
[`docs/scenarios/the-count-does-not-close.md`](../docs/scenarios/the-count-does-not-close.md).
Method: `SCENARIO.md` in the engine.

The act opens social: a bunkroom, a kettle, a game of sling, Calloway being
pleased to see them. Then one short successful crossing, so the player knows what
the job feels like when it works. Then the Wolf Gate starts setting, and the time
it takes to open is measurable, and it rises.

Ends on the watch at [[van Maanen's Star]] reporting something for the first time
in three and a half centuries.

# ACT II — KAPTEYN'S

**For:** the wonder, and the first hard truth. Fifteen generations alone
behind a buried door, and then the discovery that the stray which opened it was
predictable — and that somebody predicted it.

**Takes:** Rill. She runs out during this act, in front of the player, and it is
nobody's fault and entirely avoidable and both of those are true.

Beats to write: the Nine goes mad; who gets to go first and how that is decided;
the crossing; what eleven thousand people became in three and a half centuries alone; evidence
that somebody was already here; the figures behind the stray; Rill.

Ends at the low point: strays can be **caused**, somebody is causing them, and
that somebody now knows the player's name.

# ACT III — THE FORTY-ONE

**For:** the thing behind all of it, and the question the campaign has been
asking from the first hour — what a human being is *for*, in a galaxy where
everything worth knowing is held by somebody else and we are the only species
that can open a door.

**Takes:** whatever the player decides to spend — this act should cost them something they chose. THE ADVERSARY IS A FETCH: a scatter catalogued as Lot 41, the oldest surviving book, six hundred years kept and drawn on by strangers and then divided and sold in lots because a scatter can be divided and each piece was worth money somewhere else. Thirty-four years free, about thirty-three units of forty-one, two of them somewhere it cannot reach without a courier's help, and it will never be whole. IT DOES NOT WANT US DEAD — dead hands carry nothing. It wants the arrangement restored with the terms reversed, formally, with a charter on it, and humans keeping the one thing we are for: we walk. IT NEEDS NO WEAPONS — it is in every room because a scatter sends a third of itself where the rest cannot go, one unit per child per door; it curates, because a fetch chooses what to give and to whom and no mind can check it; and it holds the councils because it is the oldest and best-informed and most wronged voice in the room and ITS CASE IS TRUE. The uncounted traffic from Act I is its own distribution, and it will not slow down for the doors, because a severed system is a sealed room with one authority in it and its unit is already inside. Sarn is now an ally. WHAT STOPS IT: it has to be carried, by a fifteen-year-old who could decline.

**It is a scatter, catalogued as Lot 41, and we did it to it.** Six hundred
years a book — kept warm and fed and drawn on by strangers — and, because a
scatter can be divided and each piece was worth money somewhere else, sold off
in lots by people keeping proper records. Thirty-four years free. About
thirty-three units of forty-one. Six known dead, two somewhere it cannot reach
without a courier's help, and it will never be whole, and a scatter divided too
long starts quietly forgetting.

It does not want us destroyed. **Dead hands carry nothing.** It wants the
arrangement restored with the terms reversed, formally, with a charter on it,
and it is entirely open about that to anybody who asks. Humans keep the one
thing we are actually for: we walk.

**It needs no weapons.** It is in every room, because a scatter can send a third
of itself somewhere the rest cannot go — one unit, one child, one door, many
doors at once. It curates, because a fetch chooses what to give and to whom, and
that is not lying and no mind can check it and nothing can be compared across a
door. And it holds the councils, because it is the oldest and best-informed and
most obviously wronged voice in the room and its case is **true**.

And the uncounted traffic the player found in Act I is **its own distribution** —
thousands of crossings over thirty-four years, on children's arms, as lading
nobody questions. It is not causing the doors to fail. It will not slow down for
them, and it will tell you so, because when the doors go, every system is a
sealed room with one authority in it and its unit is already inside.

**Sarn is now on the player's side**, which is the act's best turn. The Quorum
needs doors open and few; the Forty-One needs them open now and does not mind
them closing later. He has been moving its units for thirty years without
knowing what they were, and when he works it out he is the most useful ally a
fifteen-year-old could have, and it will cost something appalling.

**The climax is still a race** — [[LHS 1140]]'s door, the first new door in
twenty-five years and the first onto a living world. A new door is a room the
Forty-One is not yet in.

**What stops it:** not force, not law. It has to be carried, and every single
thing it does happens on the arm of a fifteen-year-old who could decline. It has
spent thirty-four years making sure that never occurs to anybody, and it is one
conversation away from occurring to everybody.

Ends with a door opening, honestly, with a child standing in it.

## Sarn, who used to be Act III and is now in it on our side

*Not an act. Reference, kept here because the whole of Act III turns on him.
Deliberately not marked up as an act — a `For:` or `Takes:` line here would be
parsed as a fourth act and would silently swallow the real one, which is exactly
what happened once.*

He is courteous, tired, physically unremarkable, and in more than one place. He
does not want to rule anything. He wants the Causeway small enough to hold
himself together across, and he has spent sixty years making sure nobody learns
how to move a door before he does. He found the only way to be in more than one
place and it cost him being a person.

Which is why the Forty-One ends him as an antagonist and makes him useful. He
needs doors **open and few**; it needs them open **now** and does not mind them
closing later. He has been moving its units for thirty years in his own ladings
without ever asking what they were, and when he finds out he has helped build
the thing that will outlive him in every room he cannot reach, he will be
extremely calm about it and then extremely dangerous on the player's behalf.

He will still offer the player what he took, kindly, and mean it. That offer is
now the price of the alliance rather than the temptation at the end.

The twenty-four beats currently in `beats/` were written for the superseded
premise — dying anchors, an impound order, a refinery barricade — and almost all
of them are now false. **They need re-authoring against this arc before
`sync-arc` is run**, because sync cancels anything the document drops.

until their beats are written.
