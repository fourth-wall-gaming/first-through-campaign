# Being a First-Through

The job is simple to describe. You go first, and you come back and say what is
there.

Most days that means a parcel, a passenger, a tallyman's manifest and a
sandwich. Some days it means standing in front of a housing that has done
something nobody has an explanation for, with four hundred people behind a
cordon rope waiting to hear what you found.

## What a crossing is like

Almost nothing. That is the honest answer and new players are always slightly
disappointed by it.

You step onto the plate. The air changes. You are somewhere else. There is no
flash, no lurch, no tunnel, no noise. The only reason you know you crossed is
that the smell is different and the boots you are wearing belong to a different
station.

Nothing dead crosses with you — not clothes, not boots, not a bag — so you
undress at one end and dress at the other, and every hall in human space has a
rail of boots in it that nobody owns. The exception is your **membrane**: a
living second skin, grown to you specifically, worn as ordinary clothing. It is
alive, so it crosses.

## What it costs

Each crossing takes something off you that does not come back. It is small and
it accumulates and it is visible: the membrane clouds across the shoulders
first, then the forearms, like breathed-on glass. Somebody who has done two
thousand crossings does not have clouded forearms. They have **white** ones.

Around twenty-two a body stops being able to do it at all, and after that you
cannot cross, not once, not ever. The word for that is **frayed out**, and
being landlocked is the ordinary end of every first-through's career, and
nobody in six hundred years has worked out what a person is supposed to do
afterwards.

That is not a secret. The adults will tell you if you ask them. Some of them
will tell you if you don't.

## Carrying

Anybody over about twenty-two who wants to go anywhere has to be **carried**,
in your arms, through the door, by you.

It is harder than crossing alone and it costs more. A child is cheap — minutes,
maybe. An adult is dear: a fortnight off the end of your life, counted, and
three or four in a row finishes anybody. Nobody pretends otherwise and nobody
rounds it down.

A carry can also **set** — the door going solid under you with somebody in your
arms, refusing to open for two. When that happens there is exactly one correct
answer and every instructor at the Yards says it the same way:

> Put them down. Not ease off. Put them down, on whichever side you are on, and
> let go, and do not spend four seconds being brave about it.

Pushing a set is how people get hurt, and it is always, always a decision
somebody made while having a bad day and deciding they could have it both ways.

## Your knack

Most first-throughs have one. They surface somewhere in the first year or so
and nobody can tell you when, and *"what's your knack?"* is the first thing one
first-through asks another.

**The Listen** tells you something about the far side before you step through —
air, pressure, heat, whether there is a floor, whether anybody is standing
there. It is never detailed and it is the single most valuable thing anybody
out here can have, and it is why a first-through survives a door that has
moved. Two or three per intake.

**The Quick** is reflex and speed past what a body should manage. Common,
obvious, and the one every twelve-year-old in the Yards wants.

**The Set** is taking what ought to break you. **The Reckoning** is always
knowing precisely where you are. **The Knowing** is understanding a mechanism
by putting your hands on it. **The Read** is seeing what people are about to
do, and is rare, resented, and worth not mentioning.

**The Hold** — keeping a door where it is, for a few minutes, by paying
attention to it — is rarer than all of them and you will probably never meet
somebody who has it.

Everybody also has Life Support, which is the membrane rather than you, and
which can be taken off you by anybody with the right tool in under a minute.
That is worth remembering.

## What keeps you alive past a door

Two things, and there is no third: what you trained your body to do, and what
you are wearing.

Your **pressure-skin** holds you at survivable pressure and temperature for
**two environments only**, chosen when you draw your kit — vacuum and cold, or
vacuum and hard radiation, or a toxic atmosphere and heat. You choose in
advance, and you choose wrong roughly once per career.

Your **sensor hood** gives you infrared, a pressure gradient and an echo mode,
which is the stated reason a first-through can walk into an unlit unmapped
station and not die in the first ten metres.

Your **charge** at the small of your back is finite and only refills when you
rest.

And if there is trouble it will be a stunner, a shock baton and four restraint
loops, because out here almost nobody reaches for a blade. A weapon that
reliably kills a person reliably opens a wall, and a wall is the only thing
between everybody in the room and vacuum. Winning a fight means the other
person is on the floor and tied. Losing one means you are.
