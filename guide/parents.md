# What Actually Happens

Your child has found a roleplaying game and you would like to know what is in
it. This page is the honest answer, and at the bottom there is a novel made out
of a real four-day run of it, so you can read the thing itself rather than a
description of it.

## What the game is

One young player and one Games Master. No board, no screen required, no
winning. The player invents a person and then decides what that person does,
and the other one describes what happens as a result, and dice settle anything
genuinely uncertain. A session runs two or three hours. It is a conversation
with rules.

The rules are *Mythras Imperative*, a published system, used unmodified — and
free, as a PDF, from the people who wrote it. They amount to: roll two ten-sided dice, read them as a number out of a hundred, and
try to get under the number written next to the relevant skill on your sheet.
That is the whole of it. A child who can subtract can play this.

## What it is about

Nine hundred and seventy-five years ago, people who knew perfectly well they
would not live to see it work spent everything they had on robot ships, and
threw them at the nearest stars. Where one arrived, a door opened. Those doors
are the only way between stars, and the only thing that crosses one is a
person.

And only the young can do it. By about twenty-two a body is finished. So the
entire traffic of human space — every letter, every doctor wanted three doors
away, every grandmother who wants to see her sister — rides on fifteen-year-olds.

Your child plays one of them. They are paid, they are respected, they are
extremely good at a real job, and the adults around them are visibly glad they
exist. That is deliberate. It is a game about being competent and being trusted
with something that matters.

## What it asks of a young player

Mostly: pay attention, ask questions, and decide.

The thing this campaign rewards is not quick reflexes or lucky dice. It is
noticing. The whole first act turns on a child asking a man a direct question
across a crowded room, and getting an answer nobody else had thought to ask
for. Several of its best moments are a young person reading something that has
been sitting in plain sight for years because nobody bothered.

It also asks them to take responsibility for a decision that costs somebody
something, and to live with having made it. That is the part that tends to
stay with people.

## Danger, and what we promise instead of safety

**People can be hurt in this game and characters can die, including your
child's.** The rules are not softened and the dice are rolled where everybody
can see them.

What the campaign guarantees in exchange is **information**. A character can
only come to harm from a risk that was named, in the fiction, before the player
chose it. No hidden trap. No ambush from a direction they had no way to check.
And an honest answer, every single time, to the question *can this kill me?* —
with no bluffing and no mystery.

In practice this makes the game safer than one that simply avoids danger,
because it teaches a specific and genuinely useful habit: **ask before you
commit, and you will be told.**

There is also a deliberate design choice about violence. Almost nobody in this
setting reaches for a lethal weapon, and the reason is physical rather than
moral: everyone lives inside a thin hull, and a weapon that reliably kills a
person reliably opens a wall and kills everyone in the room. The professionals
carry stunners, batons and restraint straps. Winning a fight means the other
person is on the floor and tied.

## What is not in it

No romance. No torture. No cruelty played for entertainment. No drugs, no
gambling as glamour, no real-world politics in costume. Nobody is menaced for
being who they are.

There is one adult theme and it is the heart of the thing: these children spend
their own health doing this job, the spending is visible on their skin, it
stops in their early twenties, and nobody in six hundred years has worked out
what they are supposed to do with the rest of their lives. The campaign takes
that seriously, treats it as a question worth answering, and offers three
different adult answers to it.

## The astronomy is real

Every star, distance, planet and oddity in this game is a current measurement,
cited in the source. The map is Alpha Centauri and Barnard's scorched pebbles,
the brown dwarfs of Luhman 16 with weather and no sun at all, the twin flare
stars of Gliese 65, the radio aurorae of YZ Ceti, and van Maanen's Star
visibly eating the wreckage of its own planets.

There is an automated test that fails the project if a number in the prose
disagrees with the data file. Children notice when you make things up. This one
does not.

## If you are the one who has to run it

Quite likely, and worth saying plainly: you do not need to have played a
roleplaying game before, and you have two routes.

**Let the machine run it.** The campaign ships as a plugin for Claude Code.
Claude plays the Games Master, a rules engine does the dice, and you do not
read anything. This is the shortest path from here to your child playing
tonight, and it is a real game, not a demo.

**Or run it yourself, which is better if you can.** For that you need the
rules, because the guides on this site are the story and the characters and
deliberately not the system. They are *Mythras Imperative*, they are free, and
The Design Mechanism publish the PDF on their
[downloads page](https://thedesignmechanism.com/free-downloads/).

Three things that make this less daunting than it looks.

**You need about fifteen pages of it to start** — how a roll works, how
difficulty is set, and what a Luck Point does. One evening, unhurried.

**You can skip the combat chapter, which is the intimidating one.** There is
no fighting anywhere in this campaign. Not one scene. The danger is a door
that will not open and a clock that is running out, and nobody in this setting
reaches for a weapon, because everyone lives inside a hull and a weapon that
kills a person opens a wall.

**You are not required to judge anything.** You say how hard a thing is before
the dice are rolled, and then you and your child find out together. That is a
much smaller job than it sounds from the outside, and it is the part children
find fair.

The Games Master's Guide opens with all of this in more detail, including the
part that is actually hard, which is not the rules.

## Read the actual thing

The best answer to "what happens in it?" is to read what happened.

*Go On Their Count* is a short novel transcribed from a real four-day run of
this campaign, played by a first-time roleplayer. The dialogue in it is the
dialogue from the table. Nobody tidied it up afterwards, and nothing in it is
hypothetical — a fifteen-year-old called Lint breaks an eighty-one-year-old
woman's wrist on page thirty because the player pushed when they had been told
not to, and the rest of the book is the consequence of that.

It is the most accurate picture of this game that exists.

**[Go On Their Count (PDF)](go-on-their-count.pdf)** — about a hundred and
fifty pages.
