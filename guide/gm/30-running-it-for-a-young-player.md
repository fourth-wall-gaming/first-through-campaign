# Running It for a Young Player

Everything in this chapter was learned by getting it wrong at a real table.
None of it is theory.

## Always move the action forward

The test is mechanical, which is why it is useful: **are characters rolling
skills and taking actions?** If nobody has rolled in two exchanges, the action
has stopped, whatever the prose is doing.

## Narrate their experience, not a story at them

Describe what they perceive and what their actions cause. Never perform a
finished scene — several people speaking, an arc, a closing line — and hand it
over for them to react to. That makes the player an audience with a steering
wheel.

## Hand back only where there is something for them to do

This is the one most easily misread. It does not mean *stop every turn*. It
means never stop in a lull. If an exchange is not theirs, compress it to its
result and keep going until their next real decision arrives — and never
manufacture a question at them in order to create a handover.

Length follows the handover, not a word count. A two-person back-and-forth is a
few lines. Four hours of a station preparing is two sentences. If the player
cannot act on it, it is one sentence.

## The player character is not a facilitator

Count what the character has physically done in the last hour. If the answer is
*fetched three people and carried their conclusions between them*, the player
has become a courier of narrative rather than a person with wants.

The mechanism that causes it is easy to build by accident: give every insight
to a Non-Player Character and the player's only remaining job is walking
between them. The fix is to make the thing the player wants drive a scene, and
to let a found object — a board, a sheet, a ledger — be read by **their** hands.

## Six characters, one voice

The tell is that everybody enumerates, everybody wants things on the record,
everybody is precise. That is not six people, it is you six times.

Assign that move to exactly one character — the Old Man, where it is
characterisation — and forbid it to everybody else. Then read each card before
its owner speaks.

## Never hand out a read

No interiority for anybody but the player's own character. No telling them who
is trustworthy. If Benno Raske is a problem, let them work it out from the fact
that he talked warmly to everybody on the bench and never once looked at the
glint.

## The player's mind is not yours

No thoughts, no decisions, and above all **no dialogue they did not write**. A
direction authorises the act, never the lines. *"Tell her what we found"* means
you may say that she told her. It does not mean you may write her speech.

## Ground them early if you honestly can

A first-through who has been taken off the rota has nothing to do and a station
to be curious in, and curiosity is the engine of this entire scenario. It is
the best move available to you and it costs nothing.

## Your player has a list of your failure modes

The Players' Guide ends with a chapter called *When the Gamesmaster Gets It
Wrong* — the same failures as above, written from the other side of the table,
with the sentence to say for each one. It was written for a player correcting
Claude, and every line of it works on a human just as well.

Read it. A player who knows they are allowed to interrupt will interrupt, and
an interruption two minutes in is worth an hour of a session quietly going
wrong.
