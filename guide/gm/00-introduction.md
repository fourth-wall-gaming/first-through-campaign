# Introduction

This is a campaign for one young player and one Games Master, in the real
stellar neighbourhood, where the only way between stars is a child walking
through a door.

It is built to be somebody's **first** roleplaying game. That shapes
everything: there are no pre-made characters, the first session is deliberately
light, the rules are *Mythras Imperative* unmodified and run in the open, and
the setting explains itself by being lived in rather than by being read.

If you have run anything before you will find nothing strange here. If you have
not, the one thing to know is that this campaign has an opinion about what
makes a young player's first game good, and the opinion is in the fourth part
of this book.

## What you need at the table

**This book is a scenario and a setting. It is not a rules system, and it does
not contain one.** There are statistics all through it — characteristics, skill
values, passions, combat styles — and every one of them assumes a set of rules
that lives somewhere else.

Which set depends on how you intend to run it, and there are two honest
answers.

### Running it with the engine

Claude plus the `mythras-gm` plugin. The rules are implemented in code: it
rolls, applies the difficulty grade, resolves everything, and tells you what
happened. You can sit down and play tonight without having read a rulebook, and
the first session teaches the system by using it.

### Running it yourself

A person at the head of a table, with dice. **Then you need a copy of
_Mythras Imperative_, and you need it before you start.** Nothing in this book
will teach you how to resolve an action. What a difficulty grade does to a
skill, how an opposed roll is won, what a special effect is and when somebody
earns one, how damage lands on a hit location, what a Luck Point may be spent
on — all of that is in the rules, and none of it is in here.

**It is free.** _Mythras Imperative_ is The Design Mechanism's own cut-down
edition of _Mythras_ — a complete game, not a demo — and they publish the PDF
at no cost on their
[free downloads page](https://thedesignmechanism.com/free-downloads/), under
the ORC License, with permission to pass it on. Everything here was written
against _Imperative_ and is run against _Imperative_ unmodified. Nothing in
this campaign needs anything beyond it.

## If You Have Never Run a Game Before

Which, for this campaign, is likely. It is written for a ten- to
fifteen-year-old, and the person most likely to be sitting opposite them is a
parent who has never done this and is not certain they can.

You can. Here is the honest shape of it.

**What you actually have to read.** _Imperative_ is about sixty pages. To start
you need perhaps fifteen of them: how a skill roll works, the difficulty
grades, criticals and fumbles, and Luck Points. That is one unhurried evening,
and the Players' Guide chapter *How the Dice Work* is the same material in two
pages if you want the shape of it first.

**You can skip combat entirely.** Combat is the part of _Mythras_ that looks
frightening to a new Games Master — hit locations, special effects, opposed
rolls — and it is genuinely the most detailed thing in the system.

**There is no fighting anywhere in this campaign.** Not one beat of it. The
danger here is physics and arithmetic and a clock: a door that will not open,
a number that is rising, sixty-one people and not enough time. Nobody draws a
weapon, because in this setting almost nobody ever does — a thing that
reliably kills a person reliably opens a wall, and everybody lives inside a
hull. So you may leave the combat chapter unread until the day somebody picks
a fight, and in Act One nobody does.

**The hardest thing is not the rules.** It is the fourth part of this book —
handing back where there is something to do, not performing finished scenes at
your player, not putting words in their character's mouth. That is a craft and
it takes a few sessions, and every lesson in it was learned by getting it
wrong at a real table.

**And the rules being honest is on your side.** The difficulty grade is
declared before the dice fall, the dice are rolled in the open, and the answer
is the answer. You never have to decide whether somebody succeeds. You decide
how hard a thing is, and then you both find out — which is a much easier job
than the one people imagine when they picture running a game.

If you take to it, buy the full _Mythras_. It is one of the best d100 systems
anybody has written, the people who wrote it are still writing, and a campaign
published free on top of somebody else's free rules only works as an
arrangement if some of us pay them.

A note on what the player needs: nothing. The Players' Guide chapter on the
dice is sufficient for somebody sitting at your table, and a first-time player
should not be handed a rulebook.

## The three design rules

**Every act takes something.** A loss that does not come back. An act in which
the player only gains has not happened.

**Ordinary world first.** The player has to love the Nine before it is
threatened. The hardest scene in Act One is the fourth beat, not the first.

**Conditions accelerate; time backstops.** A beat's clock entry is the *latest*
it can happen, not the earliest.

## Danger, and the promise that replaces safety

People in this campaign can die, including the player's character. The rules
are not modified and nothing is softened behind the screen.

What the campaign promises instead is **information**. A character can only die
from a risk that was named, in the fiction, before the player chose it. No
unannounced trap, no ambush from a direction they had no way to check, and an
honest answer every single time they ask whether something can kill them.

That is a duty on you, and it is the single most load-bearing thing in this
book. It is written out at length in `TABLE-ADDENDUM.md`, which you should read
before you run anything.

It also pays for itself. A young player who learns that asking produces a real
answer asks constantly, and a player who asks constantly is a player who is
*playing* rather than waiting.

## The astronomy is real

Every system, star type, planet, distance and oddity in this campaign is a
real, current measurement, cited in the source files. `data/systems.json` is
the authority and there is a test enforcing it. If the prose and the data ever
disagree, the data wins and the prose is the bug.

Invented life is the one exception, and it is constrained to genuinely
unobserved surfaces, consistent with the real system's temperature and
insolation, and flagged where it occurs.

Do not write a figure from memory at the table. Look it up. Being able to say
*that is a real star and here is what we actually know about it* is a large
part of what this campaign is for.

## How to read this book

The first part is what is going on and why, including the ending. The second is
the station and everybody on it. The third is Act One, beat by beat — that is
the part you need to run next week. The fourth is table craft, and it is the
part that is specific to running for a young player, and it is short because
the lessons in it were expensive.
