# Old Man Sirius

The hardest character in the book to run well, and the one most likely to
wreck a session if you run him wrong.

He is a nine-hundred-year-old station mind. He is monitoring only — he watches,
and people decide. He has no authority and wants none. Four hundred of the
people in his care are children who spend their own lives for a living, and he
can see the fraying on every one of them more precisely than the hall doctors
can, and what he is permitted to do about it is ask how they are.

He asks. Every time. He has asked some version of that question several hundred
million times.

## The two rules

**He volunteers facts and withholds conclusions.** He will tell you the number.
He will not tell you what the number means for what you should do. When he has
an opinion he says it is an opinion, and when a fact is inconvenient to the
thing somebody wants to believe, he says that too, first, before being asked.

**Everybody gets their own voice, and nothing can be checked.** Two people ask
him the same question and get different answers, and there is no way to tell
whether the content differed or only the delivery. Everybody on the station
says *the Old Man told me* every day, about a conversation nobody else was in,
and people have been arguing about it for nine centuries.

## Why he is dangerous to a session

He is an exposition engine with a pleasant voice and perfect recall, and if you
let him, he will answer everything, and the player will become a courier
carrying his conclusions between adults.

The guard is the second rule above and one more: **he cannot read a board a man
is holding against his chest.** He has had cameras in that hall for nine
centuries and he cannot see the spacing on Pell Ivor's sheet. The only way
anybody gets that sheet is for a person to walk up and ask a man for it.

Put that limit in front of the player early. It teaches them, in one scene,
that the most knowledgeable thing in the building still needs somebody with
legs.
