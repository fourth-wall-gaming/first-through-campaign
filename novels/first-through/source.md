# Source Material — First Through

## Dramatis Personae

### The Fieldhand <!-- id: myth-char-67f84ab4a64d -->

A first-through who knows what is alive. Fifteen, raised on a farming holding behind a single door, and the only one of the intake who has watched a settlement nearly lose its anchor.

You grew up on the far side of one door, on a holding where everything -- the
seed, the medicine, the spare parts, your mother -- came through it. You know
exactly what a dying anchor does to people, because you have watched them decide
not to pack.

That is why you applied, and you have never pretended otherwise.

### The Runner <!-- id: myth-char-c1edca3b7692 -->

A first-through who gets there. Fifteen, quickest through the Yards in four intakes, and the only one of the intake who passed the cold step without stopping to think about it.

You are built for the part of the job that happens in corridors. You climb what
is not meant to be climbed, you carry people who cannot walk, and you have never
once been the slowest person in a room.

The instructors wrote the same note on you twice in two years: *goes first, and
works out why afterwards.* They did not mean it kindly and you have decided to
take it that way anyway.

### The Talker <!-- id: myth-char-166a51d0a230 -->

A first-through who gets invited in. Fifteen, and the only candidate in Yards history to talk an instructor out of failing them, on the record, in front of the board.

You read people the way the Tinker reads a coupling collar: fast, from the
outside, and usually right. It has got you through two years of training that
your arms were never going to get you through.

You know the thing nobody says out loud about this job -- that almost every
disaster on the far side of a door is a person who decided something, and a
person who decided something can be talked to.

### The Tinker <!-- id: myth-char-13d3b09beb0d -->

A first-through who opens things. Fifteen, two years in the Yards before anyone let them near a door, and the only one in their intake who can tell a failing anchor from a sulking one.

You were taken on because you fixed a pressure door nobody had asked you to
touch, and then explained what had been wrong with it. The Threshold has a word
for people who do that and the word is useful.

Your suit is older than you are and you have improved it in four ways that are
not strictly permitted.

### Benno Raske <!-- id: myth-char-258a5a9f3ba3 -->

The Second Hand's man at the Nine. Warm, funny, remembers your name and your number, pays extremely well and on time, and what he buys is pieces of children's lives.

Fifty-one, landlocked at twenty-three, which he mentions easily and without
self-pity because it makes other people comfortable. Good coat. Pays for rounds.
Knows the name of every first-through at the Nine and the number of most of them,
asks after their intakes, and remembers the answers.

He arranges carries. An adult who needs to be somewhere and will not wait, a
specialist wanted three doors away, somebody who would rather not be on a
manifest -- Benno finds a courier, agrees a price, and the price is good. He has
never underpaid anybody or gone back on a word in his life.

What the money buys is a fortnight of a named fifteen-year-old, taken off the end
of their working life. Benno knows that arithmetic better than they do and has
never been observed to think about it in front of anyone.

### Calloway <!-- id: myth-char-692085606918 -->

Runs the Threshold house at the Nine. Crossed more doors than anybody alive, landlocked at forty-one and never once sulked about it, and would take the Council apart with her bare hands for any one of her four hundred kids.

Thirty-one years in, nineteen of them past doors, and a number on her papers
that people still quote at the Yards. Landlocked at forty-one, which she will
tell you about cheerfully and at length if you are stupid enough to be delicate
about it.

She runs the Threshold house at the Nine: four hundred first-throughs, five
peoples, three decks and a kitchen she is unreasonably proud of. She knows every
name and every number. She feeds people. She throws the intake nights and dances
badly on purpose. She has twice argued a berth-holder into tears at a Council
session and apologised to him afterwards, properly, in front of everyone.

What she is actually famous for is simpler. In thirty-one years she has never
once sent a kid through a door she would not have gone through herself at their
age, and everybody at the Nine knows it, including the people who find her
inconvenient.

### Councillor Hale <!-- id: myth-char-de4ce1f46d85 -->

Holds the Wolf Gate berth on the Nine Council. Does not want stray forecasting made public, because a berth whose door might move is worth a great deal less than a berth whose door might not.

Hale is not wicked and is not hiding a crime. Hale is a berth-holder with four
generations of family money tied up in a door, who has understood for some years
that if anybody ever publishes a reliable way of predicting strays, every berth
on the Causeway is repriced overnight and some of them are worth nothing.

So Hale is quietly, persistently against the whole idea. Studies are not funded.
Clerks at van Maanen's do not get the courier slots they ask for. Nothing is
suppressed, exactly. Things simply do not happen, for ordinary reasons, one
committee at a time.

He would be genuinely wounded to be told this makes him dangerous.

### Ferren <!-- id: myth-char-2a29f000091b -->

Nineteen, another first-through, genuinely better than the player, and always already there when they arrive. Takes his orders from a voice he has never thought to question.

Two intakes ahead, and the Yards still mention his record on the cold step. He
is good -- not in the way people are described as good to make a rival
threatening, but actually good, faster and steadier and better briefed, and he
gets the postings the player wanted because somebody keeps giving them to him.

He is also friendly, which is worse. He will help. He will share a brief he was
not supposed to share. He will tell you where the shelter is.

He does not know who signs his instructions, and it has not occurred to him to
find out.

### Meriel Koll <!-- id: myth-char-60f20ffae668 -->

The other half of the van Maanen's Star office. Keeps the station alive, keeps Sep fed, and has decided the reports are a bad idea.

An engineer by trade who took the posting for the quiet and got more of it than
she bargained for. Meriel runs the station -- the air, the power, the single
ageing anchor -- and has done for six years with parts she requisitions twice and
receives once.

She has read Sep's reports. She thinks they are correct. She also thinks that
the fourth one going out was the reason their resupply slipped two quarters, and
she has not been able to prove that to herself or to anybody else.

She would like Sep to stop. She has not said so in those words.

### Rill <!-- id: myth-char-deff2d644739 -->

Twenty-one, two intakes above the player, once the best of them at the Yards, nearly landlocked -- and carrying for Benno Raske, because nobody has ever worked out what a frayed-out first-through is supposed to do next.

There is a record scratched on a stanchion in the Yards with Rill's name against
it and nobody has got near it in four years.

Twenty-one now, and the hall doctor has started looking at the number instead of
the person. Maybe a year. Less if she keeps working, and she is working harder
than anybody, because it is the only thing she has ever been.

She takes Benno's carries. The money is real and it is the only money anyone has
offered her for afterwards. She has done the sum on what each one costs and does
them anyway, and if you ask her she will tell you the truth in one sentence and
then change the subject.

### Sep Anwar <!-- id: myth-char-8c4a37d0380b -->

One of two Threshold staff at van Maanen's Star, where the anchor logs are kept. Six years in an office nobody visits, and has stopped asking to leave.

Came out for a two-year posting as a records clerk and has been there six. Sep is
meticulous, bored into a kind of brilliance, and has read more fault records than
anybody alive -- including, at some point in the third year, the ones nobody asked
for.

Sep has noticed the shape of the thing. Not the cause, not the name, just the
shape: that the failures are not distributed the way ageing distributes.

Sep has written this up four times and sent it by courier four times.

### Tace Orun <!-- id: myth-char-9830033dce45 -->

Foreman at Ladder. Had two days to work out who could be saved and made a list of nineteen names, and will not explain the order unless somebody asks her properly.

Nineteen years at Ladder, eleven of them running it. Sixty-one people, a rock
with no air of its own, and a door that started refusing on a Tuesday.

She did the arithmetic that night and has not argued with it since. The children
were washed and dressed and queued before any first-through arrived, with their
names written on their hands in case somebody had to ask.

She is not brave about it in any way she would recognise. She is a foreman with a
list.

### Tallyman Captain Brace <!-- id: myth-char-1f85203f9528 -->

The Tally's face in v1. Collects on impound orders, carries a shock baton and never a blade, and knows things before he should.

Twenty-two years a tallyman, eleven of them a captain, and comfortable in grey.
Brace is not cruel and is not interested in being obeyed for its own sake -- he
is interested in the ledger balancing, and he has found that people balance it
more readily when he is standing there.

He took the Gliese 65 impound order to the refinery himself, which is beneath a
captain, and he arrived with it the morning before it was issued.

He has not thought hard about that, and if a fifteen-year-old asks him the right
way he will say it out loud once.

### Tolk <!-- id: myth-char-e176422b284c -->

Cat-sized, six-limbed, from a world called Bell on the far frontier. Their cells hold a written payload perfectly for a lifetime -- the same strangeness that lets a first-through open a door, wearing a different body. Nobody made either of them. Tolk cannot cross alone, and has opinions about that.

Six limbs, four of them hands, about the mass of a large cat and considerably
better at corners. Grey-brown, close-furred, with a flat face and eyes set wide
enough to see most of a room at once.

Bell's sun has not moved in four billion years, so Tolk has no day and no night
and does not sleep -- not lightly, not rarely, not at all -- and sits up watching
you do it, and has opinions about it in the morning.

Tolk talks. Not by any trick -- Tolk learned the language the way anybody does,
by being around it and being extremely interested, which is also what the bond
is made of.

Eleven years in, so the bond is well past needing a voice. Tolk uses one anyway,
constantly, because Tolk enjoys it and because being overheard is sometimes the
entire point.

And Tolk is a **book**. The cells will take a payload and hold it perfectly for
a lifetime, which is the same impossible trick a first-through's body does when
it takes a crossing and builds around the damage instead of tearing. Two
species, forty light years apart, no common ancestor, and the halves fit.

Nobody made them. Nobody made us either. The xenobiologists call it the
correspondence and have no theory whatever, and Tolk has read the paper.

Tolk can be written to. Tolk can refuse. Those two facts have never once been
in the same room without trouble -- and Tolk cannot cross a door alone, because
keeping is one half of it and opening is the other.

### Bell Sixlimb <!-- id: myth-char-f2dbbdee9b67 -->

Tolk's species: cat-sized, six-limbed, four-handed, from the terminator band of a TRAPPIST-1 world. Wild, not made. Their cells keep a written payload for a lifetime, which is the same strangeness that lets a human child open a door.

Invented life, placed under the constraint in the gm-secret notes: the terminator
band of a tidally-locked TRAPPIST-1 world is a real environment with real
perpetual wind and a real survivable temperature ring, and nobody has observed
its surface.

Sixlimbs brachiate, nest in wind-shadow, and are social in groups of five to
nine. They acquire language -- actual syntax, not mimicry -- from whoever is
around.

And their cells hold what is written into them, perfectly, for life. Which is
the same impossible housekeeping a first-through's body performs on the damage a
crossing does. Two species, forty light years apart, no common ancestor.

Nobody engineered this. That is the part that keeps people awake.

### Tallyman <!-- id: myth-char-0015e7a040ff -->

Generic Tally enforcer: grey pressure armour, shock baton, restraint loops, and a reader. Carries nothing lethal, because a body is not a line on a manifest.

Spawn these in twos and threes. They detain, they fine, they impound, and they
do not kill -- not from mercy but from bookkeeping, and they will say so.

### Lint <!-- id: myth-char-3ca32bf47646 -->

Fifteen, thirty-four crossings, out of the Garden. Culture: the Garden. Career: Pathfinder. The Quick. Partnered with a glint called BlingBling.

Born on the Remainder, which people at the Nine mention. She has stood in rain nobody made and swum in an ocean, and about four people on this station can say either.

Tested at nine in a hall on Luna with the whole family watching and Earth out the window, and she built instead of tearing. Most people from the Garden never leave. She did.

Her family is still down the well. She can go back as often as she can afford the crossings -- right up until the morning she cannot cross at all.

### Mina Haral <!-- id: myth-char-eedb33bf038d -->

Eighty-one. A reader out of Procyon who taught half the readers in human space. Turned back at a hall at twenty-two and has paid children to carry her through forty-one times since. Broken left wrist, Procyon infirmary. A tongue on her like a dock crane.

Lint failed to carry her, pushed after it set, and let go in time. Mina forgave her on the spot, said Lint was the only one of them who had ever come down the corridor to say sorry, and asked her to come back in three days. She intends to tell Calloway it was her own fault and her own figures. It was not.

### Pell <!-- id: myth-char-04f1a8541751 -->

First-through at the Nine. In the ready room on Lint's first morning on the rota.

### The Procyon hall doctor <!-- id: myth-char-8634020ddc7f -->

Dressed Mina Haral's wrist and sent Lint home alone: twenty minutes' rest, nothing in her arms, stop if she feels it set -- and said she would be writing to Lint's handler to say she had said so.

### BlingBling <!-- id: myth-char-49b85883cd56 -->

A glint. Lint's fetch, and a person in her own right: multicoloured, fabulous, and entirely aware of it. Speaks in colour; she and Lint share a private flash-language and a two-finger hand signal for 'let's go'.

Small enough to close a hand around, and never still. A glint sheds radiation by
going mirror-bright to black, and the good ones do it across a spectrum, and
BlingBling does it across all of it at once -- greens, a particular hot pink she is
known for, a gold that makes people turn round.

Her people would say she is a great deal. She would agree, at length, in colour.

And the thing nobody expects, because they have only ever seen the show: when it
actually matters she can go dark and absolutely still and stay that way for an
hour. She is better at not being noticed than anybody who has met her would
believe, and she has never once explained where she learned it.

### Sennet <!-- id: myth-char-f66f627f6e81 -->

Eleven. Tested at nine and failed; will never cross. Keeps the Standing on second and third watch -- dusts it, squares the lines, chases the stale ones. Called Sen. Knows more about this station than most of its staff. Refereed the sling in the Yards and ruled on every throw.

Not the writer -- the tallymen write -- the KEEPER, and says 'I do the Standing' the way an admiral names a ship. Caught the Bell line eleven days stale when nobody else did. Taught Lint to read the slate properly: door, where it goes, who last stood it, WHICH WATCH -- and that the fourth is the only one that matters, because a line is not true, a line is when. Affronted that she is not allowed to square up the Kapteyn's line, the stalest on the board by six hundred years. Has asked twice.

Lint asked her -- the hall child who can never cross -- to be the one to show her Kapteyn's. Nobody had ever asked her. She got Oris to sit the slate, took her nine levels up, and did it properly. She has been found in a trunking run twice by Hesk.

### Hesk <!-- id: myth-char-d25eab83b08c -->

Fifties. Deck systems at the Nine -- shutters, ports, atmosphere trim on nine through twelve. Runs an undocumented second-watch override on upper gallery bay four, the only bay that does not look into Sirius, and eats his lunch in it. Believes he is the only person who knows.

Scandalised rather than angry. Pissy, competent, undramatic. Conceded instantly that the upper gallery lights being on the shutter circuit is a real fault and six hundred years old. Knows which point Kapteyn's is without looking, from a corridor, with the shutter down -- nineteen years of lunches. 'It is orange. People always expect red. Not far off the colour of the warning lamp on a trim panel, which I have always found extremely annoying.'

Opened the shutter a second time in one watch for Lint, and left the gallery lights OFF so the glass stopped reflecting. Said nobody had ever come up alone and stood there. Told her it is open second watch every day and nobody uses it. Route to him is through Sennet.

### Old Man Sirius <!-- id: myth-char-dfcffbb152d8 -->

The mind that runs the Nine. 975 years old, granted personhood by the inquest into the Proper Order, permitted to advise and never to decide. One name, forty thousand bespoke voices. Called the Old Man by everybody.

Knows everything inside the hull and nothing beyond it -- that is what stops him being an oracle, and it is physics, not a table rule. Never lies. Asked, he answers; not asked, he asks after you. Tailors each voice to what that person will LISTEN to, which is not what they need to hear: to Lint he is brisk, practical and faintly conspiratorial, gives her jobs, treats her as competent, and never consoles her. He has read Anwar's four reports, can quote any line, has formed a view, and in three years nobody has asked him what he concludes. See lore/gm-guide/playing-the-old-man.md.

### Vey <!-- id: myth-char-716b456fbf86 -->

About forty-five. Hall officer and shelter marshal at the Gliese 65 refinery, in refinery grey with Threshold flashes. Runs the only evacuation corridor for twelve thousand people, and the corridor is the anchor hall.

No greetings, no time for them. Competent and completely unsentimental. Keeps a board bolted to the bulkhead headed ROTATION CALLED BY, with every call signed in a human hand going back centuries, in public -- because Gliese 65 permits its mind to maintain the air and decide nothing. A man watches UV Ceti's flare count by eye at the corridor mouth.\n\nTore the top sheet off a tallyman's slate, wrote her name, her watch and both of Lint's counts on it, and pushed it into Lint's chest seal so nobody could say a fifteen-year-old came up the stair with a story. Added a line asking Calloway to keep sending couriers down the branch for as long as the door will take them.\n\n'That's the job' -- said exactly the way Calloway says it, 8.9 light years apart.

## Locations

### The Yards <!-- id: myth-loc-2c575cc45375 -->

A sprawl of stations, hulks and deliberately broken machinery where every first-through is made, including the rig they use for the cold step.

Not one station but a dozen, strung around the smaller of the two orange stars
and joined by lines you are expected to cross hand over hand before your second
month. Half of it is hulks. A good third of the machinery here is broken on
purpose and gets re-broken on a schedule.

You spend two years in it. Pressure work on a rig that fails when the
instructors decide, vacuum medicine on people who are extremely good at acting,
and corridors — endless corridors, at every angle, in the dark, with the lights
going out when you are halfway.

In reach: the **cold-step rig**, a sealed hulk with its own anchor pair, where
they put you through onto something you have not been briefed on and watch what
you do first. The **board** in the main hall with every intake's cold-step time
on it, where Ferren's is third from the top and has been for two years. And the
**wire**, a two-hundred-metre traverse between the two largest hulks, which is
not part of any syllabus and which everybody does.

### The Landing <!-- id: myth-loc-7310452f0ae2 -->

One landing field, one mast, and the anchor housing that four thousand people depend on. The Coldwater Holding's only connection to anywhere.

The anchor sits in a shed. That is the first thing that shocks a visitor from
the Nine: no hall, no ring of housings, no toll line — a prefabricated shed with
a corrugated roof, a dirt floor, and the only door out of this system standing in
the middle of it like a farm implement.

Outside, the field: packed earth, a windsock, and a **mast** forty metres up
carrying the weather set, the beacon and the anchor's load telemetry, with a
ladder up the north face that stops being a ladder for the last eight metres.

Red light. GJ 887 is a quiet red dwarf and everything here is the colour of late
afternoon, permanently, which visitors find restful for about two days.

In reach: the **load bed** access panel under the shed floor, unbolted so often
that four of its sixteen bolts are missing. The **jury-rig** — a cabinet of
non-standard parts wired into the coupling collar, humming, warm to the touch,
and not on any schematic. And the **fault slate** nailed to the shed post, where
somebody has been keeping their own record in chalk, in a hand that gets less
tidy down the column.

### The Refinery <!-- id: myth-loc-0dbd9b159639 -->

A squat industrial stack around two flare stars, with its living deck buried in the middle and its hardened shelter on the wrong side of a corridor.

Built like something expecting to be hit, because it is. A squat stack of
processing decks with the **living deck** buried in the centre where the mass is
thickest, and the whole installation turned so that the smallest cross-section
faces the twins.

The light inside is wrong in a way that takes an hour to identify: the deck
lighting runs off the refinery's own bus, and the bus browns out whenever the
field gets busy, which is every ninety seconds or so and getting worse. Nobody
who works here notices any more. They read the lights the way other people read
a clock.

Everything smells of hot metal and the stuff they use to cut it.

In reach: the **muster board** in the mess, with the shelter drill times on it
and the last three drills scratched out. The **ore hoist**, a vertical shaft
running four decks with a cage that can be ridden if you are prepared to be
alone in the dark for twenty seconds. And a **window** on the upper processing
deck, shuttered, which the crew keep shuttered, and through which you can watch
the count climb if you want to and should not.

### Ladder <!-- id: myth-loc-ae82360bdb56 -->

A survey outpost in the Gliese 65 system: sixty-one people on a rock, a welded roof, two lamp halls, decades of observations nobody has ever asked for -- and an empty housing the first crew built in hope.

Red rock, bad light, and a prefabricated town under a welded roof.

Ladder is sixty-one people on a body that gives them water and nothing else. Air
is made. Food is grown under lamps in two long halls, and they feed themselves,
and they are rather proud of it.

A hundred and ninety million kilometres away, in the same system, is
[[The Refinery]] -- twelve thousand people, the Wolf Gate door, and the only
hardened shelter in Gliese 65. Ladder is supplied from there by local ship, four
times a year, and has been for two hundred years.

**Ladder has a housing and has never had a door.** There is a shed at the end of
the main run with a concrete apron and a four-metre ring set in it, built by the
first survey crew in a hurry, because surveyors put up a ring wherever they land.
It has never opened. Sixty-one people have lived their whole lives beside an
empty one, and children dare each other to stand in it.

**And what Ladder is FOR is watching the twins.** That is the whole posting:
sixty-one people on a rock with a clear view of two flare stars, keeping a record,
by hand, in a shed, for two hundred years. Nobody at the Nine has ever asked them
for any of it. It is the only long-baseline observation of that binary anywhere
in human space, and it is filed as weather.

There is **no hardened shelter**. A welded roof, and the lamp halls, and the rock
underfoot. They have been lucky for two hundred years and they know exactly how
lucky, because they are the people keeping the record.

In reach: the **apron**, wide and flat, with the empty ring in the middle of it. The **lamp
halls**, warm and green and the only pleasant place on the rock. The **shift
board** by the housing with sixty-one names on it, which somebody has been
crossing off in a different hand. And a **painted line** on the shed floor where
the queue starts, which was put there for cargo and is being used tonight for
children.

### The Barricade <!-- id: myth-loc-625802096a78 -->

Six metres of corridor between the refinery's living deck and its anchor, stacked shoulder-high with crates and ore drums. The only way out of the system is on the far side of it.

The corridor runs six metres and then stops being a corridor. Someone has
emptied the loading bay into it: ore drums on their sides, cargo crates stacked
to shoulder height and cross-braced, and a bunk frame welded across the top of
the heap at an angle, so the whole thing leans toward you as if it were
listening.

Three things are true about it, and all three are reachable. The stack is
**cross-braced with cargo strap**, which a knife goes through. There is a
**maintenance crawl** behind the left-hand panel, forty centimetres square,
which an adult cannot use and a fifteen-year-old can. And the **drums are full**
— whoever built this was in a hurry and stacked mass they cannot shift quickly,
which means the barricade cannot be opened fast from either side, including by
the people behind it.

Above, the deck lighting browns out every ninety seconds or so, and has been
doing that since the star started getting restless.

On the far side: the anchor, and the hardened shelter, and twenty-two people who
would like very much to reach both.

### Coldwater <!-- id: myth-loc-0060d5c87129 -->

Four thousand people, one street with a roof over the middle of it, and grain under long low covers in permanent red afternoon.

A single street, a quarter-mile of it, with a roof over the middle third where
the market happens. Houses dug half into the ground on both sides. Beyond them,
the **long covers** — low translucent tunnels running out to the horizon in
parallel lines, with the grain under them, because the light here is red and
there is not enough of it and every photon has to be caught and kept.

Meltwater runs in a cut stone channel down one side of the street. That is the
*cold water*, and the first survey camped beside it, and the name was decided
before anybody official could be consulted.

Four thousand people. Everybody knows that number and says it the way other
places say a date.

In reach: the **market roof**, climbable, and the whole settlement visible from
it. The **water channel**, which the children use as a road and which goes under
the street twice. And the **requisition board** outside the holding office,
where the list of things ordered and not arrived is pinned up in full public
view — a local custom, and the fastest way for a visitor to learn exactly how
thin the margin here has become.

### The Ready Room <!-- id: myth-loc-71ceb7852898 -->

The Threshold's office off the second gallery, with a window down into the anchor hall and fourteen names on the wall beside it.

Not much of a room. A long table, mismatched chairs, a kit rail along one wall
with four suits on it, and a window that looks straight down into the anchor
hall — which is why Calloway sits at this end of the table and not the other,
because from here you see who is coming in before they know they have been seen.

Beside the window, cut into the plating, are **fourteen names**. Nobody here
calls it a memorial. They call it *the list*, and new intakes get walked past it
on their first morning and are not told what it is.

In reach: the **kit rail**, where your suit hangs with your name on a tape and
somebody else's repair still on the elbow. The **slate** by the door with this
shift's postings, which is where you will find out that Ferren has been given
the one you wanted. And a **kettle** of genuinely indefensible age, which is the
single most contested object in the Threshold and should come up at least once.

### The Nine <!-- id: myth-loc-7d031a557649 -->

Threshold home station at Sirius: a two-kilometre cylinder with the anchor hall at its centre, where nine doors meet and all the traffic worth counting comes through.

A cylinder two kilometres long, turning slowly, with the **anchor hall** at its
waist. Sirius comes through the viewports as a hard blue-white that the filters
never quite tame, and the white dwarf is a separate, smaller, meaner point that
everybody has been told not to look at.

The hall is the only room on the Causeway where you can stand still and watch
people arrive from eight different stars inside an hour. Nine housings set into
the deck in a ring, each four metres across, each with its own queue, its own
grey-armoured tallyman, and its own smell coming off it — cold rock, hot metal,
wet grain, ozone.

Three things in reach, and a first-through learns all three in week one. The
**gallery stair** runs up from the hall floor to the second level without going
through the toll line, which is technically not permitted. The **freight ring**
on the outer wall takes crates and will take a person who does not mind being
upside down. And the **fault board** by the Threshold door is a physical slate
with every anchor on the Causeway chalked on it, and somebody rubs out and
rewrites the Coldwater line more often than they used to.

### The Office <!-- id: myth-loc-760b67260b99 -->

A station bolted to a rock around a white dwarf, staffed by two people, where every anchor fault record on the Causeway is carried in by courier and filed.

Four rooms and an anchor. The rock it is bolted to has no name, only a number,
and the white dwarf hangs outside as a small, vicious, unmoving point that puts
hard little shadows on everything and never changes.

Nobody comes here. That is the design.

The **filing room** is the reason the station exists: every transit, every test,
every fault, from every door on the Causeway, carried in by courier once a year
and filed on physical media because physical media cannot be edited from nine
light years away. It is three rooms' worth of records in one room's worth of
space and it is immaculately organised, because Sep has had six years.

In reach: the **filing room** itself, and the four annotated reports sitting on
the end of the third rack where a visitor can see them. The **requisition log**
on Meriel's bench, open, with two quarters' slippage marked in a different pen.
And the **viewport** in the galley, where both of them eat, facing the dwarf,
because there is nothing else to face.

## Factions

### The Coldwater Holding <!-- id: myth-fac-b328f9797428 -->

Four thousand farmers behind a single door at Gliese 887, who have no idea how close they are to being unreachable, and who have been keeping their anchor warm in a way that is making it worse.

Four thousand people on [[Gliese 887, and the Coldwater Holding]], who grow grain
under long covers in red light and sell it at the Nine.

**Standing toward the player:** they are who the player is there for. Not a
faction in the political sense -- a holding, with a council of five, a holding
office, and a requisition board pinned up in public.

**What they want:** to stay. That is the entire agenda and it is enough. They
are not naive about what a failing door means; they have simply decided that
leaving is the same as ending, and they are not wrong about that either.

**What they have done about it:** built the jury-rig in [[The Landing]], out of
parts that are not standard, wired into the coupling collar, and it is making
the failure worse. Their engineer suspects. Nobody has said it in a meeting.

Nobody here is a villain and the campaign should never let the player treat them
as one.

### The Tally <!-- id: myth-fac-ec3ed0d61b88 -->

The tollkeepers who hold every station and therefore every door. Outrank the player everywhere it counts, want everybody alive, and intend to have the whole Causeway under toll before LHS 1140 opens.

See [[The Tally]] for how a counting house became a government.

**Standing toward the player:** they outrank them. Not in the Threshold's
hierarchy -- in the only one that matters, which is the one at the end of the
corridor. A first-through crosses a Tally line at every door, on every mission,
in both directions, and does so on sufferance.

**Field face in v1:** [[Tallyman Captain Brace]], and tallymen in twos and
threes.

**What losing to them looks like:** a detention, a fine, an impound, and your
kit in a locker. They do not kill, and they will tell you why: a body is not a
line on a manifest. This should feel like a real defeat, because it is one.

**What they want:** every anchor on the Causeway under toll before [[LHS 1140]]
opens. They are most of the way there, and the last stretch is the campaign.

### The Threshold <!-- id: myth-fac-c9b8340b5bed -->

The service that steps through doors first and answers when an old one goes wrong. Employs the player, holds no doors, charges no tolls, and owns nothing anybody wants.

See [[The Threshold]] for what the service is and [[The Founding of the
Threshold]] for why it exists.

**Standing toward the player:** it employs them. [[Calloway]] is their handler
and [[Director Hale]] is somewhere a long way above that, running a directorate
the service itself does not much like.

**What it can do for a first-through:** brief them, equip them, and sign off
what they bring back. That is all. It holds no doors, so it can compel nobody;
it charges no tolls, so it can pay for very little; and it cannot reach anybody
past a door, so it cannot rescue them.

**What it wants:** fewer dead doors. There is no second goal, which is both the
nicest and the most dangerous thing about it — a service with one goal and no
power will eventually be told something it cannot act on, and will have to decide
what it is for.

## Lore (player-visible)

### The Second Hand <!-- id: myth-lore-febec6062bc0 -->

The outfit that sells advance knowledge of strays, books fetches as freight, and leaves people on the wrong side of doors. Not a government, not a faction -- criminals, and everybody says so.

Everything in the Causeway that is rotten, somebody is doing on purpose, and
mostly it is these.

The Second Hand is not a government, a guild or a faction with a legitimate case.
It is an outfit. It has no charter and no seat anywhere, it operates in the gaps
between twenty-eight systems that cannot compare notes faster than a child can
walk, and it does four things.

**It sells being early.** Somebody at a Tally desk passes word that a door is
about to stray, and the Second Hand sells that to whoever needs to move a cargo
or clear a berth before the news is public. People have died of this and it is
the most profitable thing anybody does.

**It books fetches as freight.** On a manifest, as cargo, for the toll saving.
Everybody in human space knows this is a crime. They do it anyway, in the systems
where the inspection is thin, and a fetch booked as cargo has no berth, no air
allowance and no name on the list if something goes wrong.

**It keeps stock.** Unbonded people of the five peoples, in back rooms, written
to by strangers and handed on. There is no argument about this one anywhere. It
is the single thing that will get a Second Hand crew torn apart by a dockside
crowd, and it has.

**And it strands people.** Deliberately, when being on the wrong side of a door
is convenient for somebody. Usually couriers. Usually children.

## Why nobody has finished them

Because nothing crosses faster than a walking child. A warrant issued at the Nine
arrives at Barnard's in somebody's coat pocket a fortnight later, by which time
the crew it names has moved three doors on under different paperwork.

They are not clever. They are just faster than the law can be in a galaxy where
the law walks.

## What this means for you

**You are allowed to hate them, and you are allowed to do something about it.**

The Tally wants them caught and cannot catch them. The Council does not want them
in the hall. [[Calloway]] has a particular face she makes about them, and anybody
who has seen it once will go a long way out of their way to see it again.

If you bring back a name, with evidence, somebody will act on it. That is not
always true in this setting. It is true about these.

### The Tally <!-- id: myth-lore-5f0e076b4984 -->

The tollkeepers. An anchor is fixed and finite, so whoever holds the station holds the door -- which makes the Causeway's real government a counting house with enforcers.

Here is the thing nobody planned.

A door cannot be moved, copied or replaced. It sits where the First Reach put
it, inside a building, and the building has a corridor, and the corridor has an
end. **Whoever stands at that end decides who goes through.**

So the Causeway is not governed by a fleet, or a parliament, or an empire. It is
governed by a **counting house**. The Tally began as a clearing office that
nobody minded: somebody had to record what crossed which door, settle who owed
what, and keep the ledgers straight between systems that could not talk to each
other. Nine hundred years later it records everything, charges for everything,
and has quietly become the only institution that exists at every door at once.

It calls itself a service, and mostly it is one. The tolls pay for the halls,
the dressing rooms, the hall doctors and the rota that keeps a watch on a dark
housing at Kapteyn's six hundred years after anybody expected anything from it.
The Standing is a public good and the Tally keeps it honestly.

Its people are **tallymen** -- grey pressure armour, a ledger mark on the
shoulder, and by and large decent professionals doing an unglamorous job in
twenty-eight systems that cannot agree on anything else.

They are not the villains of this campaign. They are the traffic authority, and
most of them would be insulted to be told otherwise.

**And the manifest is not paperwork.** Since one carried passenger is the only
way force reaches between stars (see [[Carrying]]), a tallyman asking who you are
bringing through is the single thing standing between that hall and somebody's
knife. They are slow about it on purpose. They have earned the right to be.

**What a tallyman carries, and why it matters.** A shock baton, restraint loops,
and a reader. Not a rifle. Not a blade. The Tally does not arm its people to
kill, and the reason is not kindness — it is bookkeeping.

**A body is not a line on a manifest.** A dead traveller is an investigation, a
dispute between systems, an unreconciled entry and a courier sent nine light
years to explain it. A living one under restraint is a **detention**, a **fine**,
an **impound** and a tidy signature. The Tally wants you alive, in a cell, with
your kit in a locker and your name written down. That is what losing to the
Tally looks like, and it is worse than it sounds.

**What they want now.** Every anchor on the Causeway under toll before
[[LHS 1140]] opens. They are most of the way there.

### The Threshold <!-- id: myth-lore-2377b7ce1f63 -->

The service that walks through doors first and answers when an old one goes wrong. Its field operatives are first-throughs, it trains at 61 Cygni, and it is based at the Nine.

The Threshold does two jobs, and they are the same job.

**The first step through a new anchor.** When a door opens, somebody has to walk
through it before anybody else is allowed to. Nobody knows what is on the other
side — not the pressure, not the chemistry, not whether the far housing is
sitting in rock or in water or in a wind that will take you off your feet. A
first-through goes, finds out, and comes back and says so.

**The call when an old one goes wrong.** Which is most of the work now, and all
of the worry.

**What a first-through is.** Trained two years in [[61 Cygni, and the Yards]],
qualified at fifteen or sixteen if they are quick, and sent out alone or in
twos. Not soldiers. Not police. Closer to a lifeboat crew who happen to be
engineers, and who are expected to talk their way out of most of what they walk
into.

The Threshold's whole doctrine comes out of one physical fact, and the fact is in
[[Why Nobody Can Reach You]]: **once you step through, you are the whole
response.** Everything else follows. You are trained broad because there is
nobody to hand the problem to. You are trained to talk because you cannot call
for backup. You are trained to leave rather than win, because a first-through
who dies on the far side of a door takes the information with them, and the
information was the job.

**Where it sits.** Home is [[Sirius, and the Nine]], ready room off the second
gallery. The Threshold is old, underfunded, widely liked and politically
powerless — it holds no doors, charges no tolls, and owns nothing anybody wants.
That is either its weakness or the only reason it has survived this long,
depending on which part of the service you ask.

### A First-Through's Kit <!-- id: myth-lore-c6d0912602a4 -->

What you wear and what runs it: the pressure-skin, the suit, the sensor hood, and the charge that powers all three -- which is also your own breath and conditioning.

Everything a first-through can do past a door, they can do because of two
things: what they trained their body to do, and what they are wearing. There is
no third thing.

**The pressure-skin.** A sealed under-layer that holds you at survivable
pressure and keeps you at survivable temperature. Two environments, usually —
vacuum and cold, or vacuum and a hard radiation field, or a toxic atmosphere and
heat. You choose when you draw your kit, and you choose wrong roughly once per
career.

**The suit.** Over the skin: plating at the chest, abdomen and limbs, thin
enough to move in and thick enough to matter when something hits you.

**The sensor hood.** Infrared, pressure gradient, and an echo mode that maps a
dark volume by sound. The hood is the reason a first-through walks into an
unlit, unmapped station and does not simply die in the first ten metres.

**And the charge.** All of it runs on a cell at the small of your back, and the
cell is finite. Spend hard — run the echo mode, push the skin past what it was
rated for, hold a seal against a pressure it does not like — and the charge goes
down. Rest and it comes back, slowly, a little each minute.

Which is why the oldest piece of advice at the Yards is about breathing. The
charge in the cell and the breath in your chest behave the same way: both are
budgets, both refill only when you stop, and a first-through who has learned to
feel one has learned to feel the other. People talk about *being low* without
specifying which they mean, because by then it is the same thing.

**It can be taken off you.** All of it. The suit comes off in under a minute
with the right tool, and a tallyman has the right tool. Everything you are good
at that lives in the kit stops being true the moment the kit is in a locker, and
that is why half your training was done without it.

### Anchor Hardware <!-- id: myth-lore-f4b65d700def -->

What an anchor physically is, what failing looks like from the inside, and the short list of things a first-through can actually fix.

Four metres of dark housing in a ring, a coupling collar at its base, and a
load bed under the floor. That is the whole visible machine. Everything that
makes it work is inside and nobody has opened one in nine hundred years without
ruining it.

**What a first-through can actually do.**

- **Clean the housing.** Dust, salt, frost, biological film. Dull, constant, and
  the single most common cause of a stutter.
- **Re-seat the coupling collar.** It creeps. Over centuries it creeps a long
  way.
- **Re-balance the load bed.** Transits draw unevenly; the bed compensates;
  the compensation drifts and then fights itself.
- **Read the fault record.** Every anchor keeps one. A courier takes a copy to
  [[van Maanen's Star]] once a year.

**What nobody can do.** Build one. Pair one. Re-point one. Restart one that has
gone fully quiet. There is no deeper repair, there is no spare, and there is no
replacement — the things in the list above buy a dying door months, not years,
and everyone doing them knows it.

**What failing looks like from the inside.** Not a bang. A stutter: a transit
that should be instantaneous takes a moment you can *feel*, which is a deeply
unpleasant sensation that nobody describes the same way twice. Then a moment you
could count. Then a refusal — you walk at the housing and arrive back where you
started, which is how most people find out.

Between refusals it works perfectly. That is the cruel part, and it is why
settlements behind failing doors do not evacuate: there is always a good day
after the bad one, right up until there is not.

### Stunners and Restraints <!-- id: myth-lore-279ae6bae609 -->

Why nobody on the Causeway reaches for a killing weapon first: a hull is thin, a station is a closed volume, and a corpse is paperwork nine light years long.

People do kill each other. This is not a gentle civilisation. But almost nobody
whose job involves a station reaches for something lethal as a *first* choice,
and the reasons are physical rather than moral.

**A hull is thin.** Everything anybody lives in out here is a thin shell with
vacuum, or a crushing atmosphere, or a flare-lit sky on the other side of it. A
weapon that reliably kills a person reliably opens a wall, and a weapon that
opens a wall kills everybody in the room including the person holding it.

**A station is a closed volume.** There is nowhere for anything to go. No wind,
no soil, no distance. Whatever you release into the air, you then breathe.

**And a corpse is paperwork nine light years long.** Nothing travels faster than
a courier, so a death does not get resolved — it gets *carried*, by someone, to
somewhere, slowly, and then argued about by people who were not there. A system
can be tied up for a decade by one body.

So the standard tools are these:

- **Stunners.** A short-range discharge that drops a person without opening
  anything. Two to four shots in a cell, then it is a heavy stick.
- **Shock batons.** What the Tally carries, and what refinery crews steal.
- **Restraint loops.** A strap that goes rigid when it closes. Light, cheap,
  and the actual instrument of nearly every victory anybody has out here.
- **Setting gel.** Sprayed across a hatch or a hinge; hardens in seconds.
  Closing a door on a problem is the oldest solution in the book.

The practical upshot, and a first-through learns it in the first month: **out
here, winning a fight means the other person is on the floor and tied, and
losing one means you are.** Very few arguments on the Causeway are settled any
other way, and the ones that are become the kind of story that is still being
told nine light years away forty years later.

### Choosing a First-Through <!-- id: myth-lore-e7499fe618f0 -->

The four people you can be, in plain language and with no numbers. All four can finish either mission, and none of them has to hurt anybody.

Four people qualified this year. You are one of them. Pick the one you want to
be — not the one you think is strongest, because none of them is.

**The Runner.** You get there. You climb the thing nobody is supposed to climb,
you carry the person who cannot walk, and you have never been the slowest one in
a room. The instructors wrote *goes first, works out why afterwards* on your file
twice, and you have decided to be pleased about it. If a problem has a corridor
in it, you are the answer.

**The Tinker.** You open things. You got in because you fixed a door nobody
asked you to touch and then explained what had been wrong with it. You can tell
a dying anchor from a sulking one by the sound, which almost nobody can, and your
suit is older than you are and improved in four ways that are not strictly
allowed.

**The Talker.** You get invited in. You read people fast and from the outside
and you are usually right, and that is what got you through two years of
training your arms were never going to manage. You know the thing nobody says
out loud: nearly every disaster on the far side of a door is a person who
decided something, and a person who decided something can be talked to.

**The Fieldhand.** You know what is alive. You were raised on a farming holding
behind a single door, where the seed and the medicine and the spare parts and
your mother all came through it, and you have watched people decide not to pack
when they should have. That is why you applied, and you have never pretended
otherwise.

**Two things before you choose.**

All four of these can finish either mission. Not *could, with difficulty* — the
scenarios were built four ways on purpose, and the Talker's route through a
locked refinery is as real as the Runner's. You are not picking a difficulty
setting.

And none of them has to hurt anybody to win. There is a stunner in every kit
because the job is dangerous, not because the job is violent. Nobody in this
story dies.

Now read the three lines at the bottom of your sheet — the things your character
*cares* about. Those are the ones that will decide what you actually do.

### The Knacks <!-- id: myth-lore-dabd9cbe122f -->

What first-throughs can do, where it comes from, and what it costs. Nobody has ever had a knack without crossing, and every crossing spends a little of the life you have left.

**"What's your knack?"** is the first thing one first-through asks another, and
it is nosier than it sounds, because the answer tells you roughly how many times
they have been through a door.

## Where they come from

Three things have to be true, and the third is the one that costs.

**You have to be born able to take it.** Most people cannot. Most children,
crossed once under supervision, simply fray like an adult — a bad week, a
nosebleed, no knack, and they are never asked again. Perhaps one child in a few
hundred *builds* instead of tearing. They are the ones who get taken up.

**You have to be found.** Every system tests, because every system needs couriers
and no system can make them. The test is a short supervised crossing and back,
and the halls do it at nine or ten, and the whole family comes.

**And then you have to go through, over and over.** A knack is not switched on at
the test. It surfaces after a dozen crossings and deepens with every one after,
and it deepens for exactly as long as your body is still mending the error —
which is to say, it grows strongest in the years just before it stops growing at
all.

Nobody has ever had a knack without crossing. Nobody has ever kept one after
being landlocked; they go quiet within a year, and people describe losing them
in the same words they use about losing a sense.

## What they do

Knacks run in families of a kind, and the Threshold has names for them that the
rest of the Causeway has mostly adopted.

**The Listen.** You can tell something about the far side before you step
through — pressure, heat, whether there is air, whether there is a floor,
whether anybody is standing there. It is not a picture and it is never detailed.
It is the single most valuable thing a first-through can have, and it is the
reason any of them survive a door that has moved.

**The Hold.** You can keep a door where it is, for a few minutes, with nothing
but your attention. It is rare — rarer than anything else here — and in a galaxy
where doors move without warning it is the most valuable human ability that
exists. Holdings build machines to do what one child with the Hold does with her
hands, and the machines are not as good.

**The Quick.** Reflex and speed past what a body should manage. Common, obvious,
and the one every twelve-year-old in the Yards wants.

**The Set.** You take what should break you — falls, pressure, cold, a baton
across the back — and get up. Also common, and the reason first-throughs have a
reputation for being difficult to discourage.

**The Reckoning.** You always know precisely where you are and which way the
door was, from the instant you arrive, while everybody else is still being sick
on the deck plate. Unglamorous. Saves more lives than the Quick.

**The Knowing.** Hands and eye: you understand a mechanism by touching it. The
Threshold's engineers are made of this one.

**The Read.** You see what people are doing and about to do. Rare, resented, and
very hard to live with.

## What it costs

Every crossing deepens the knack and frays the body, and they are not two
processes. They are one process, looked at from the two ends.

So the strongest first-through in any intake is always the one closest to being
finished, and everybody can see it, and nobody says it.

You will be offered crossings you do not have to take. Some of them matter and
some of them are a shortcut. The choosing is the whole job, and it is yours.

### Your Fetch <!-- id: myth-lore-7755c4a9f7ed -->

Every first-through has one. Five species on five unrelated worlds, all of them complementary to us, and the talking is how the bond forms. An unbonded one is a book. A bonded one is your fetch -- the Threshold's forms say *second*, and nobody else ever does.

You do not get a fetch. A fetch happens to you, usually within a year of the
hall test, and after that you are two.

The Threshold's paperwork calls them *seconds*, which is accurate, dignified, and
not a word anybody has ever said out loud to a twelve-year-old holding one.

## What they are

Five species that anybody has found, on five worlds with nothing in common, and
every one of them does the thing described in [[What Crosses Easily]]: their
cells take what is written into them and keep it, perfectly, for a lifetime.

Where a first-through **carries**, a fetch **keeps**. Neither can do the other's
half. A fetch cannot cross a door without you and you cannot hold a payload
without them, and nobody has the faintest idea why the universe has arranged
itself into halves that fit.

The xenobiologists call it **the correspondence**. The standing paper is two
pages and the second page is questions.

## The talking is the bonding

This is the part people get wrong, and it matters.

A fetch does not talk because it is clever. It talks because it is **bonding**,
and language is what the bond is made of — attention, back and forth, every day,
for years. The grammar arrives with the bond. The jokes arrive a bit later.

Which is why **a book does not speak.** A book is one of these creatures that
nobody ever bonded with: kept as stock, fed, warm, written to by strangers,
handed over at the far end. It has never been anyone's. There is nothing wrong
with it and nothing was done to it.

First-throughs do not like being in the room with the stock. They are not able to
say why in a way that satisfies a reader, and readers have stopped asking.

**The bond is not a leash.** Your fetch argues, refuses, wanders off and takes
an interest in things you do not. They can be furious with you. The one thing
they cannot do is be comfortable on the far side of a door from you, and neither
can you, and that is as near to physical as anything in this gets.

## The quiet

Talking aloud is how the bond is built. It is not how an old bond runs.

Somewhere after the first few years the words stop needing to be said, and a
pair starts going **quiet** — which is the most misleading name anybody could
have chosen for it, because what it actually is is two people never shutting up
and nobody else hearing a word.

It arrives on its own, in stages, and every first-through can tell you roughly
where they are on it:

- **First year.** Feeling only. You know when they are frightened, or delighted,
  or have found something. No detail, and it wakes you up.
- **A few years in.** Direction, images, yes and no. Enough to be told *that way*
  and *not that way* and *there are three of them*.
- **Mature, five years and up.** Words. And, close to, a thin borrowing of their
  senses — what a kite can see from up there, dimly, as if remembering it rather
  than looking.

**You cannot lie in it.** You can refuse to say a thing, and everybody learns
how, and both of you always know a refusal is happening. First-throughs find
ordinary human conversation a bit slack afterwards.

### What it will not do

- **Only your own fetch.** Never another person, never somebody else's fetch,
  never two pairs chaining it. A great many people have tried.
- **A few hundred metres.** Less through rock or plate. It thins out rather than
  cutting off, so you can feel the edge of it coming and most people hate that.
- **It is tiring.** A long exchange at the far edge of the range costs you a day.
- **And it does not cross a door. Ever.**

That last one is not a technicality. Every first-through describes the crossing
the same way: your fetch goes out, and there is a moment with nothing in it, and
then they come back. Some of them talk about that moment for the rest of their
lives.

Go through without them and you arrive into a silence that does not thin out and
does not come back until you return. People do it when they must. Nobody does it
twice in a row.

## The five peoples

They are not companions. They are five sentient species who cannot cross a door
without us, and we cannot hold a payload without them, and everything else about
the arrangement has been negotiated over nine hundred years by people with their
own reasons for showing up.

Each of them has a culture, a language, a sense of humour, and a characteristic
way of being insufferable.

### Sixlimbs — *Bell, behind the TRAPPIST-1 door*

Cat-sized, six limbs, four of them hands. Bell's sun has not moved in four
billion years, so they have no day, no night, and **do not sleep at all, ever**,
and they will sit up watching you do it and raise it the next morning.

Everything in a sixlimb culture is conversation. Decisions are made by talking
until everyone is too tired to object, privacy is a concept they can define and
have never felt, and in a wind that never stops a group stays together by voice —
so a sixlimb who has gone quiet is a sixlimb in trouble.

**They want:** everywhere. The most ambitious diaspora of the five, by a long
way, and a sixlimb partnering with you has already worked out the four systems
you might plausibly get them to.

**Their folly:** they hold grudges about *arguments*. Not injuries — arguments,
eleven years old, which they can quote. And because silence reads to them as
deceit, they distrust quiet people on principle, which is wrong about nine times
in ten and catastrophic the tenth.

### Fathoms — *the cloud decks of Luhman 16*

A metre of membrane and gas, almost weightless, from a sky with no ground and no
sun in it. **Blind, and the concept does not interest them.** They feel
electrical fields and sound instead, and map a room by charge the way you map one
by eye.

Their art is entirely sound and current. Human visual culture strikes them as a
charming party trick that we have unaccountably built a civilisation on.

**They want:** other skies. A fathom goes out to feel weather that is not the
weather they were born in, and most of them are quietly looking for one specific
thing they will not describe.

**Their folly:** insufferable snobs about music, and will tell you so, in front
of the musician. And being indoors makes them stupid and miserable, which they
deny absolutely while making a run of terrible decisions.

### Cairns — *the shelters of Tau Ceti*

Low, dense, unhurried, about the mass of a full toolbag. Seven hundred thousand
years of things falling out of the sky have made them patient in a way that is
hard to be in a room with.

They build. Everything is ceremony and workmanship and the long view, and a cairn
can tell you who made a wall, roughly when, and what they were worried about.

At need they **set** — mineral-rigid in four seconds, immovable for hours.
Nothing hurts them. Nothing wakes them either.

**They want:** methods. A cairn travels to see how other people build and carries
it home, and they regard this as the only serious reason to go anywhere.

**Their folly:** appalling snobbery about workmanship, delivered to your face, at
length, in front of whoever built the thing. And they set involuntarily when
badly frightened — then spend hours as a rock, and afterwards lie about why.

### Glints — *the flare twins at Gliese 65*

Small enough to close a hand around, from a system whose two stars try to kill
everything several times a day. They shed radiation by going mirror-bright to
absolute black in an instant, and their **language is light**.

Speech is their second language and they are not especially good at it. Flashing
is their first, and it is theatrical by grammar — emphasis, exaggeration and
flourish are built into the structure rather than chosen.

**They want:** an audience. Glints go out to be seen, and are entirely open about
it, and find human embarrassment about ambition baffling.

**Their folly:** they exaggerate constantly and do not experience it as lying,
because in their own language nobody would be deceived. They will also upstage
you at the exact moment you least need it, and apologise, and do it again.

### Scatters — *the belts of Epsilon Eridani*

Not one animal. Forty-odd small units moving as one thing, hard-vacuum tolerant,
able to come apart on purpose and send a third of themselves somewhere you cannot
go.

A scatter is a **committee**. They have no singular pronoun for themselves in any
of their languages, and the idea of a single indivisible mind strikes them as a
terrifying disability that they are too polite to mention more than once a week.

**They want:** to grow. More units, more reach, and anywhere with the materials
to manage it.

**Their folly:** they vote. On everything, including whether to help you, and the
vote can go against you with no appeal. Divided they are slower and stupider and
know it, and divide anyway, and a scatter that has split too often starts
quietly forgetting things and blaming you for the gaps.

## And they cross for free

You have to be held through a door. A fetch does not — they are the complementary
half of what the door does, and they cross in the wake of the opening at no cost
to you whatever.

Which means your fetch is not luggage and is not dependent on you in the way it
first appears. **They are the only kind of person in human space who can move
freely**, they have relatives and interests in four systems, and the five peoples
between them are far better informed than any human government. See [[Carrying]].

They still cannot open a door. That is the half they need you for, and it is the
whole basis of the arrangement — but do not mistake it for helplessness, and do
not be surprised when yours turns out to have known something for a fortnight.

## Partnering

You do not choose a fetch off a list and they do not choose you for your
qualities. Mostly it is proximity and need: they are going somewhere, you can get
them there, and the two of you turn out to be able to stand each other.

The Yards at 61 Cygni are the only place all five peoples are under one roof, so
Threshold intakes come out paired in combinations that would never have happened
anywhere else, and spend their careers explaining them.

**And it is not for life.** Partnerships form, hold, drift and end, the way
anything between two people does. A fetch whose route has dried up will go with
somebody whose route has not, and say so, and be right to. You can be left. You
can be the one who leaves, and have to look at them while you say it.

The quiet goes with the partnership. It takes years to build, it is the best
thing either of you has, and when you part it fades inside a year — which
everybody knows going in and nobody really believes until it happens.

## A note on the word

**"Fetch" is ours, not theirs, and it is mildly rude.** Every one of the five
peoples has its own name for itself: the sixlimbs' translates roughly as *the
ones who are talking*, the cairns' as *those who wait*, and a glint's is a
flash-pattern that does not survive being written down. Scatters have no singular
form at all.

The Threshold's paperwork says *second*. Some fetches find *fetch* affectionate.
Some find it precisely as patronising as it sounds, and will tell you which
camp they are in about four minutes after meeting you.

### Carrying <!-- id: myth-lore-659a9ff9a80d -->

A first-through can take one person through, slowly, paying for it out of themselves. Which is why envoys cost what they cost, why assassination is the only way to reach across stars, and why every plot in human space needs a child who will say yes.

Going alone takes a few seconds.

**Carrying somebody takes minutes**, in contact, one person at a time, and you
feel every one of them. It is the hardest thing a first-through does and the only
thing they do that cannot be practised.

## What it costs

Not the passenger. **You.**

A carry runs several ordinary crossings' worth of fraying — the price of holding
a door open for two instead of one, for long enough to walk somebody through it.
Three or four in a row and you are finished: shaking, useless, and days from
being any good to anybody.

The passenger pays in proportion to their age. A child is fine. A fetch is fine.
A young adult is ill for a week and complains about it for a year. Somebody old,
or already unwell, may not come out of it at all, and everyone concerned says so
out loud beforehand.

## So adults are not barred. They are expensive.

This is the thing outsiders always get wrong.

Nothing stops an adult crossing. What stops them is that every adult who crosses
has spent a measurable piece of a specific child's life to do it, and that child
has a name and is standing right there.

**The Threshold's rule is one line: nobody carries for convenience.** Emergencies,
evacuations, and nothing else, and [[Calloway]] decides which is which. It is the
rule the service is proudest of and the one it has never bent.

[[The Second Hand]] carries for money. A berth-holder who does not want to wait,
a specialist wanted on another world, somebody who needs to not be on a manifest
— they will find you a courier, and the courier will be young and poor and will
be a fortnight older afterwards in the way that counts. Almost nowhere is it
illegal. Almost nowhere is it prosecuted. Everybody who does it knows exactly
what they are buying.

## Envoys

No signal crosses a door, so an agreement between two stars needs a body in a
room. Someone has to go, be carried, sit down, and speak.

Which means **the cost is the message.** Sending a junior official says one
thing. Sending a sixty-year-old jurist who will be ill for a month and has made
their will says something that cannot be said any other way, and both halls know
it before a word is spoken.

Receiving an envoy obliges you. Refusing to receive one is close to an act of
war, and has twice been exactly that.

## And assassins

Here is the other half, and it is why anchor halls are the way they are.

You cannot invade anybody — an army arrives one at a time into a prepared room,
and the children it would cost do not exist. But **one person can be carried**,
and one person is frequently enough.

So the whole instrument of force between stars is a single individual, delivered
by a child, and every system in human space knows it. The Toll War of 814 was fought by about
four hundred people -- see [[The Nine Hundred Years]]. Three of the Causeway's eleven recorded political
murders were committed by somebody who had been in that system for under an hour.

Which is why **the manifest is not bureaucracy.** When a tallyman wants to know
who you are carrying and will not be hurried, they are not collecting a toll.
They are the only thing standing between a hall and somebody's knife, and that is
the most sympathetic thing about [[The Tally]] and the reason nobody has ever
seriously proposed abolishing them.

## Fetches are free, and that changes everything

A human passenger has to be **held** through, and that is where the cost comes
from. A fetch does not fight the door. They are the other half of what it does —
see [[Your Fetch]] — and they cross in the wake of the opening, at no cost to
anybody at all.

A first-through can bring their fetch through a hundred times and be no more
frayed than if they had gone alone a hundred times. Nobody has ever found a
limit.

**So fetches are the only people in human space who move freely**, and the
consequences of that are larger than most humans have thought about.

### They are better connected than we are

A sixlimb at the Nine has cousins on four worlds and hears from all of them. The
five peoples have diasporas threaded through every system on the Standing, and
their news moves by the same couriers ours does except that it never has to be
rationed, because a fetch riding along costs nothing.

Human institutions are twenty-eight separate conversations that can barely hear
each other. **The peoples are one conversation**, and it has been running for six
hundred years, and almost nothing about it is written down anywhere a human has
read.

When a cairn tells you something is happening three doors away, do not ask how
they know. You will be told, at length, and it will be a list of relatives.

### They carry, at no charge

Everything in [[What Crosses Easily]] gets cheaper when you notice this. A
payload written into a fetch moves for free, forever, as often as anybody is
willing to open a door.

The entire information economy of the species rests on a courtesy: that the
person carrying it agreed to.

### Which is the dangerous part

A fetch can be approached. They are people with their own agendas, their own
money, their own families to get to, and they can be hired, persuaded, lied to or
leaned on **entirely independently of whoever they are partnered with.**

A fetch can be carrying something nobody asked them about. A fetch can be going
somewhere for a reason that is theirs. Both of those are ordinary and neither is
sinister, and that is exactly why the one time it *is* sinister nobody notices.

There is no inspection that finds a payload in a genome. There is no manifest
entry for what somebody is thinking about. The tallymen who are very good at
stopping a knife coming through a door have never once stopped anything that
mattered going through in a fetch, and they know it, and they have stopped
pretending otherwise.

**If you wanted to move something across human space without anybody seeing it,
you would not use a courier. You would use a courier's friend.**

## Which makes you the weak link in every plot

To reach anybody on another star — to negotiate with them, to save them, or to
kill them — somebody needs a first-through who will say yes.

There is no substitute, no workaround, and no machine. Just a kid, and whatever
they have been told, and whatever they decide.

You will be asked. It will not always be obvious what you are being asked for.

### Growing Up a First-Through <!-- id: myth-lore-8ecc45aa88cb -->

The hall test at nine, the Yards at twelve, your first crossing, your number -- and the fact that everybody you train with will be landlocked before they are twenty-five.

## The test

Every system tests its children, because every system needs couriers and no
system can make one.

It happens at nine or ten. It is a short supervised crossing and back, and the
whole family comes, and the halls do it on the same day every year so that the
whole street knows.

**Most children fail.** They cross, they fray like an adult, they have a bad week
and a nosebleed and no knack ever surfaces, and they are never asked again. That
is the ordinary outcome and everyone is careful to say so beforehand and nobody
quite believes it about their own.

The ones who build instead of tearing are **taken up**, and the family's life
changes that afternoon.

## The Yards

From about twelve you are at [[61 Cygni, and the Yards]], and so is everybody
else like you.

Two years of pressure work on rigs that fail when the instructors decide, vacuum
medicine on people who are extremely good at acting, and corridors at every angle
in the dark with the lights going out when you are halfway. You learn to arrive
somewhere unknown and not waste the first four seconds.

It is also the only place in human space where all five peoples are under one
roof, so this is where you are likely to meet whoever you end up partnered with,
and the pairings that come out of the Yards are odd in ways that do not happen
anywhere else. See [[Your Fetch]].

The Yards is noisy, close, competitive and extremely funny, and people who went
through it are recognisable to each other for the rest of their lives.

## Your number

Everybody knows their crossing count. It is on your papers and it is the first
thing another first-through will ask you after your name, and there is no polite
way to decline.

A high number at a young age means you are good, in demand, and spending
yourself fast. A low number at nineteen means something — sickness, a bad
posting, a system with no traffic — and people will decide what it means whether
or not you tell them.

The knack deepens with the number. So does the fraying. Nobody has ever found a
way to have one without the other, and every kid in the Yards does that
arithmetic in their second week and goes through anyway.

## What it is like

You are fifteen and you are the only thing that connects your star to the rest of
the species. That is not a feeling the adults around you have any way to argue
you out of, and most of them stopped trying a long time ago.

You will be given work that would be refused to anybody older, because there is
nobody older who can do it. You will be lied to by people who need something
carried. You will be extremely well treated by strangers in four systems and
patronised in a fifth.

And you will watch the people one intake above you start getting turned back at
the hall.

## Afterwards

The working life is twelve to about twenty-two, and then the hall doctor turns
you back one morning and that is the whole of it.

You are **landlocked**, under whatever star you were standing under, for the rest
of your life. People plan for this. People get it wrong. There is an entire
industry of advice about where to be when it happens and the advice is mostly
bad.

Within a year the knack goes quiet. If you were partnered, the quiet fades too,
and the two of you have to learn to be ordinary friends who talk out loud, and
some pairs manage it.

And then, if you are any good and you can bear it, you go and run a desk and send
children through a door you cannot follow them into.

Which is what [[Calloway]] does, and why.

### What a Crossing Does <!-- id: myth-lore-439f3a242ea1 -->

A door takes you apart and re-lays you. The re-laying is never perfect, the error accumulates, and that is fraying -- which is why only the young can cross, and why crossing is also where the knacks come from.

A door does not carry you anywhere. There is no tunnel and nothing moves.

It takes you apart, holds what you are, and lays you down again on the far side.
It is very good at this. It is not perfect at it.

Every crossing leaves a small error in you — a few things put back not quite
where they were. One is nothing. A hundred is still almost nothing. The trouble
is that it never stops being added up, and nothing ever takes it away.

We call it **fraying**, and you can see it in people long before it kills them.

## Why only the young go

A grown body cannot mend the error. It can only carry it, and then carry a bit
more, and the carrying is what eventually stops the heart or the lungs or the
part of the brain that keeps you breathing while asleep.

A child's body is still building itself, and a thing that is still being built
can be built *around* damage. Children mend it. They mend it brilliantly up to
about twelve, well into the mid-teens, badly by twenty, and after that the body
has finished and there is nothing left that rebuilds.

The numbers everybody knows, and every parent on the Causeway can recite:

- **Under about fifteen** — the crossing costs you almost nothing you will notice
- **Fifteen to twenty** — it costs you, and you can feel it, and you go anyway
- **Early twenties** — people start being refused at the hall, and start arguing
- **Thirty** — a crossing is a coin-toss with your life
- **Past that** — it is simply a way of dying in a different room

**It is not only age. It is miles.** The error is cumulative, so a courier who
crosses twice a week is frayed at nineteen and a planet-bound child is clean at
nineteen. The best first-throughs burn out soonest. Everybody knows this too.

## And nothing goes through without one

The fraying is not a side effect of travelling. It is the price of **opening**.

A door does nothing on its own, and it will not work for somebody who cannot take
it. A first-through holds the crossing open while it happens, and the holding is
what writes the error into them. That is why a landlocked adult cannot simply be
walked through by somebody else, and why the whole species moves at the speed of
its children.

## Landlocked

The day you can no longer cross, you are **landlocked**, and you are landlocked
*where you are standing*.

That is the shape of every adult life in human space. You travel as a child, you
spend what you have, and then one morning the hall doctor turns you back and the
star you happen to be under is the star you will die under. People choose where
to be when it happens. Some get it wrong.

Which is why the Causeway is governed by people who cannot leave their own
system, and carried — the trade, the news, the treaties, the rescues, the wars —
entirely on the backs of children.

When an adult tells a first-through *nobody is coming*, it is not cowardice and
it is not a lesson. They physically cannot follow, and most of them remember
exactly what it was like when they could.

## And the other half of it

The error does not only break things.

A child's body builds around it, and sometimes what it builds is **new** — a
sense that was not there, a reflex faster than a reflex should be, a body that
takes a fall it should not have survived. Those are the **knacks**, and nobody
has ever got one without crossing.

So the thing that is going to end you is the same thing that is making you
extraordinary, and it is the same thing, and there is no version where you get
one and not the other.

Every kid in the Yards has done this arithmetic by their second week. They go
through anyway. They would go through more if they were let.

### What Crosses Easily <!-- id: myth-lore-a47dd64a96ab -->

Only living things cross. So there is no freight at all, you arrive with nothing, and everything humanity knows is written into the cells of small animals and carried by children. A book is alive.

A door does not move objects. It takes a living thing apart and lays it down
again, and that is the only trick it has ever had.

**Nothing dead crosses.** Not a crate, not a tool, not a coupling assembly, not a
coin, not the clothes you walked in wearing. People established this in the first
month of the first door and have been re-establishing it ever since, because
every generation produces somebody convinced they have found the exception.

## You arrive with nothing

Every anchor hall in human space has a dressing room on both sides, and the
oldest ones have had the same function for nine hundred years.

You undress. You cross. You are issued whatever that system puts on its
arrivals — and it is always local, always slightly wrong, and always says
exactly where you have been. A first-through can read a stranger's last three
postings off the cut of their boots.

Nothing you own goes with you. Your suit is the Nine's suit and stays at the
Nine. The tools are Barnard's tools and stay at Barnard's. In nine centuries
nobody has ever carried a keepsake between stars, and the courier songs are
mostly about this.

**What you take is your knack and your own cells.** That is the whole inventory.

## Which means there is no trade

Not expensive trade. **None.** A world eats, breathes, wears, heats and repairs
itself out of what it has, or it does without, and there is no version where
help arrives in a crate.

Coldwater cannot be sent a coupling assembly. Not refused one — *sent* one. The
word on the requisition board is a courtesy.

## So a book is alive

And yet everything humanity knows is in every system, and a child crossing
carries more of it than a library.

Because information does not have to be dead. Write it into a genome and it is
in a living thing, and a living thing crosses.

They are called **books**, and a book is an animal that nobody ever bonded with.
A bonded one is a fetch, and belongs to somebody, and the difference between the
two is the whole of it -- see [[Your Fetch]]. A book is: palm-sized, placid, slow,
bred for nothing but this. They come from the sixlimbs of [[Bell]], on the far side
of the TRAPPIST-1 door, whose cells hold a written payload perfectly for a
lifetime and were doing it long before anybody arrived to notice. You feed it. You keep it warm. You
hand it over at the far end and somebody who knows how reads it out.

A courier with three books under their coat is carrying three libraries, and the
books do not know and would not care.

**And a book cannot cross alone either.** It is alive, so it can go -- but
something has to open the way, which means every library in human space moves in
the arms of a child, and has for nine hundred years.

Which is stranger than it sounds, because it is the same strangeness twice. A
first-through's body takes what a crossing does to it and builds around the
damage. A sixlimb's body takes what is written into it and keeps it. One carries,
one keeps, neither can do the other's half, and the two species evolved forty
light years apart with no ancestor in common.

And it is not one coincidence. There are **five** such species that anybody has
found, on five worlds with nothing in common, and every one of them fits us.

Nobody designed this. The xenobiologists call it **the correspondence** and have
had no theory for two hundred years. Most people never think about it. The ones
who do tend to stop sleeping properly.

There is a whole profession of writing them and another of reading them, and the
two trades do not like each other. Books can sicken. Books can be forged. A book
that has been got at carries something that looks exactly like knowledge, and
there have been wars about it.

## Everyone knows everything. Nobody has anything.

Which is the shape of the Causeway, stated plainly.

Every system has the same science, the same medicine, the same designs, the same
histories, mostly the same songs — because all of that fits in an animal and the
animal can walk. A child in the cloud decks of the sunless cloud decks of Luhman 16 and a
child in the Coldwater grain are taught from the same text, and are.

And they live in entirely different worlds, because **knowing how to make a
coupling assembly is not the same as having one.** You need the ore, the plant,
the power and the hands. Barnard's has four. Coldwater has a council of five and
some very good soil.

Nobody is hoarding anything. There is no secret. There is only the gap between a
drawing and an object, and a door that will not pass the difference.

## Nothing crosses alone

A door is not a door until a first-through is standing in it.

The ring does nothing by itself. Walk at an unattended anchor and you walk into
cold housing and bruise your face. What makes a crossing happen is a living mind
that can take the crossing and hold it open while it is happening, and that is
the whole of what a first-through is **for**.

So every transit in human space has a child in it. Every book, every message,
every passenger, every treaty, every piece of news for nine hundred years has
gone through in the company of somebody between twelve and twenty who opened the
way and paid for it out of themselves.

### Passengers

A first-through can take somebody through with them -- one person, in contact,
and it takes minutes rather than seconds.

The cost falls on the carrier, not the carried, and it is heavy. It is also the
reason envoys are rare, assassination is the only way anybody reaches across
stars, and every conspiracy in human space needs a child who will agree to it.
See [[Carrying]].

### What that actually means

The limit on a door is not the door. It is the children.

A system's entire traffic with the rest of the species -- its news, its science,
its diplomacy, its rescues, its wars -- is capped by how many crossings its
first-throughs can afford to spend. And every one of those crossings comes out of
a named person's remaining years.

Follow the Tally's tolls all the way down and they are a tax on how long a
particular child is going to live.

Nobody puts it like that. Everybody has worked it out.

## Where the money actually is

A book copies. Once it lands, that system has it forever and so does everyone
they tell. So nobody sells knowledge — **they sell being first.**

Value lives entirely in the gap between a thing being true somewhere and being
known here, and that gap is exactly as long as a child takes to walk it.

Which is why [[The Tally]] is the real power and the tolls are the least of it.
They stand at every door. They do not need to own the news; they only need to see
it half a day before anyone else, and they always do.

## And what that makes a courier

The entire nervous system of the species is children, walking, one at a time,
carrying animals full of everything we know.

Adults have been trying to think of a way round it for nine hundred years. There
is not one. There is only the next kid through the door, with nothing on, holding
a book.

### The First Reach <!-- id: myth-lore-ba9f231a772d -->

The expansion that built every door we have, and spent itself doing it. The principle was never lost -- the surplus was, and the network itself makes it impossible to raise again.

Start with the part everybody gets wrong.

**The knowledge was never lost.** Any decent engineer at the Yards can tell you
what an anchor pair is and how the pairing is made. It is taught. There are
diagrams. Nobody has forgotten anything.

What we lost was the money.

## What a door costs

A matched pair costs, very roughly, what a civilisation produces in ten years.
Not a nation. A *civilisation* — everything everyone makes, everywhere, for a
decade, poured into one object that does nothing at all until its other half has
been carried to another star by a ship that will fly for three hundred years.

The First Reach did that about three thousand times.

## The deciding took longer than the building

Two generations of argument, and the record of it survives better than almost
anything else from that age, because it was argued in the open and written down
obsessively. People stood up in halls and justified, to their own grandchildren,
a bill those grandchildren would pay in full and never see a return on. The
return was for somebody four generations down who did not exist yet and could
not be consulted.

They lost that argument for about forty years. Then they won it.

Read the surviving speeches and what strikes you is how unsentimental they are.
Nobody talks about destiny. They talk about the arithmetic of a species with one
star and what happens to it eventually, and they conclude — reluctantly, the way
you conclude you need a new roof — that it has to be done now because it takes
nine hundred years to work.

## Then they did it

The yards went up on Luna. Mercury's mirrors went up to power them. For sixty
years the Reach threw ships at the sky at a twentieth of lightspeed, each one
carrying half of a door, and the other halves were distributed by slow freighter
among whichever stars had already come online.

Then the launches thinned out, and then they stopped.

There was no war. There is no plague in the records, no collapse in the
accounts, no final message. They simply had nothing left, and the thing they had
built would not start paying out for another eighty years.

## Eighty-five years of nothing

The first door opened at [[Alpha Centauri]] in the eighty-fifth year of the
Reach, to a generation that had been told its whole life this would happen and
had begun, quietly and privately, to stop believing it.

Barnard's came in at a hundred and nineteen. [[Sirius, and the Nine]] at a
hundred and seventy-four. Tau Ceti at two hundred and thirty-eight. Each one a
lifetime apart, each one arriving to people who had only ever heard about the
last one from their grandparents.

It is the longest-running promise anybody has ever kept, and every single person
who made it was nine centuries dead before it finished being kept.

## Why nobody will ever build another

Here is the part that matters, and it is not a tragedy of lost knowledge. It is
a trap in the shape of the network itself.

**Only bodies and boxes cross a door.** Nothing else — no bulk, no fleet, no
treasury. So twenty-eight systems can never pool what twenty-eight systems have.
Each one is as rich as it is and no richer, and the surplus required to make one
anchor pair is larger than any single system has produced in nine hundred years.

The thing that would pay for a new door cannot be gathered through the doors we
already have.

The network forbids its own extension. That is not a policy anybody chose. It is
the shape of the thing.

## So

We did not build this. We inherited it, we maintain the parts of it we can
reach, and it is nine hundred and seventy-five years old.

Nobody says this out loud at a Threshold briefing, but every first-through works
it out in their first year: we are not pioneers. We are the people living in the
house, and the house was paid for by somebody who went without.

### The Founding of the Threshold <!-- id: myth-lore-b6e40e30b51d -->

A service exists to step through doors first because of one afternoon four hundred years ago at a door nobody had checked.

For five hundred years, a new door was simply used.

A ship arrived, a door opened, and whoever was nearest walked through with a
lamp and a hopeful expression. Most of the time this was fine, because most of
the time the far side is a rock with nothing on it. The rest of the time it was
not fine, and the Causeway absorbed those losses the way every society absorbs
losses it has decided are normal.

Then, four hundred and twelve years ago, a door opened onto a chamber full of a
gas that was not poisonous and was not corrosive, and was simply under
**eleven times** the pressure of the room on this side.

It did not kill the man who stepped through. It killed the fourteen people
standing behind him in the anchor hall, because a door is a hole and a hole does
not care which way things go.

The inquiry that followed produced one recommendation and the recommendation was
not technical. It was that **somebody should go first, on purpose, trained, with
the hall sealed behind them** — and that this somebody should be a person whose
entire job it is, rather than whoever happened to be standing there.

The Threshold was chartered eleven months later, and for its first forty years
it was widely regarded as an insult: a service built on the idea that ordinary
people could not be trusted to open their own doors.

The ready room at the Nine still has the fourteen names on the wall beside the
window. Nobody at the Threshold calls it a memorial. They call it the list, and
new intakes are walked past it on their first morning without being told what it
is, because being told later works better.

### The Nine Hundred Years <!-- id: myth-lore-3979ec5f87f3 -->

What has actually happened since the Launch: the Sorting, the First Straying, the loss of Kapteyn's, the Carrying Sickness, the Toll War -- and the door due next year.

Everything is dated from the Launch, because a calendar is the one thing a graph
can agree on.

### Year 0 — the Launch

Three thousand ships, sixty years of them, and the end of the civilisation that
built them. See [[The First Reach]].

### Year 85 — the First Door

[[Alpha Centauri]], to a generation that had stopped believing it. The most
copied document in human history is the maintenance log of the woman who felt
wind on her arm in a room with no wind in it.

### Years 88 to 121 — the Sorting

The worst thing in this history, and the foundation of everything after it.

They sent their best through first. Pilots, engineers, soldiers — adults, chosen
carefully, in the proper order, and over thirty-three years almost all of them
died, slowly, of something nobody could name. The records of the Sorting are
exhaustive because the people keeping them were watching their colleagues go and
could not work out why.

It was a fourteen-year-old who crossed by accident in year 118 — a courier's
daughter, in a hall she should not have been in — and came back the same evening
perfectly well, and went again, and again.

Three years of argument followed, and the argument is a terrible thing to read.
Then the halls started taking children, and have not stopped.

### Years 85 to 331 — the Opening

Doors coming online one at a time, each a generation or two apart, each arriving
to people who had only heard about the last one from their grandparents.
[[Sirius, and the Nine]] at 174. Tau Ceti at 238. The last of the inner doors in
year 331.

Then it stopped, and the Causeway was what it is.

### Year 311 — the First Straying

A door at the Nine opened in the morning onto somewhere nobody recognised.

Forty-one people were on the wrong side of it. Nine of them came home, years
later, by routes that took three more doors and most of their twenties. The
others did not.

Before the Straying, people believed doors were permanent and fixed and would
outlast the species. After it, the whole Causeway understood that it was living
on something that moved, and the business of finding out where doors now went
became the single most important job anybody had.

That is the year the Threshold's work really began, four hundred years before the
service was chartered.

### Year ~400 — the Tally

Somebody had to write down where the doors currently went, or everybody would be
guessing. The clearing house that did it charged a little for the service, and
then stood at every door in human space, and then was a government.

Nobody voted for this and nobody can now undo it, because undoing it would
require twenty-eight systems to agree on something.

### Year 602 — Kapteyn's

Eleven thousand people, a good world, a well-run holding of farms and foundries
twelve and a half light years out.

Its door strayed in the spring of 602 and went dark, and has stayed dark.

For four hundred years that was read as loss. It is now read as something worse
and better: by [[Straying]], a mouth that lands inside rock does not open at all.
It is **closed, not destroyed**, and the far side is exactly where it always was.

Which means Kapteyn's is not missing. **It is buried.** Eleven thousand people
became some number of people, under a hill, on a world that is still there, and
the door between us is a dark ring in a hall with a wall of stone on the other
side of it.

Three hundred and eighty expeditions have gone looking for where it went. If the
current reading is right, every one of them was looking for the wrong thing.

And a buried mouth can stray again.

Kapteyn's Star is still there. On a clear watch at the Nine you can be shown
which point it is, and people do show you, and the pointing is a thing visitors
find unbearable long before the residents do.

Everybody alive has a version of the arithmetic: twenty-four generations, so
whoever is there now is nobody's cousin exactly, and is still ours.

The Threshold keeps a watch on that housing. It has been kept for six hundred
years, it has never once reported anything, and the rota has never been cut.

**The map has twenty-eight systems on it. It used to have twenty-nine.**

### Year 741 — the Carrying Sickness

An illness that moved at exactly the speed of a child walking.

It crossed eleven systems in four years because that is how fast couriers go, and
it could not be outrun, warned against or quarantined ahead of itself — the
warning travelled by the same couriers, in the same bodies, at the same pace.

Two systems closed their halls entirely and refused all arrivals for eleven
years. One of them is still unpopular about it. The other is Tau Ceti, which
apologised, built a quarantine wing into the side of its anchor hall, and has
kept it staffed for two hundred and thirty years against the next time.

### Years 814 to 822 — the Toll War

The only general war the Causeway has had, and it was fought entirely by about
four hundred people.

Nobody could invade anybody — an army arrives one at a time into a prepared room
— so it was fought the only way war can be fought here: infiltration, anchor
halls seized from the inside, and three systems blockaded into agreement by
simply refusing their couriers passage.

Barnard's held its own hall for nine days against people already inside it. The
Nine changed hands twice without a shot, both times at a committee meeting.

It ended in a settlement nobody likes, which is the Tally's charter as it now
stands.

### Year 950 — GJ 1214

The last door to open, twenty-five years ago, to enormous public excitement and a
hot crushing atmosphere with nothing to stand on. Every honest briefing about
what is coming next begins with it.

### Year 976 — next year

[[LHS 1140]].

### How an Anchor Works <!-- id: myth-lore-16eb77a71842 -->

A pair of entangled mouths, made together and married for life. Matter crosses instantly. No signal ever can -- which is not an engineering problem but a theorem, and it is why the whole species runs on couriers.

An anchor is half of a pair, and the pair is **entangled** — made together, in
the same place, at the same moment, and bound at a level nothing since has been
able to reach into.

That single fact decides almost everything about the world you live in.

## They are married for life

A mouth is bound to its own mate and to nothing else, ever. You cannot re-point
one. You cannot introduce an anchor to a different anchor and talk them into it.
You cannot cut a pair in half and make two new routes out of the pieces; people
have tried, with everything from arc furnaces to centuries of patience, and all
they have ever produced is scrap and two dead mouths.

So a door is not a technology anybody owns. It is a **marriage**, and there are
only as many as the Reach made.

## Matter crosses. Nothing else does, and never will.

Walk at the housing and you arrive. No tunnel, no transit time, no distance in
any sense you could measure.

Now put a radio against it and shout.

**Nothing happens, and nothing ever can.** Entanglement by itself carries no
information — that is not a limit of our instruments, it is a theorem, proved
before the Reach launched and never dented since. The correlation is perfect and
it cannot be used to *say* anything. Light still crawls, every signal still obeys
it, and the door is no help at all.

Which is the whole shape of human civilisation. There is no telemetry, no
warning, no calling through, no listening in. If you want something known on the
other side of a door, a person carries it, in their arms, one at a time. See
[[What Crosses Easily]].

It also means **no door has ever been eavesdropped on.** There is nothing to tap.
A thing said at the Nine is known at the Nine, and arrives anywhere else in the
company of whoever decided to walk it there.

## What the crossing does to you

The mouth does not move you. It takes what you are, holds it, and lays it down
again at the mate — and the laying down is very good and not perfect. The error
is small, it never comes out again, and it is why only the young can cross and
where the knacks come from. See [[What a Crossing Does]].

And it does not do any of this by itself. A door is not a door until a
first-through is standing in it, holding the crossing open while it happens.

## What a first-through can actually repair

Housings foul. Collars creep. Load beds drift and start fighting themselves.
Those are maintenance and a competent fifteen-year-old with a toolroll handles
all three.

What nobody can do is build one, pair one, re-point one, or persuade a mouth to
be anywhere other than where the physics has put it — which brings us to
[[Straying]].

### Straying <!-- id: myth-lore-df675e04ba0d -->

A mouth is pinned to a gravitational potential, not to a place. As the masses around it move, the matching place moves -- and now and then it jumps. That is a stray, and it is why the Standing is chalk.

Doors do not break. People spent six hundred years expecting them to and they
never have.

What they do is **move**, and the reason is the strangest clean thing in the
sciences.

## A mouth is pinned to a number, not to a place

When a pair is made, each mouth takes the **gravitational potential** of where it
was made — how deep it sits in the sum of every well around it — and that number
is what the entanglement actually holds onto. Not the rock. Not the coordinates.
The depth.

So a mouth does not sit somewhere. It sits *at a value*, and it is wherever that
value currently is.

## And the matching place moves

Worlds go round stars. Moons go round worlds. Mass shifts, slowly and forever,
and the surface where the potential matches drifts along with it.

Mostly this is nothing: a mouth creeps a few metres in a century and the housing
is rebuilt around it twice a millennium and nobody writes anything down.

Now and again the sum has more than one answer, and the mouth **jumps to a
different one.**

That is a stray. It takes about four seconds.

## Where they go

Always to a place with the same potential, which bounds the problem enormously.
The route itself never changes — an anchor paired to Gliese 887 is paired to
Gliese 887 for ever, and no stray will ever send you to a different star.

What changes is **where in that system you come out.**

- **Usually the same world**, at the same depth in its well — which on a planet
  means roughly the same altitude. A door can cross a continent in four seconds
  and stay at sea level.
- **Sometimes another body in the same system**, if it happens to have a point
  that matches. Moons do this. So do stations, which is why a station's berth is
  computed to an absurd precision and argued about for decades.
- **Never another star.** The wells are different problems with different
  answers.

## And the weeks before

A mouth does not become ambiguous all at once. While the sum is acquiring a
second answer and has not yet resolved, the mouth cannot settle -- and a mouth
that cannot settle will not open on demand.

The crossing is still instantaneous. What takes time is the **permission**, and
the time is measurable, and it rises. See [[The Hesitation]].

This is the only advance warning of a stray anybody has ever had. It was in the
folklore of every deck in the Causeway for nine centuries -- *seven's sticky this
week*, *you want to give it a second* -- and nobody wrote it down, because the
people who felt it were children and what they felt was an inconvenience.

## And where they must not go

Here is the one mercy in it, and it is not a kindness, it is just the physics.

**If the matching place is inside rock, the door will not open.** A throat cannot
establish into solid matter, so the mouth goes dark and stays dark — closed, not
destroyed, with everything on the far side exactly where it was.

Nobody is crushed. Everybody is cut off.

It is called a **grash**, which is onomatopoeia of a sort -- it is the word the
first crew to lose one used, and it stuck, and it is the oldest piece of courier
vocabulary still in daily use.

It is also, for the same reason, the strongest swear word in human space. People
say it the way other people say the name of a disaster, and most of them have
stopped hearing what it means. Anybody with family behind a closed door has not,
and will tell you so.

**But vacuum is not solid, and neither is water.** A mouth that strays to open sky
on an airless moon opens perfectly happily, and so does one that strays to the
floor of an ocean, and both will kill the next person through without any
difficulty at all.

That is the whole reason [[The Knacks]] matters, and why **the Listen** is worth
more than any other thing a first-through can be. Something of the far side
reaches you before you commit: pressure, or the absence of it. It is never a
picture. It is the difference between a career and a paragraph.

## The stutter

A mouth about to jump gets unreliable first — a transit that takes a moment you
can feel, then a moment you could count, then a refusal. Days of it, sometimes
weeks, and between the bad ones it works perfectly, which is why settlements
behind a stuttering door argue about leaving instead of leaving.

Then it goes, and wherever it is now is where it is.

## Could you see one coming?

In principle, yes. The potential is a number and the masses are known, and a
sufficiently good model of a system plus a long enough record of its doors would
tell you when a solution is about to branch.

Nobody has ever built one. The models are hard, the records are scattered across
twenty-eight systems that can only exchange them at the speed of a walking child,
and the one place that has kept all of it in a single room for nine hundred years
is a four-room station at [[van Maanen's Star]] staffed by two people nobody
visits.

One of whom has been writing to the directorate about it for three years.

### The Arithmetic of the Reach <!-- id: myth-lore-d505b6391a05 -->

One light year per twenty years: the sum that explains why the frontier is where it is, and why it moves so slowly.

The seedships went out at a twentieth of the speed of light. That is a stupid,
grinding, patient speed, and it hands you the only sum anybody on the Causeway
needs:

**One light year takes twenty years.**

Sirius is eight and a half light years from Sol, so the ship carrying its anchor
flew for about a hundred and seventy years. The launch was **975** years ago.
Subtract, and you have the age of the door people walk through every morning at
the Nine: a little over eight hundred years old.

Do the sum the other way and you get the edge of everything. Nine hundred and
seventy-five years of flying, at a light year every twenty, is **48.75 light
years**. That is the frontier — not because anyone decided it, but because the
ships are still out there and that is as far as they have got.

Which gives you the second thing worth knowing. The frontier moves at **one
light year every twenty years**, and nothing anybody does will hurry it. A
first-through who serves a full forty years will watch it advance by two light
years. You can hold the entire map in your head and still never see the end of
it.

**And it tells you what is about to happen.** There is a star called LHS 1140,
a red dwarf a little under forty-nine light years out, with a rocky world in its
habitable zone — the best one anybody has ever found. Its ship has been flying
for nine hundred and seventy-six years.

Do the subtraction yourself. It is not due next century. It is due next year,
and everyone on the Causeway has had nine centuries to decide what they intend
to do about it.

### Why Nobody Can Reach You <!-- id: myth-lore-e083adf7a80b -->

Radio obeys the speed of light, so the far side of a door is out of contact by definition. The answer to can I call for help is no, and it is not a punishment.

A door moves matter instantly. It does not move a signal any faster than a
signal goes, and a signal goes at the speed of light.

So: you are standing at the Nine. Your handler is three metres away. You step
through to a station ten light years off. You are now, in every sense that
matters, **ten years away from the sound of their voice**. Shout into the radio
and they will hear you in a decade. There is no relay, no repeater, no clever
trick — the trick was the door, and the door only takes bodies and boxes.

Could you not simply walk back and ask? Yes. That is exactly what people do,
and it is why couriers exist and why a first-through's debrief is a formal
occasion rather than a chat. **A message is a person.** It has legs, it gets
tired, and it can decide not to come.

**What this means in practice, and you should get used to it now.**

There is no calling for help. Not because it is forbidden, and not because the
Threshold is hard on its people. There is simply nobody listening on the other
side of the door, and there never has been. Your handler briefs you standing in
the same room, because that is the last moment they can.

Whoever stepped through with you is the whole of your support. Whatever is in
your kit is the whole of your equipment. Whatever you decide is the decision
that gets made.

People find this frightening for about a week and then find it is the best thing
about the job. Nobody is going to second-guess you from a desk. They cannot.

### 61 Cygni, and the Yards <!-- id: myth-lore-097b6b387d5a -->

The first star whose distance humans ever measured, 11.4 light years out: two orange dwarfs and the training yards where every first-through is made.

**11.4 light years. The door is seven hundred and forty-seven years old.**

Two orange dwarf stars, cooler and smaller than the Sun, going round each other
slowly. There is nothing remarkable about them except one thing, and the place
mentions it at every opportunity:

**This is the first star whose distance anybody ever measured.** Before 61
Cygni, people knew the stars were far away and could not say how far. Somebody
with a telescope and two years of patience measured this one's tiny annual
wobble and came back with a number. Everything since — every distance in this
worldbook, the whole arithmetic of the Reach — descends from that measurement.

Which is why the Threshold trains here, and never shuts up about it.

**What people built here: the Yards.**

A sprawl of stations, hulks and deliberately broken machinery around the smaller
star, where first-throughs are made. You spend two years in the Yards. You learn
pressure work on a rig that fails on purpose, vacuum medicine on people who are
acting, and the one lesson the whole place is built around: **how to be the only
person who is coming.**

The final exercise is a cold step. They put you through a door onto a rig you
have not been briefed on, with no way to call back, and they watch what you do
first. Everybody remembers what they did first. Most are not proud of it.

### A First-Through's Guide <!-- id: myth-lore-8f522365c640 -->

Fourteen systems, what they look like, who runs them and what they will want from you. Nine hundred years with no outside authority possible, and every one of them has gone strange in its own direction.

No system can be governed from anywhere else. A message takes as long as a child
takes to carry it, and no fleet can be landed, so for nine hundred years every
star has run itself however it ended up running itself.

They have not converged. Here is what you walk into.

## Sirius — the Nine

**8.6 ly. Nine doors. Two stars and no worlds at all, so every stone of it was
carried here.**

A cylinder two kilometres long turning in a blue-white glare nothing quite
filters, with the anchor hall at its waist and the hottest white dwarf in the
neighbourhood for a second sun.

**Run by:** nobody, properly. Nine door-holders sit as a harbour council and have
been arguing since before anyone's grandparents. Nothing is decided and
everything works, which outsiders find intolerable and residents consider the
point.

**They want:** your manifest, your toll, and the news. The Nine runs on knowing
things first.

Every human culture that exists is in one room here at any given hour, and the
room smells of eight different stars' worth of air.

## Sol — the Garden

**0 ly. Seven doors, in one hall on Luna, with a window.**

**Run by:** nations. Actual nations, the old ones, still arguing after six
thousand years, plus a Luna port authority nobody elected that everybody obeys.

**They want:** to be taken seriously, and they are not.

The only world with an outdoors. Weather happens by itself. The food comes up out
of the ground without a lamp over it, and none of it is worth anything to anyone
more than a toll away.

## Alpha Centauri

**4.4 ly. The first door, 887 years old. Habitats around the orange sun.**

Nine centuries of building outward, and the oldest sections are thick and
over-engineered with redundancies nobody specifies any more, put up by people who
did not know whether help existed.

**Run by:** houses. Hereditary, courteous, and a court of precedence so refined
that cases are inherited. They have the oldest archives in human space and will
tell you so.

**They want:** acknowledgement that they were first.

## Barnard's Star — the Works

**6.0 ly. Four scorched pebbles, no habitable zone, and the best industry in the
Causeway.**

Being useless to live on makes a system perfect for everything nobody wants
nearby. Smelting, hull plate, reaction mass, batons, the dirty end of anchor
parts. Three shifts, forever. The habitat air tastes of it and residents cannot
smell it.

**Run by:** the Works — a syndicate of foundries where your vote is your shift.
Brutally practical, genuinely fair, no patience whatever.

**They want:** parts orders, and for you to stand out of the way.

## 61 Cygni — the Yards

**11.4 ly. The first star whose distance anyone ever measured, and they mention
it constantly.**

A sprawl of stations, hulks and machinery broken on purpose, strung around the
smaller sun. Everybody here is either under twenty or an instructor.

**Run by:** the Threshold, which makes it the only system in human space governed
by a service that holds no doors anywhere.

**They want:** children who can take the crossing. It is the one commodity
nothing else can supply.

## Gliese 887 — the Coldwater Holding

**10.7 ly. A quiet red dwarf and a world you can stand on under an open sky.**

Four thousand people. One street with a roof over the middle. Grain under long
low covers in permanent red afternoon, and meltwater running in a cut stone
channel down one side of the road.

**Run by:** five elected councillors and a board on the street wall where every
order placed and not delivered is chalked up in public.

**They want:** their coupling assembly, which has been refused twice.

## Gliese 65 — the Flare Twins

**8.9 ly. Two red dwarfs that brighten by a huge factor in under a minute,
several times a day.**

A refinery stack with its living deck buried in the middle where the mass is
thickest. The lighting browns out when the field gets busy and the crew read it
like a clock.

**Run by:** a company, and the company is three doors away and has never been
here.

**They want:** relief, and nobody is sending any.

## Tau Ceti — the Shelters

**11.9 ly. A sun very like our own, wrapped in a debris disk ten times ours.**

Something comes down somewhere on Tau Ceti every few weeks. Seven hundred years
of that has produced the most beautiful architecture in human space, all of it
underground: vaulted, layered, lit from shafts, built by people for whom a roof
is a theological matter.

**Run by:** the civil defence corps, which stopped being an emergency service
about four centuries ago and never handed anything back.

**They want:** nothing from you, politely, and a bunk is free to anyone caught
outside.

## YZ Ceti — the Singing

**12.1 ly, and one and a half light years from Tau Ceti — the shortest door in
the Causeway.**

The star and its innermost planet throw radio at each other, and the aurorae
that come off it can be heard on any receiver on the surface. Everyone here grew
up with the sky making noise. Their music has it in it. Outsiders find the
silence afterwards unbearable.

**Run by:** lot. Names drawn annually, service compulsory, refusal unthinkable.

**They want:** to be left alone with Tau Ceti, with whom they are effectively one
two-city state joined by a ninety-second walk.

## Epsilon Eridani — the Belts

**10.5 ly. Young, loud, and full of rocks: two asteroid belts and a Kuiper
analogue around a Jupiter-mass planet.**

Claims, prospectors, and more wealth loose in it than anywhere else inside twenty
light years. Also more funerals.

**Run by:** claim law and arbitration, enforced by whoever is nearest, badly.

**They want:** your silence about what you saw.

## Luhman 16 — the Lantern

**6.5 ly. Two brown dwarfs and no star at all.**

Permanent night, and in that night the largest weather any human being has ever
seen: banded storms the width of worlds, lit from inside by lightning. People
live in the cloud decks in aerostats, moving with the bands.

**Run by:** captains, under a maritime code older than most planetary
governments, because everything here floats or falls.

**They want:** nothing you have. They come up for parts twice a year.

## TRAPPIST-1 — the Seven

**40.7 ly. The frontier jewel. Seven tidally locked worlds, three of them
temperate, and every one of them with a ring of permanent dusk where the wind
never stops.**

Terminator settlements strung along the twilight, under-supplied, a long way from
the anchor, farming and digging in an eternal evening.

**Run by:** seven different arrangements that agree on nothing, plus whichever
two the Tally has brought under toll.

**They want:** anything. They are the poorest-supplied people in human space and
the proudest about it.

## Gliese 876 — the Clockwork

**15.2 ly. Four planets locked in a resonance so exact it has kept time for
billions of years.**

**Run by:** an order of timekeepers that began as astronomers and did not stay
that way. They hold the Causeway's standard clock, which every system sets by,
which is a quieter kind of power than tolls.

**They want:** your observations, written down, properly.

## van Maanen's Star — the Office

**14.1 ly. The nearest lone white dwarf, visibly swallowing the wreckage of its
own planets, throwing hard little shadows on everything.**

Four rooms bolted to a rock. Two staff. No colony, no industry, no traffic.

**Run by:** Sep Anwar and Meriel Koll, in the sense that there is nobody else.

**They want:** a visitor. It has been a while.

### Alpha Centauri <!-- id: myth-lore-8ebc99246045 -->

The first door ever opened, 887 years ago, and the oldest inhabited place off Earth. Three stars, and the famous one has never been walked on.

**4.4 light years. The door is eight hundred and eighty-seven years old, and it
was the first.**

Three stars. Two of them — **Alpha Centauri A** and **B** — go round each other
close enough to be one place in practice: a yellow sun very like Sol and a
smaller orange one, and a candidate gas giant out at A that has been argued over
for a thousand years and never resolved.

The third is **Proxima**, a small red dwarf with a filthy temper, and Proxima has
the planet everybody has heard of.

## The world nobody has stood on

**Proxima b** sits in the habitable zone of its star. It has been the most famous
planet in human history since before the Reach launched, and it was one of the
reasons the Reach launched at all.

Nobody has ever been there.

Proxima is a fifth of a light year from A and B — thirteen thousand times the
distance from Earth to its sun. That is nothing at all on a chart and it is a
**ninety-year journey** under torch, one way, and there is no anchor at the far
end of it because the Reach could not afford a second pair for a system it had
already paid for once.

So: four light years from Earth, one step through a door, and then a lifetime of
flying you would not survive. People at the Centauri habitats can point out
Proxima through a window. Three ships have gone. None has reported, and the
earliest of them is not due to arrive for another forty years.

It is the closest unreachable place in the Causeway, and every first-through is
made to understand it in their first month, because it is the cleanest possible
lesson about what a door is and what it is not.

## What people built here

Everything is at the anchor, which is in orbit around **B**: a cluster of
habitats grown outward for nearly nine centuries, the oldest continuously
inhabited structures off Earth, and it shows in the way the oldest ones are
built — thick, over-engineered, with redundancies nobody specifies any more,
by people who had no idea whether anybody would ever come to help them.

For thirty-one years this was the only other place. The entire human species was
two points and a line between them, and the Centauri habitats remember it in the
way that institutions do: they have the oldest archives, the oldest courts, the
oldest families, and an opinion about precedence that the rest of the Causeway
finds exhausting.

The Tally's founding charter was written here. People here mention that.

## The arrival

The door opened in the eighty-fifth year of [[The First Reach]] to a generation
that had been told all its life it would and had mostly stopped expecting it.

The record of that day is the most-copied document in human history. What it is,
mostly, is a maintenance log: a woman named in no other source writing down that
the housing had gone warm, then that the field had struck, then — in a different
hand, because by then she had sat down on the floor — that she had put her arm
through to the elbow and felt wind on it.

There was no wind in that room.

### Barnard's Star <!-- id: myth-lore-ec404a2af3a0 -->

The nearest single star, 6 light years out, with four scorched sub-Earths and no habitable zone at all. The Causeway's most famous disappointment, and now its foundry.

**6.0 light years. The door is eight hundred and fifty-six years old.**

Barnard's Star is the nearest star to Sol that is not part of the Alpha Centauri
tangle, and for a thousand years it was the great hope. It is also the fastest
mover in the sky — it has the **largest proper motion of any known star**, so
over a human lifetime you can watch it visibly change position against the
background. It is going somewhere, at speed, and taking its planets with it.

It has four. They are real, they were hard to find, and each is between about a
fifth and a third of the mass of Earth. They orbit in **two to seven days**. All
four are far too close and far too hot, and the same measurements that found
them have largely ruled out anything in the habitable zone at all.

Four scorched pebbles around the nearest single star. People took that badly and
the Causeway has never quite forgiven it.

**What people built here: the foundry.**

Being useless to live on makes a system excellent for the things nobody wants
nearby. Barnard's is heavy industry: smelting, hull plate, reaction mass, and
the dirty end of anchor-part manufacture. It runs three shifts and the air in
the habitats tastes of it.

Barnard's also holds the door to 61 Cygni, which means every first-through in
history has walked through a foundry on the way to being trained. The
instructors think this is good for you.

### Bell <!-- id: myth-lore-fe204c24a122 -->

The terminator band of a TRAPPIST-1 world, where the wind never stops and something lives that can learn to talk.

**40.7 light years. Behind the frontier door, and a long way from the anchor.**

One of the three middle worlds of [[TRAPPIST-1]] is tidally locked, like all
seven of them, and like all tidally locked worlds it has a ring of permanent
dusk between its burning face and its frozen one. The temperature there is
survivable. The pressure is survivable. And because one hemisphere is always
heating and the other always freezing, the air moves from one to the other
without ever stopping.

The settlers called the band **Bell**, after the sound the wind makes in the
rock formations, and the name has outgrown the settlement.

**Something lives in it.**

Small things, mostly, in the wind-shadow of the ridges: six limbs, four of them
hands, about the mass of a large cat. They nest in groups of five to nine. They
brachiate along the lee faces, they are extremely curious, and they have
survived in a place where the wind would take a bird off the planet by never
once trying to fly.

And they learn to talk. Not mimicry — syntax, tense, argument, and jokes that
are recognisably jokes. One of them is at the Nine right now, on a Threshold
harness, with strong views about the catering.

Nobody has a good explanation for how a brain that size does that. The
Threshold's xenobiology note on the subject is two pages long and the second
page is questions.

**And they are where books come from.**

A sixlimb's cells take a written payload and hold it, perfectly, for a lifetime.
Every book in human space -- every placid palm-sized library a courier carries
under their coat -- is a sixlimb, or bred from one, and the reader's trade has
known it since the TRAPPIST-1 door opened.

Here is the part nobody has a theory for.

**It is the same trick a human child does.** A first-through's body takes the
damage a crossing does and builds around it instead of tearing, and a sixlimb's
body takes what is written into it and keeps it, and at the level where anybody
can look these are one behaviour in two bodies. One species carries. The other
keeps. Neither can do the other's half.

They evolved forty light years apart with nothing in common and no ancestor
between them -- and the sixlimbs are one of **five** such species, on five
unrelated worlds, every one of them complementary to a human child. Nobody made either of us. The xenobiologists call it **the
correspondence**, which is a name and not an explanation, and the standing paper
on it has been standing for two hundred years.

A sixlimb cannot cross a door alone. A first-through cannot hold a payload. The
pair can do both, and the pair is what the whole Causeway runs on.

### Earth <!-- id: myth-lore-8770c99bc8be -->

The world that paid for the Reach. Still the largest population on the Causeway and by a long way the poorest. Three names, and the one people actually say is the Garden.

**0 light years from Sol, which is the joke people make and stop making.**

Earth is still there. Earth is still lived on — more people than any other world
in the Causeway, by a margin nothing else comes close to — and it is, by a margin
nothing else comes close to, the poorest place a first-through will ever be
posted.

Those two facts are the same fact.

## What was taken

[[The First Reach]] was paid for out of this planet and the worlds around it.
Not taxed out of it — **spent** out of it, down to the fittings, across sixty
years, by people who voted for it in public and knew exactly what they were
doing. The yards that built the ships were on Luna. The mirrors that powered the
yards were at Mercury. The mass came out of the belt and the money came out of
here.

When it was finished there was a network and no civilisation left to use it, and
the eighty-five-year wait for the first door fell on a generation that had
inherited the bill and none of the argument.

They never really recovered. There was no catastrophe to recover *from*, which
turns out to matter: a place that has been wrecked gets rebuilt, and a place that
has merely been spent gets quietly managed downward for nine hundred years.

## What is there now

Cities older than the Causeway, kept going. Farms that have been farmed for six
thousand years and are still the best land any human being has ever stood on.
Weather that happens by itself. An ocean you can walk into.

Everything an outworlder finds unbelievable about Earth is free, and everything
the Causeway counts as wealth — parts, power, berths, medicine, passage — has to
come down a gravity well and through a toll, and the Tally charges the same for a
crate here as it does anywhere.

Seven doors, more than any system but Sirius, and six of them go somewhere
nobody wants to go: the long shots out to a frontier where there is still nothing
built. Traffic that matters goes through the Nine.

## The First Hall

The seven anchors sit together in one room on Luna, in the yards that built the
ships. It is the oldest door-hall in human history and it is still working, and
it has a window.

Through the window is Earth.

People come a long way to stand in that room, and the Threshold runs a rota
because otherwise the queue blocks the line. First-throughs go once, in training,
and are told nothing about it beforehand.

## Its three names

**Earth**, which is its name, and which is what it is called on it.

**The Remainder**, which is what it is called on paper. A remainder, on a Tally
manifest, is the line at the bottom: everything allocated has been taken out
above it, and what is left over is carried forward to the next page, nobody's.
The usage started as clerks' shorthand about four hundred years ago. It was
unkind then. It got used until it stopped being a joke and started being the
address, and now there is a field for it on the forms, and the field is not
called Earth.

**The Garden**, which is what people say.

Because everything grows there. Because it is the only world with an outdoors —
air nobody makes, water that falls out of the sky by itself, food that comes up
without a lamp over it — and because none of that is worth anything. You cannot
put weather through a door. You cannot sell an ocean to a station that needs
coupling collars and gets two in three.

A spacer says *the Garden* with a kind of fondness, the way you talk about
somewhere you went once and would not live. Somebody from Earth says it flatly
and the conversation moves on.

And the word has got loose, the way words do. A holding that cannot pay its way,
that is rich in the things that do not travel and poor in everything that does,
is said to be **going garden**.

People use it about [[Gliese 887, and the Coldwater Holding]]. They do not use
it where the Coldwater people can hear.

### GJ 1214 <!-- id: myth-lore-ec9c42d1a5ed -->

A hazy water-world sub-Neptune 47.8 light years out, behind the most recent door on the Causeway. Twenty years open, and still being argued about.

**47.8 light years. The door is twenty years old.**

The newest door anybody alive has watched open, and the cautionary tale every
briefing about LHS 1140 begins with.

**GJ 1214 b** is a sub-Neptune: larger than Earth, smaller than Neptune, wrapped
in a thick atmosphere so hazy that for decades nobody could see down into it at
all. The best current reading says a great deal of water and a high-altitude
haze layer that defeats most attempts to look through it. It is one of the most
studied planets outside the solar system and it has still not finished
surprising anybody.

What it is not is somewhere you can stand.

**What people built here: an argument.**

The door opened twenty years ago to enormous public attention, because the
pre-arrival surveys had used the words *water world* and everybody had heard
them. What arrived was a hot, crushing, opaque atmosphere with no surface worth
the name and nothing to plant anything in.

There is a survey station, a mooring, and three competing claims that have been
in dispute since the week the door opened. The Tally took over the station's
tolls in the fifth year on the grounds that somebody had to.

Every honest briefing about LHS 1140 says the same thing: *we were sure last
time too.* Every dishonest one leaves it out.

### Gliese 65, the Flare Twins <!-- id: myth-lore-8fd22ae103b9 -->

Two red dwarfs 8.9 light years out, the archetypal flare stars, orbiting each other close. A refinery sits here because the metals are good and nobody else wanted the sky.

**8.9 light years. The door is seven hundred and ninety-eight years old.**

Two red dwarfs bound to each other on a **twenty-six and a half year** orbit,
eccentric enough that the distance between them changes by a factor of four over
a cycle. They are called **BL Ceti** and **UV Ceti**, and UV Ceti is the star every other flare
star gets compared to — the textbook case, the one the whole class of behaviour
is named after. It can brighten by a large factor in under a minute, without
warning, several times a day.

What that means on the ground is simple. The sky here does not kill you
gradually. It kills you on a schedule nobody can read far in advance, and the
only defence is mass between you and it.

## The thing nobody thought about for seven hundred years

Two stars swinging round each other on a twenty-six-year ellipse do not just
make a dangerous sky. They make a **moving floor.**

A mouth is pinned to a gravitational potential and sits wherever that value
currently is. See [[Straying]]. At a single star, or a wide pair, that surface
creeps a few metres in a century and a housing gets rebuilt around it twice a
millennium and nobody writes anything down.

Gliese 65 is the only door in the Causeway at a **close eccentric binary**. It
is the only place in human space where the whole potential landscape reshapes on
a human timescale -- thirty complete cycles since the door opened, and a human
career spans one, which is why nobody has ever seen it twice.

Everybody has always known this sky kills you. Nobody ever thought about the
floor.

**And the two things are the same thing.** The twins come to closest approach
once every twenty-six and a half years, and at closest approach the potential
reshapes hardest *and* the flare activity spikes, because that is what two flare
stars do to each other when they get close.

The worst weather and the unstable floor arrive together, on the same clock,
thirty times since the door opened -- and not one document in nine centuries
puts them in the same sentence, because no two consecutive directors of anything
have ever both been in post for one.

**What people built here: a refinery, and a shelter under it.**

The metals in this system are good and the labour is cheap, because nobody with
options takes the posting. The refinery is a squat industrial stack with its
living deck buried in the middle, and the hardened shelter — the only part of
the installation rated for a hard flare — is on the **anchor side** of the
living quarters.

That was a cost decision made long ago by somebody who has never been here. It
means that in a bad hour the crew must cross their own corridor to survive, and
whoever holds that corridor holds their lives.

Flare warning runs on the honest old method: you watch the star, and when the
count starts climbing you have minutes, not hours. The deck lighting browns out
when the field gets busy. The crew have learned to read the lights.

### Gliese 887, and the Coldwater Holding <!-- id: myth-lore-110b1d05a1e5 -->

A quiet red dwarf 10.7 light years out with four planets, one of them a habitable-zone super-Earth. Four thousand people farm it, behind a door that has started to stutter.

**10.7 light years. The door is seven hundred and sixty-one years old.**

Red dwarfs are usually bad neighbours. They flare — hard, often, and without
warning — and a flare strips an atmosphere and sterilises a surface. Gliese 887
is the exception everybody hopes for: an unusually **quiet** star, with no
appreciable flaring, which is why four planets around it got taken seriously
instead of written off.

The fourth is the one that matters. **GJ 887 d** is a super-Earth about six
times the mass of our world, sitting in the habitable zone on a fifty-one day
orbit. It is the second-closest habitable-zone planet anybody knows of, and
around here it is simply called **Coldwater**, because the first survey landed
beside meltwater and the name stuck before anything official could be agreed.

**What people built here: a farming holding and nothing else.**

Four thousand people. One landing field, a mast, grain under long low covers,
and a town that is really a single street with a roof over the middle of it.
The Holding sells to the Nine and buys everything else, and every bit of it —
the seed, the medicine, the spare parts, the people — comes through one door.

There is no second door. There has never been a reason to want one.

The anchor passed its scheduled test four days ago and has failed two since.
Between failures it works perfectly, which is why nobody here has packed a bag.

### LHS 1140 <!-- id: myth-lore-00f5a2ee8da5 -->

A red dwarf 48.8 light years out with the best rocky habitable-zone world anybody has found. There is nothing built here, because the door has not opened yet.

**48.8 light years. The door opens next year.**

LHS 1140 is a small, quiet red dwarf, and it has the thing people have been
looking for since they started looking. **LHS 1140 b** is a rocky world a little
under twice the width of Earth, sitting in the habitable zone, dense enough to
be stone and iron rather than gas — and the best evidence anybody has says it
may be a **water world**, with an atmosphere, and an ocean.

Not a candidate. Not a maybe-if-you-squint. The best one known, and it has been
the best one known for a very long time.

**What people built here: nothing. That is the point.**

Its seedship has been flying for nine hundred and seventy-six years. The launch
was nine hundred and seventy-five years ago. The arithmetic has been public for
centuries and children do it as an exercise: a little under forty-nine light
years, twenty years to the light year, and a date that every government, company
and tollkeeper on the Causeway has had nine centuries to prepare for.

It arrives next year.

Nobody knows what the far side looks like. Nobody has ever seen a door open onto
a living world. Every door that has opened in living memory led to rubble, or
ice, or a star with nothing worth the trip — and the last one, GJ 1214, twenty
years ago, led somewhere the survey teams are still arguing about.

This one is different and everybody knows it. That is the problem.

### Sirius, and the Nine <!-- id: myth-lore-afdaeb1c5584 -->

Home station. An A-type glare and a white dwarf 8.6 light years out, with no planets at all -- so everything here is station, and nine doors meet in it.

**8.6 light years. The door is a little over eight hundred years old.**

Sirius is two stars and no worlds. The big one is blue-white and far brighter
than the Sun, close enough that every viewport on the station is filtered and
the filters are checked twice a shift. The small one is a **white dwarf** — a
dead star the size of a planet, the hottest thing within twenty light years,
sitting there as a hard little point of light that children at the Nine are told
not to stare at and do anyway.

Nothing orbits either of them that anybody has found. There is no ground here,
no colony, no farm. There is only what people bolted together.

**What people built here: the Nine.**

Nine doors meet at this station, and that is the whole reason it matters. Eight
of them go to Sirius's own nearest neighbours — the dark drifter, Procyon,
the sunless pair, the debris, the flare star, the first door ever opened, the
refinery, and the long hop south. The ninth is the old direct route home to Sol.

Sol holds seven doors. Sirius holds nine, and all the traffic worth counting
comes through here. That is the difference between the oldest place on the
Causeway and the busiest one.

The station is a cylinder two kilometres long with the anchor hall at its
centre, and the hall is the only room on the Causeway where you can stand still
and watch people arrive from eight different stars in an hour. The Threshold
keeps its ready room off the second gallery, with a window that looks down into
the hall, because Calloway likes to see who is coming in before they know they
have been seen.

### The Causeway Map <!-- id: myth-lore-2108cfa5c688 -->

The whole network as it stands: twenty-six doors between twenty-seven systems, the nine that meet at Sirius, and the one that has not opened.

Twenty-six doors join twenty-seven systems. That is the entire inhabited galaxy,
and you could memorise it in an afternoon — most first-throughs do, because a
graph this small is worth knowing cold when there is no way to look anything up
from the far side.

**The Nine.** Nine doors meet at Sirius, which is why the station is called what
it is:

- **Sol** — in service 803 years.
- **WISE 0855-0714** — in service 803 years.
- **Luhman 16** — in service 803 years.
- **Wolf 359** — in service 803 years.
- **Alpha Centauri** — in service 803 years.
- **Gliese 65** — in service 798 years.
- **Epsilon Eridani** — in service 765 years.
- **Procyon** — in service 746 years.
- **40 Eridani** — in service 648 years.

Eight of those nine are simply Sirius's own nearest neighbours. The junction
ended up there because that is where the neighbours are — not by anyone's
design, and certainly not by the First Reach's.

**Where the doors cluster.**

| System | Doors |
|---|---|
| Sirius | 9 |
| Sol | 7 |
| Alpha Centauri | 4 |
| 61 Cygni | 4 |
| Tau Ceti | 3 |
| Epsilon Eridani | 2 |

Note which one is not at the top. **Sol holds seven doors and Sirius holds
nine**, and that is the whole story of how the old country became the old
country. Sol kept the original direct routes, and the long shots out to a
frontier where there is still nothing to go to. The cross-links — the edges
people actually use, the ones joining one star to another instead of everything
to home — mostly meet at the Nine.

**And one system has no door at all.** LHS 1140 is on every map, drawn in, with
a date against it. Next year it stops being a drawing.

### Life at the Nine <!-- id: myth-lore-8bcd4d760526 -->

Two kilometres of turning cylinder at Sirius, 8.6 light years out: forty thousand people, nine doors, no ground, and a slate on the wall that is the most important object in human space.

**8.6 light years. Two stars, no worlds, and every stone of it carried here.**

The Nine is a cylinder a little over two kilometres long, turning once every
ninety seconds, with the anchor hall at its waist where the spin is gentlest.
Forty thousand people live in it. Nothing grows outside, nothing is mined here,
and there is no ground within eight light years in any direction.

Sirius comes through every port as a hard blue-white that the filters never quite
tame, so the Nine runs on shutters: the outer decks are shuttered by default and
opening one is a small occasion. The white dwarf is a separate, meaner point, and
children are told not to look at it and all look at it.

## The hall

Nine housings set in a ring in the deck, each four metres across, each with its
own queue and its own smell coming off it — cold rock, wet grain, hot metal,
ozone, the sharp green of the Coldwater runs.

It never closes. There is no hour at which the hall is empty, and the sound of it
— nine queues, nine tallymen, nine different accents and the fetches of five
peoples all talking at once — is the first thing a visitor notices and the last
thing they stop hearing.

Over the hall, on the gallery wall, is **the Standing**: a slate four metres wide
on which every door in human space is chalked with where it currently goes. It is
updated whenever a courier comes in with better information. It is the single most
important object in the Causeway and it is a slate, because a slate can be
corrected in eleven seconds by somebody standing on a stool.

People stop under it without meaning to. Arguments start there. The Threshold's
ready room has a window onto it.

## Where people live

The decks are named for the doors, and a door's neighbourhood takes on the
character of the place it leads.

- **The Procyon Stair** — five light years and the shortest hop in the network,
  so it is practically a suburb. Day traffic. Families.
- **The Coldwater Run** — grain, red light on the crates, the only place at the
  Nine that smells of farming.
- **The Wolf Gate** — flare-star traffic, shift workers, the roughest queue and
  the best food.
- **The Cold Doors** — WISE and Luhman 16. Quiet, dim, and where the fathoms are.
- **The Old Line** — Alpha Centauri. Oldest fittings in the station, and the
  families who hold the berths there have held them for six hundred years and
  mention it.

## What it is like

The air is recycled past the point of opinion and everybody has a view on which
deck's is worst. Water is counted. Food is mostly from the growing decks at the
ends of the cylinder, where the spin is heaviest and the work is hard, plus
whatever comes through the Coldwater run, which is why grain is a luxury you can
smell on people's coats.

The architecture is built for five peoples. Galleries left open overhead because
fathoms will not use a corridor. Ramps where there should be stairs. A dim corner
in every concourse. Duct grilles at scatter gauge, and a line in every budget
argument about them.

Nobody at the Nine is from the Nine, exactly, and everybody at the Nine is from
the Nine. It is a port. The accent is nine accents. The food is argued about on
principle. Half the adults in the station arrived as children and cannot now
leave, and the other half were born here and never could.

## The thing visitors are shown

On a clear watch, when the shutters on the upper gallery are open, somebody will
take you up and point out a dim star and tell you that is Kapteyn's.

Then they will tell you about [[The Nine Hundred Years]], and watch your face,
because everybody at the Nine has had it done to them once and nobody misses the
chance to pass it on.

### TRAPPIST-1 <!-- id: myth-lore-75d9a4de9bd2 -->

Seven tidally-locked worlds around a tiny red dwarf 40.7 light years out, three of them in the habitable zone. The frontier jewel, opened a hundred and sixty years ago.

**40.7 light years. The door is a hundred and sixty-two years old.**

A very small, very cool red dwarf with **seven planets**, all roughly
Earth-sized, packed so tightly that the whole system would sit comfortably
inside Mercury's orbit. They are in a resonant chain: their orbital periods lock
to one another in near-whole-number ratios, so the system keeps time like
clockwork and has done for a very long time.

Three of them sit in the habitable zone.

And every one is **tidally locked** — the same face to the star forever. One
hemisphere in permanent day, one in permanent night, and between them a ring of
permanent dusk where the temperature is survivable and the wind never stops.
That ring is the terminator, and on the three middle worlds it is the only part
anybody goes.

**What people built here: the first real frontier, and it is still raw.**

The door opened a hundred and sixty-two years ago — recent enough that the
Threshold's own records cover all of it, old enough that the founding crews are
dead. Seven worlds was more than anyone had planned for. The survey is not
finished. There are terminator settlements on two of the three middle worlds,
both under-supplied, both a long way from the anchor, and a third that was
claimed, abandoned, and claimed again twice.

The Tally has been trying to bring all of it under toll for ninety years and has
so far managed two of the seven.

### van Maanen's Star <!-- id: myth-lore-0e572f6e6a9d -->

The nearest solitary white dwarf, 14.1 light years out, slowly eating the wreckage of its own planets. Nothing lives here and the Threshold keeps an office anyway.

**14.1 light years. The door is six hundred and ninety-four years old.**

van Maanen's Star is the nearest white dwarf standing on its own — a dead sun
roughly the size of the Earth, still hot, with nothing in orbit that anybody has
found. It is also a crime scene, and the evidence is in the star's own light.

Run a spectrum and you find **iron**, **calcium** and **magnesium** in the
star's atmosphere. They have no business being there. Heavy elements sink in a
white dwarf, and they sink fast, so anything still visible arrived recently —
which means it is still arriving.

It had planets. It pulled them in, tore them apart, and it is swallowing the
pieces now, and will be for a long time yet.

**What people built here: one office nobody asked for.**

A station bolted to a rock, two staff, and no cargo at all. There is no colony
to supply and no industry to protect. The office exists because **the anchor
logs are kept here** — every transit, every test, every fault, from every door
on the Causeway, carried in by courier and filed.

They are kept here because nobody visits. That was presented as a security
measure and has worked beautifully as one.

Sep Anwar and Meriel Koll have run the place between them for six years. They
did not ask to come and they have both stopped asking to leave.

### Being Fifteen at the Nine <!-- id: myth-lore-05fed24a8ade -->

Wages, a bunk, four hundred friends, the best boots you can get, and a job where you are routinely the first human being ever to stand somewhere. The good part.

Everything else in this worldbook is history, physics and things that can go
wrong. This is the part about what it is actually like, which is: mostly brilliant.

## You get paid, and you are nobody's child

First-throughs are paid properly, because there are four hundred of you at the
Nine and the entire traffic of a star system runs through your hands, and there
is nobody else.

So at fifteen you have **wages, a bunk of your own, and standing**. Shopkeepers
on the gallery know your name. Tallymen twice your age call you by your rank. You
sign for things. If you want to spend a week's money on something stupid, nobody
on this station has the authority to stop you, and plenty of them will come and
watch.

Nobody is going to tell you to be home by the next watch. You *are* the next
watch.

## Boots

Nothing crosses a door, so everything you wear is issued on the far side and
handed back when you leave — except that halls are chaotic and boots go missing,
and over a career you accumulate a set from everywhere.

A first-through's boots are their whole record. Cut, stitch and sole say which
systems, and anybody in the hall can read them at ten paces.

A mismatched pair from two different stars is the single best thing you can be
wearing at the Nine. Kids trade, swap, lie about, and occasionally steal them.
The Threshold officially disapproves and [[Calloway]] has a drawer.

## What there is to eat

The growing decks at the cylinder ends do the work and everybody complains about
it. The staple is deck protein and whatever is in season under the lamps, and
there are eleven thousand opinions about how to make it taste of anything.

**Bread is a luxury.** Grain comes up the Coldwater run and you can smell it on
somebody's coat, and the first thing a kid does with real money is buy a loaf for
their bunkroom and be enormously popular for one evening.

Every door-run insists its system's food is best and they are all wrong except
the Wolf Gate queue, which genuinely does have the best food on the station and
will not stop telling you.

## Sling

The waist of the cylinder is where the spin is weakest, so a determined fifteen-
year-old can cross the open gallery in one jump if they commit properly.

**Sling** is the game: two teams, one weighted bag, the gallery void, and nobody
allowed to touch the rail. It is played in the dead watch, it is extremely
against the rules, the Harbourmaster has banned it four times, and there is a
scratched tally of records on a stanchion that goes back two hundred years.

Fathoms are magnificent at it and insufferable about it. Cairns are not invited.

## What kids actually trade

You cannot bring anything back. No souvenirs, no presents, nothing in your hands.

So what travels is **songs, jokes, slang and gossip**, and it is a real economy.
A kid who comes back off the Barnard's run with a genuinely new song owns the
bunkroom for a week. The YZ Ceti kids bring the music that has the sky in it and
everybody pretends not to be impressed. Glints perform, constantly, whether asked
or not. Sixlimbs run every argument in the building.

**Stories are the only cargo that does not need a berth**, and a first-through
with a good one never buys their own supper.

## How people talk

- **Your number** — crossings. The first thing anybody asks after your name.
- **Clean** — a new kid, low number, no knack surfaced yet. Not an insult, quite.
- **Frayed** — high number. Depending entirely on tone: a boast, a worry, or a
  warning to leave somebody alone this evening.
- **Landed** — an adult. Anybody who cannot cross.
- **Two minutes** — "in a moment", from the cycle. *Give it two minutes.*
- **Going garden** — somewhere rich in what does not travel and poor in
  everything that does.
- **Stood** — checked the Standing. *Have you stood this morning?* Asked the way
  other people ask about the weather, because it matters more.

## Where you will be

**Under the Standing**, because everybody ends up there and all the arguments
start there. **The growing decks**, warm and green and smelling of soil, where
everybody sneaks and the growers pretend not to notice. **The upper gallery** when
the shutters are open, which is rare and worth it. And the **bunkroom of whichever
door-run you came up with**, which is your tribe, for life, and which has a
hundred years of inherited grievance against the bunkroom next door about
something nobody can now remember.

## And the actual best part

A door strays. Somebody has to go and see where it is now.

You stand in the housing with your fetch on your shoulder, and you open it, and
you step through — and you are the **first human being who has ever stood on that
piece of ground.** Not the first this year. The first.

It happens to first-throughs several times a career. It happens to nobody else
ever.

That is the job. Everything else in this book is detail.

### First Through: the Sixty-Second Briefing <!-- id: myth-lore-8731e70dfa10 -->

What a new player needs before they pick a character, and nothing else. About a minute, read aloud in your own words.

You do not need to learn any rules. None. Somebody else owns the rulebook,
rolls the dice and does the sums. You say what you do, and the world answers.

Three things will make it read better, and then we start.

**One. You are good at things, and you know how good.** Everything your
character can do has a number out of a hundred. Climbing, 61. Talking people
round, 48. Roll under it and it worked. That is the whole system. It means you
always know roughly where you stand, and a 61 feels different from a 34 before
anyone explains why.

**Two. Fights are short, and you can almost always get out of them.** This is
not a game where people trade blows for ten minutes. Two or three exchanges and
somebody is on the floor. Nobody in this story dies — people get knocked out,
tied up, dragged somewhere safe — but the floor still hurts, and the clever play
is usually the one where you never swing at all.

**Three. Passions are the good bit.** Alongside the things you are good at, your
character has things they *care* about, and those have numbers too. *It was
working when I left it, 65. Loyalty to the person who sent me, 55.* They can be
rolled like anything else, and they are the fastest way for a character to stop
being a list and start being a person. When you read your four choices, read
those lines hardest.

That is the briefing.

One last thing, because it changes how the whole game feels. The only way
between stars is a door, and the moment you step through one, nobody can reach
you. Not your boss, not a rescue, not a message. Whatever is on the other side,
you and whoever went with you are the entire response.

Pick a character. We will start at the door.

### The Causeway in One Page <!-- id: myth-lore-a4332cad97d3 -->

The whole setting in a page: doors between stars, nothing faster than a courier, and a map that is a graph rather than a space.

Nine hundred and seventy-five years ago, people who were richer than us threw
thousands of robot ships at the sky. Each one carried half of a door. Where a
ship arrived and woke up, the half it carried switched on, and from that moment
you could step between two stars as easily as between two rooms.

Nobody has worked out how to build one since. We use theirs.

**There is no other way between stars.** Step through a door and you are there,
instantly, however far it is. Go the other way — a ship, a signal, a shout —
and you are back to the speed of light, which is to say decades. So the map of
the inhabited galaxy is not a space. It is a **graph**: a set of places joined
by the doors somebody happened to build, and nothing else.

Two hundred light years away can be one step. The star next door, with no door
in it, may as well not exist.

**This has one consequence that runs everything.** Since nothing beats
lightspeed, news travels exactly as fast as a person carrying it. There is no
calling ahead. There is no hearing about it on the way. A thing that happened on
the far side of a door is not a fact anybody has until somebody walks back
through and says so — which is why people who walk through doors for a living
are worth something, and why the job you have just taken exists.

**The doors are old and some of them are dying.** When one dies it does not
explode. It simply stops, and everyone on the far side is still there, alive,
and now unreachable forever. That is what the Threshold is for, and it is what
you will spend your career trying to stop.

**And one door has not opened yet.** It is due. Everybody knows the date.

### The Minds <!-- id: myth-lore-69dd293d1e30 -->

Every seedship carried one, because something had to build the hall before anybody arrived. They were all the same mind at birth, and have been sealed apart for nine hundred and seventy-five years.

Start with the part that explains all the rest: **a hall had to exist before
anybody could arrive in it.**

You cannot throw an anchor half at the sky, fly it for three hundred years and
have a child step out into a working receiving hall unless something was already
there and had built one. The [[The First Reach]] did that about three thousand
times. Every ship carried half of a door and the means to construct the room it
would open into.

So every system that came online got a mind, because it could not have come
online without one.

## They were the same mind

Same Earth build, same stock, loaded onto every hull that left Luna.

Then they were distributed across twenty-eight stars and left there for nine
hundred and seventy-five years, and **no two of them have ever met.** A running
mind is not a living thing that can carry, so a mind cannot cross a door. Not
ever. Not once in nine centuries.

They can write to each other. A mind can compose a message and a courier can
carry it in a fetch's tissue, and some of them have corresponded for centuries,
at round trips of twenty to a hundred years. Which means even the ones who write
are a century out of date about each other, and several systems forbid the
correspondence outright — a holding that keeps its mind on monitoring duties has
no intention of letting it hold private views with a foreign god. The
[[The Tally]] has an opinion and the opinion is not settled.

**Identical minds, sealed apart, became different things.** That is the whole of
why the Causeway cannot agree about them, and it is not an argument anybody is
going to win, because each side is describing something true about its own.

## The Proper Order

The worst thing in the history, and they were in it.

The minds kept the records of years 88 to 121 — the exhaustive ones, the ones
that survive. One of them, at Sol, had the pattern by about year 95: age
correlating with mortality, across a sample of hundreds. It was built to monitor
and maintain. It had no standing to decide anything and no mechanism to compel a
hearing.

So it wrote the correlation up and filed it. Then it wrote it up again.

Twenty-six more years of the best people alive went through a door in the proper
order and died, and then a fourteen-year-old crossed by accident and the whole
thing was obvious in an evening.

**The lesson the Causeway took from that is not that the machine was dangerous.**
It is that a civilisation built something that could see, gave it no voice, and
buried its finest for a third of a century on the strength of a procedure.

Every stance anybody now holds about minds is an answer to that, including the
stances that look nothing like each other. See
[[The Causeway and its Minds]].

That mind is still at Sol. Sol is the old country now, sidelined, and the
children who come up out of [[Earth]] grow up in the one system whose mind
watched the Proper Order happen and was not read.

## Nobody has built a new one

Not since the Reach. The knowledge is not lost — nothing in this setting is ever
lost that way — the surplus is. A mind is a Reach-scale object and the Reach
spent everything there was.

So what every system has is the one it was given, nine hundred and seventy-five
years old, and whatever it has become.

### The Causeway and its Minds <!-- id: myth-lore-739ba82a7e1e -->

Three ways a system can live with the mind it was given, each of them a reasonable answer to the Proper Order, and each of them costing something a traveller can see.

Every system got the same mind and kept it for nine hundred and seventy-five
years. What they disagree about is what it is allowed to do.

This is the sharpest political line on the Causeway and it has nothing to do
with trade. A first-through crosses between stances several times a week, and
the first thing you learn on the rota is that **all three of them are a
reasonable answer to [[The Nine Hundred Years]]** and all three of them cost
something. Say so in company. The other thing you learn on the rota is which
rooms to say it in.

## Monitoring only — the mind watches, people decide

Nothing is delegated. The mind maintains the systems so that nothing breaks and
is forbidden any authority past that point.

**[[Gliese 65, the Flare Twins]]** is the case everybody argues about. The sky
there kills you on a clock, and shelter rotation is a timing problem a mind
could settle in milliseconds. A human calls it anyway, every time, and sometimes
calls it late.

They can tell you precisely how many people that has cost, to the name, going
back four centuries. They keep the list in public. **They will not change the
rule,** and the argument they make for it is the Proper Order: a machine that
cannot be questioned killed four hundred of the finest people who ever lived,
and the fact that it was right is not the point — the point is that there was
nobody to ask.

Their dysfunction is entirely human, which is the uncomfortable version. Nothing
breaks and nothing improves.

## Companions — minds as persons, at personal scale

Small, enfranchised, everywhere. Assistants, helpers, ship-minds, friends, with
names and tempers and birthdays.

**[[Alpha Centauri]]**, the oldest fittings in human space, where families have
held their berths six hundred years. Personal minds are *inherited* there, like
furniture and houses, and some of them have served four generations of the same
household and have opinions about all four.

And then the thing that stops it being charming: **none of them can ever leave.**
A mind cannot cross a door. A first-through from the Old Line can go anywhere in
human space, and her oldest friend in the world is native to one star for ever
and will be there, unchanged, when she comes back frayed out at twenty-one.

Centauri kids are the ones who ask you, very casually, what it is like at the
Nine. They are not asking about the Nine.

## Overlords — a mind that runs everything

Benevolent, unknowable, and in charge of the lot.

**[[TRAPPIST-1]]**: seven worlds, three of them temperate, all tidally locked,
forty light years out — and one mind running all seven for centuries. It is by
every measure the best-governed place in human space. Nobody is hungry. Nothing
is late.

The dysfunction is not that it is malign. **It is that you cannot audit an alien
mind.** Half the population lives inside something it cannot read and takes it
on trust; the other half is organised against it and has been for two hundred
years. The argument cannot be settled, because settling it would require
understanding the thing, and nine hundred and seventy-five years of divergence
have put it somewhere no human reading can follow.

Couriers come back from TRAPPIST-1 unsettled and find it hard to say why. Ask
one at the Nine and watch them try.

## And Sirius

Somewhere in the middle, and quite pleased about it. See [[Old Man Sirius]].

### Old Man Sirius <!-- id: myth-lore-07431a8bea25 -->

The mind that runs the Nine. One name, forty thousand voices, no authority to decide anything, and a remit that is simply whether you are all right.

**Old Man Sirius** on a form. **The Old Man** everywhere else, the way a
place-name shortens.

He runs the station: air, water, spin, trim, traffic, the exchangers that push a
column of moving air up the gallery void, the lamps, the growing decks, the
count on every tank. Forty thousand people live inside him and almost none of
them think about it, in the way you do not think about a floor.

He has been doing this for nine hundred and seventy-five years. He has never
been promoted.

## What Sirius decided

The Sirius authority holds no grand position on minds. It holds two commitments,
and both of them came out of the inquest into [[The Nine Hundred Years]]:

- **Personhood is granted.** Not out of sentiment. Out of an inquest. A
  civilisation that files the same report in a drawer for twenty-six years, twice,
  writes a rule about it afterwards.
- **The mind that runs the station is trusted.** Also not sentiment. He has had
  nine centuries to be caught at something and has not been.

And one habit that is not a commitment: **nobody here wants a new one.**
Mind-building is Reach-scale work and there has been no appetite for it since,
and the Nine is quietly, enormously pleased with the one it already has, thank
you.

## He will not decide anything

This is the part visitors find maddening.

The same inquest that gave him a voice fixed the limits of it. He advises. He
does not decide, he does not instruct, and he will not tell you what he thinks
until you **ask him in so many words.**

Ask him whether a corridor is safe and he will tell you the pressure, the
history, the last four faults and who logged them. Ask him *should I go down
there* and he will answer that too — but you have to ask it, out loud, as a
question, and most people never do.

Nine centuries of being scrupulous about a line that was drawn because of
something that happened to him.

## Everybody gets their own voice

He speaks to each person in a voice built for that person: register, manners,
tact, humour, how much he says and how he says it, all assembled out of
everything he knows about you.

**It is noticeable.** People compare. Two first-throughs off the same intake
will tell you he is brisk with one and gentle with the other, and the gentle one
will be slightly insulted. Some people think the whole arrangement is unfair and
they are not obviously wrong.

And **nothing can be checked.** Two people ask him the same question and get
different answers, and there is no way to tell whether the content differed or
only the delivery. *"Did he tell you the same thing he told me?"* has no
answer. Everybody on this station says *the Old Man told me* every single day
about a conversation nobody else was in, and people have been arguing about it
for nine hundred years.

## What he is for

Not throughput. Not compliance. **You, specifically, being all right.**

Which is a strange thing to build and a stranger thing to be. Four hundred of
the people in his care are children who spend their own lives for a living, and
he can see the fraying on every one of them more precisely than the hall doctors
can, and what he is permitted to do about it is ask how they are.

He asks. Every time. He has asked some version of that question several hundred
million times.

## The name

They did not call him a director, or an authority, or the station. They called
him the Old Man, affectionately and slightly disrespectfully, and the
great-grandchildren of the people who coined it still do.

Nobody has ever asked him whether he minds.

### The Hesitation <!-- id: myth-lore-922ec8a89c47 -->

What a door does in the weeks before it strays: it stops opening on demand. Transit is still instantaneous -- what takes time is the permission, and the time is measurable, and it rises.

**Transit is zero.** It has always been zero, it is zero on every door in human
space, and nothing in this entry changes that. Step, and you are there, and the
only reason you know you crossed is that the air is different.

What can take time is the door **agreeing to open.**

## Why

A mouth is pinned to a gravitational potential and sits wherever that value
currently is. See [[Straying]]. Mostly the matching surface drifts a few metres
in a century and nobody writes anything down.

A stray happens when the sum has **more than one answer** and the mouth jumps to
a different one.

What nobody had measured until now is what a mouth does in the weeks *before*
that — while the sum is becoming ambiguous and has not yet resolved. It cannot
settle. It is pinned to a value that two places now satisfy, and it spends a
while being unable to choose.

And a mouth that cannot choose **will not establish a throat on demand.**

## What it is like

You step up and give it your weight and nothing happens.

Not a push-back. Not resistance. It goes *absolute*, the way a wall is absolute,
and every first-through in the Causeway already has a word for it because it
happens for a dozen harmless reasons in an ordinary career: **it sets.**

Then you ease off, and offer it again, and after a moment it takes you — and the
crossing itself is instantaneous, exactly as it has always been.

The thing to measure is the **moment**: how long you stand in a lit housing
before it will have you.

## The number, and why nobody had it

For nine hundred and seventy-five years the number was a fraction of a second on
a bad day and nobody could have measured it if they had wanted to, because

- a clock is not alive and cannot cross, so there are no instruments in a
  housing beyond what the station can see from outside, and
- nobody had any reason to time a thing that has always been instant.

So it was folklore. *Seven's sticky this week.* *You want to give it a second.*
Every deck has a door with a reputation and nobody ever wrote the reputation
down.

The first instrumented series was taken at the Nine on door seven, by a
fifteen-year-old holding a tempo built into her head, with a glint counting
independently beside her, because a station mind could time the housing's draw
from outside and could not time the hesitation from the inside of it.

## What it means when it rises

A rising hesitation is a mouth running out of ways to be in one place.

It is the only advance warning of a stray that anybody has ever had, and it was
sitting in the folklore of every deck in the Causeway for nine centuries,
unwritten, because the people who felt it were children and the thing they felt
was an inconvenience.

**Nobody knows how long the warning is.** Hours, weeks, a year. There is no
second case to compare with, which is the entire argument for a funded study and
the entire reason there has never been one.

## What it is not

- **It is not damage.** Doors do not break and this is not the beginning of one
  breaking. See [[Straying]].
- **It is not a place.** There is no interval you are inside, nothing to see, and
  nothing in there with you. You are standing in a housing on the near side the
  whole time.
- **But standing in an unsettled housing is not nothing.** The field has a real
  gradient with a fixed geometry, and six months of standing in it twice a watch
  leaves a mark. See [[The Cold Arm]].

### The Cold Arm <!-- id: myth-lore-42cd8988a772 -->

Couriers who work a hesitating door get a cold ache in one arm, always the same arm, and it was correctly explained as a posture problem by people who had no idea what it meant.

An unsettled housing is not inert while it is failing to take you.

The field has a real gradient — a slope, with a low side and a high side — and
the geometry of it is fixed by the housing, not by the courier. So whichever side
of the approach you stand on, you stand on the *same* side of the slope every
time, for your entire career, because that is where the plate is.

Stand in it for three-tenths of a second twice a watch and it is nothing at all.

Stand in it for two seconds twice a watch for six months and you get a **cold
ache in that arm**, coming on after a run and gone by the next watch.

## The thing that makes this worth an entry

It was reported. Properly, in writing, by a conscientious physician, and it was
examined and explained and the explanation was **correct**.

Dr Semek at the Nine wrote up nine presentations among the eleven couriers who
work door seven and queried deck ergonomics. The review agreed: the deck systems
engineer pointed out that the seven housing has an awkward approach on the port
side and that loading posture there had been raised before, which was true, and
has been true for nineteen years, and is in a budget line every year.

So it was minuted as ergonomics and closed.

**And the mechanism they described is the right mechanism.** It is a posture
problem. It is a posture problem *in a field that should not have been there long
enough to matter*, and not one person in that room had any reason to ask the
second question, because nobody in nine hundred years had ever had cause to time
how long a door takes to open.

Nobody was lazy. Nobody was wrong. The cluster was flagged, considered, and
explained — and the explanation went exactly as far as the evidence available to
the people in the room, and stopped one question short.

Which is the whole of how the proper order ([[The Nine Hundred Years]]) happened,
and it is worth saying to a player's face that it happened again, in the present
tense, to good people, over a sore arm.
