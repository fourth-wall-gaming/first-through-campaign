Nine hundred and seventy-five years ago, a species with one star spent
everything it had on robot ships, and sent them out at a twentieth of the speed
of light, and did not expect to live to see any of it work.

Each ship carried half of a pair. The other half stayed home. A pair is made
together, in one place, in one go, in a silence colder and darker and stiller
than anything in nature, and if anything at all touches it while it is being
made you have four metres of extremely expensive scrap and you begin again. They
attempted four thousand, one hundred and nineteen. Three thousand and eleven
worked.

Where a ship arrived, a door opened.

A door is instant. You step up onto the plate, and you are eight light years
away, and the only reason you know you crossed is that the air is different.
Nothing else goes. Not light, not a signal, not a word, not a gram of cargo —
only a person, and whatever a person is holding. Inside a system, radio works
and everybody forgets it. *Between* stars, the only thing that moves is
somebody walking.

Which means a letter from Sirius to Earth travels at the speed of a fifteen-year-old
getting round to it.

Because that is the other half of it: only the young can cross. A crossing
costs the body something that does not come back, and it is spent fast, and by
about twenty-two a person is finished. So the entire traffic of human space —
every message, every account, every doctor wanted three doors away, every old
woman who wants to see her sister — rides on children. They are called
first-throughs. They are paid, they are housed, they are nobody's child, and at
fifteen they are the most useful people alive.

Carrying another person through costs more than crossing alone. Not an
inconvenience; days off the end of a life, counted. A child is cheap to carry
and an adult is dear, and anybody over about twenty-two cannot cross at all,
not once, not ever, and must be carried by somebody young or stay where they
are for good.

Twenty-eight systems hang off that, and nobody will ever build another door,
because the thing that would pay for one cannot be gathered through the doors
there already are. So the Causeway was not built by anybody now alive. It was
inherited, and the parts of it that can be reached are maintained.

The doors do not always stay where they are. A door is pinned not to a place
but to a depth — to the sum of all the gravity around it — and once in a while
that sum has more than one answer, and the far mouth goes somewhere else. It is
called straying. It has happened often enough that there is a word for it, and a
board four metres wide on the gallery wall at Sirius with every door in human
space written up on it by hand, and where it currently goes, and when somebody
last looked.

In the spring of year six hundred and two a door at Kapteyn's Star stopped
answering, and eleven thousand people went with it, and the line on the board
has never been changed.

The star is still there. You can see it from a corridor.

## A note on the setting

This book is a record of how sessions one to five were actually played, in
2026, and it is left as it was played. The setting was substantially rewritten
afterwards, so a few things here are no longer true of the world: couriers
undress at a door and leave their boots in a locker, the Standing is a board on
a wall, van Maanen's is a station with two staff on it, and there is a cold ache
in a courier's arm that nobody can explain.

None of those are true any more. The weave is the only thing you were ever
issued that no station issued you and it crosses with you; the boots are grown
and they come too; the Standing is alive; van Maanen's is a town of three
thousand; and the ache in the arm turned out to be a door, which is a different
book.

Read this as what happened, not as how the world works. The worldbook in
`lore/` is the world.
