# Chapter 6 — Four Thousand Points

*Day one · second watch, bay four*

Nine levels up, and the spin got lighter the whole way, and by the top gallery she was walking in that half-floating way that makes the first-throughs show off and makes everybody over thirty hold the rail.

The upper gallery was long and curved and almost empty, and shuttered — of course it was. Sirius comes through every port as a hard blue-white the filters never quite tame.

Sen walked to the fourth bay along and put her hand flat on the plate beside it.

"This one. Second watch, every day, for about forty minutes. Nobody knows. There's a maintenance override and the man who does the override is called Hesk and he thinks he's the only one who ever uses it."

A clunk somewhere behind the wall. Then the shutter went up.

---

And it was just stars.

That's the thing nobody tells you. Sirius wasn't in that bay — she'd picked the one looking the other way — so there was no glare, just the deep black and about four thousand points of it, and the Nine turning slowly enough that the whole field drifted.

Sen waited. She didn't fill it. She'd done this before and she knew the gap is part of it.

Then she leaned in next to Lint's shoulder and pointed, and her finger was small and chalky, and she sighted along it carefully to get it right.

"There. Not the bright one — that's nothing, that's a foreground thing. The dim one, just under it, orange. You'd walk straight past it."

Lint had to look twice to find it. Then she couldn't stop seeing it.

"Kapteyn's," said Sen.

Twelve and a half light years. Eleven thousand people, in the spring of six hundred and two. A good world, farms and foundries, and a door that went dark and stayed dark.

"It's still there," said Sen. "That's the bit. People go *oh, that's sad*, and it isn't sad, it's *there*. It's a whole world and it's sitting right there and you can see it from a corridor."

She lowered her arm. "And the light coming out of it now left twelve and a half years ago, so even if something happened yesterday we wouldn't—"

She stopped herself, because she's eleven and she could hear that she was showing off.

"Anyway. That's it. That's the thing everybody wants to do to you." A small, satisfied breath. "And I got to do it."

---

She stayed. Sen went back down at speed because Oris was sitting her board.

So it was her and the bay and four thousand points of light. The Nine turned. The field drifted, slowly, the whole of it together, which is the only way you ever really feel the spin once you've lived in it.

BlingBling didn't say anything for the whole ten minutes. She came off the stanchion at some point and sat on the rail beside Lint's hand, not touching, dimmed right down — not the sleeping colour, a different one, flatter — and kept her own counsel, which for her is close to unprecedented.

And then four hundred metres of curve turned out to be four hundred metres of curve. Shuttered bays, numbered. A fire locker. A long scuff on the deck where something heavy got dragged a century ago. Two notices about not using the gallery as a shortcut, one of them stencilled over the other.

Which is why she didn't notice the time going.

The clunk came from behind the wall, the same one as before, and the shutter in bay four slid down at the far end of the curve. And the lights up there are on the same override as the shutter, because of course they are, because nobody is ever up there.

It went properly dark.

BlingBling came up off the rail instantly and went to full white, which is about as much light as a hand torch and about as much subtlety as a flare, and she was extremely pleased to be useful.

---

Ninety seconds into walking back, a voice came from a hatch at ankle height, slightly muffled, deeply aggrieved.

"— who's *up* here?"

A panel swung. A head came out of the deck. Fifties. Coverall. A headlamp pointed straight at her.

"You're not Oris."

"...No."

"How'd you know about the second-watch override?" He hauled himself up out of the hatch and stood, and he was not angry, he was *scandalised*. "That's not — nobody knows about that. I don't put that on a sheet."

He peered at her. "You're one of Calloway's. The new one. Thirty-four at fifteen." A pause, and then, bitterly: "It was Sennet, wasn't it."

"I'd love to know who's asking," said Lint, perfectly pleasantly, "because the lights just went out on me four hundred metres from a stair, and I don't actually know your name."

That landed. You could see it land.

"...Hesk." He said it like he was conceding a point he hadn't expected to be playing for. "Deck systems. Shutters, ports, atmosphere trim on nine through twelve." He straightened his coverall. "And the lights are on the *shutter* circuit, up here, which is not my doing, that's six hundred years old and there's a line in the budget about it every single year—"

"It's dark, though."

"It is dark," he admitted, with enormous reluctance. "Yes. It is dark."

He got a torch out of a leg pocket, finally, and put it on the deck pointing up, which filled the whole bay with a soft bounce and was a far better idea than anything either of them was doing.

"Right. Second-watch override. Who told you?"

"Somebody who keeps the Standing and notices when a line's gone stale."

"*Sennet.*" He closed his eyes briefly. "That child is eleven years old and knows more about this station than my own staff. I've found her in a trunking run. Twice."

A beat.

"She's not wrong, though. That bay's the only one on the upper deck that doesn't look into Sirius, so it's the only one worth opening, and nobody ever asks, so I open it for forty minutes when the trim's settled and I eat my lunch in it." He said this defiantly, as if expecting to be challenged. "There's no rule against it."

Then, suspiciously: "What were *you* doing up here? There's nothing up here. I've been doing this nineteen years and there is nothing up here."

She pointed down the curve at bay four, which was shut, and had nothing there now but a grey plate and a number stencilled on it.

Hesk looked where she was pointing, and then at her, and the scandalised thing went out of his face.

"Ah," he said.

He didn't ask which star. He didn't say *oh, Kapteyn's* either. He just stood there for a second with his hands in his pockets.

"Nineteen years I've had this deck." He said it to the shutter rather than to her. "And they only ever come up here in a crowd. Fourteen of them at a time, somebody's birthday, somebody showing off to a visitor — up, point, *ooh*, down the stair, gone in four minutes."

He took his torch back off the deck.

"Nobody's ever come up on their own and stood there."

Then, briskly, because he was embarrassed: "It's orange. You'd think it'd be red, people always expect red. It's not far off the colour of the warning lamp on a trim panel, which I've always found extremely annoying."

So he did know which one it was. Without looking. From a corridor, with the shutter down. Nineteen years of lunches.

"Right." He was already walking. "Thirty seconds. Don't tell Sennet I did it twice in one watch, she'll want it in writing."

The clunk. The shutter went up. The lights didn't — he left those off, deliberately, and that turned out to be the entire difference, because without the gallery lights the glass stopped being a window with reflections in it and just stopped being there.

Four thousand points. The field drifting with the spin.

Hesk stood a couple of metres off, not crowding, eating a sandwich he had produced from somewhere, and said nothing at all for a long time.

Then: "Twelve and a half years, that light. So whatever's happening there now, we'll find out when I'm dead."

He chewed.

"Still there, though."
