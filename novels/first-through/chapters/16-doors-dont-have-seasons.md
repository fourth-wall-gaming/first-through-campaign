# Chapter 16 — Doors Don't Have Seasons

*Day three · the cordon*

"*What the grash, Call—*"

"Mm." Entirely unbothered. "Language, and go on."

"You just told me off in front of thirty people."

"I did. Both those things are true at once and you're going to have to get used to it." She folded her arms. "You've been twelve hours ahead of me since yesterday morning and I'd be an idiot to tell you off and then not ask. I don't care who's got the information. I care that it's in this hall and not in my hand."

Dunnel, from behind her, not looking up from the slate: "She does this."

"I do this."

---

"I'm the one who goes, Call. Me. Always."

Calloway looked at her for a long second.

"I know you are. That's the bit that keeps me awake." She said it quite gently and entirely without conceding anything. "And 'always' is how I lose people. Eleven of them. Every single one was somebody who was always the one who went."

She let that sit for exactly as long as it needed to.

"Right. What have the Wolf Gate lot been saying?"

"I dunno — that it's stickier than normal, or something. Just since this morning."

And Pell Ivor's head came up.

"...No."

Everybody looked at him. He went slightly red and said it anyway, because he is a tallyman and the number was wrong.

"Beg pardon, Director. It's not this morning. I started the board on **Friday**." He turned it round. "Five days. It's been going since Friday and it's been getting worse the whole time, and this morning's just the first day you could *watch* it."

Calloway's jaw moved.

"Five days."

"Five days."

"Pell." She was very quiet now. "You've been on seven eleven years."

"Twelve in the autumn."

"Have you seen it do this before?"

And he opened his mouth to say no, and then didn't, and you could see him actually go and look for it rather than answer fast — which was the single most useful thing anybody did in that hall all day.

"...It goes sticky every spring."

The hall noise, which had come back a bit, went out.

"Every spring. Half a second, maybe. Four or five days of it and then it stops and you forget about it till the next one." He was turning the board over in his hands. "I've had it eleven times. Twelve."

"And this?"

"This isn't half a second, Director." He looked up. "I've got two and a half on the page and it's still going up, and we're on day five."

---

Eleven times. Twelve. Half a second, four or five days, and then it stops.

Somewhere above them Sen was on her stool at the board with the pen in her hand and she had stopped writing.

And Dunnel said, slowly, to nobody: "Doors don't have *seasons*."

So Lint stopped talking and got out of the way, which turned out to be the most useful thing she did all morning.

---

Dunnel's line was the one that did it.

"Sirius. Fault records. Seven. Every spring, going back."

*I have nine hundred and seventy-five years of fault records and I have already looked, and the answer is nothing. There is not one entry. Not a single logged fault on seven for stickiness in eight centuries.*

"How is that—"

*Because nobody logs a sticky door.* He said it flatly. *It is not a fault. It is an inconvenience that resolves itself, which is the definition of the thing nobody writes down, and that is not a failure of the record. It is what the record is **for** and what it is not for.*

"Then Pell's board is the only—"

*Mr Ivor's board, and one man's memory of eleven springs, is the entire documentary history of this phenomenon. Yes.*

Pell Ivor looked like he would very much like to sit down.

And then Sirius said the thing nobody had asked for, and the hall went still for the third time in an hour.

*However. I have been looking in the wrong place and I have found it in the right one.*

*Faults are not the only thing I keep. I keep **throughput.** Crossings per watch, per housing, continuously, since the fourth century, because a station runs on traffic and traffic is counted whether or not anybody cares why.*

*And if a door takes half a second longer to open, several hundred times a watch, for four or five days, then throughput on that housing **dips**. Slightly. Measurably. And then recovers.*

A pause.

*It is there. It has always been there. I have seven hundred and ninety-four of them.*

---

Dunnel stopped writing.

"Seven hundred and—"

*One for very nearly every year the door has existed. A shallow dip, four to six days, followed by full recovery. It is in the data under every single director this station has had and not one of them has looked at it, including me, until eleven minutes ago.*

"So it *is* a spring thing."

*No.*

And that was the word the whole hall heard.

*That is what I wanted to tell you and it is why I interrupted. They are not annual. The gap between them is not three hundred and sixty-five days and it never has been.*

*The dips drift. Slowly, steadily, round and round the calendar, for eight hundred years. Which is precisely why everybody on this deck believes it is a spring thing — because a man works seven for twelve years and in twelve years it barely moves, so it is always roughly when he remembers it.*

*Mr Ivor is not wrong. He is just not old enough.*

Calloway had both hands on the cordon post.

"Then what's the gap?"

*I am computing it properly rather than quickly, because this is a number somebody is going to put in front of a council and I am not going to be approximately right about it.*

*Give me the watch.*

---

*Three hundred and sixty-six days and a fraction. Drifting about a quarter of a day each time.*

*Seven hundred and ninety-four of them, back to the fourth century, as regular as anything I hold.*

Dunnel: "That's a *year*. That's nearly exactly a year."

*It is nearly exactly a year, which is why nobody has ever questioned it, and it is not a year. A year is an Earth convention this station carries around for sentimental reasons. Nothing at Gliese 65 has any interest in it whatever.*

*What has a period of three hundred and sixty-six days at Gliese 65 is **the refinery.** It goes round BL Ceti once in that time, and the far mouth is in a corridor inside it, and a mouth is pinned to a gravitational potential.*

*So once per orbit the refinery passes through a part of the well where the sum gets briefly awkward, and the door is slow for four days, and everybody says it is a spring thing and gets on with their lives.*

"Then why's this one different?"

*Because of the other number, and I have that too, and it is the one I would like everybody in this hall to hear slowly.*

*The dips are not all the same depth. Most are a tenth of a second and nobody notices. Some are half a second and Mr Ivor remembers those. And the depth has **its own cycle**, much longer, sitting on top of the annual one.*

*Twenty-six and a half years.*

A long pause.

*Which is the orbital period of the twins around each other. They are an eccentric pair and they come to closest approach once in twenty-six and a half years, and when they do the whole potential landscape of that system reshapes hardest — and the annual flutter in the door becomes something else.*

*There are thirty of those in my record. Thirty deep ones in seven hundred and ninety-eight years.*

*The last was twenty-six years ago.*

Nobody said anything at all.

Pell Ivor, very quietly: "I came on seven twelve years ago."

*You have seen twelve shallow ones and no deep ones. Nobody currently working this deck has seen a deep one. The youngest person at the Nine who has is sixty-one years old and was a courier for eight months.*

"And this one?"

*This is the thirty-first.*

*It is day five and it is at two point six seconds, and the deepest value anywhere in the previous thirty is one point one.*

---

Dunnel had sat down on the bench inside the cordon.

Calloway had not moved at all. Up on the gallery, four levels of people who had been going about their business an hour ago were hanging over the rails in total silence.

And Sen was on her stool at the Standing with the pen in her hand, looking down at the hall, eleven years old, having just watched the station work out that a door has a *clock*.

*Thirty-one,* said Sirius, on the hall. *Day five. Two point six seconds against a previous record of one point one.*

*And I want to put one more thing in front of this hall before anybody decides anything, because it is a fact and it is the one that bears on what happens next.*

*The thirty previous deep cycles all ended. Four to six days, and recovery, every single time, for seven hundred and ninety-eight years.*

A pause.

*This is day five of a thing that has always stopped by day six. I do not know whether it is about to stop or whether it has stopped being that thing. Nobody does. There is no second case.*
