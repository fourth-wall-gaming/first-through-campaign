# Chapter 21 — The Shed

*Day four · dawn*

Somebody was shaking her shoulder and it was Sen, which should not have been possible, because Sen is eleven and that is a first-through bunkroom four levels from her board.

"It's *lit*."

---

The hall at a dead run, down the service stair, and she could hear it before she saw it — two thousand people making a sound she had never heard a station make.

Door seven was lit.

Nine queues, eight of them stopped dead and all of them watching the ninth. The cordon still up. Dunnel's sheet taped to the deck with nothing written under **4.1**, because nobody had stood the plate yet.

Calloway was inside the rope and Ossuary was beside her and nobody was on the ring.

Nobody knew what was through it. Nobody knew whether the apron had held, or whether that bowser had gone into the hole or onto the ring, or whether anybody over there was standing up.

Calloway saw her come off the stair.

She didn't order it. She just stepped back from the plate and left the space, and about four hundred people worked out what that meant at the same time.

---

Lint walked up to the rope. "Is my kit vacuum and radiation?"

It wasn't. Vacuum and hard cold, both — which is a good pairing and the one she had carried since the Yards. Not radiation.

And Ladder is red rock under a welded roof with the twins closing.

The kit store was nine metres behind her and a re-draw takes about six minutes. Door seven had been lit for four and nobody knew how long it stays.

Ossuary already had her seals in his hands and was waiting to be told which.

On her collar, BlingBling had gone a colour she had only seen once — flat, hard, attention — and she was looking past her at the ring rather than at anybody in the hall.

::: glint
Lin. Ask me about the twins.
:::

"Sure."

::: glint
I was hatched there.
:::

Not a performance. No colour in it at all, which from her is the loudest thing she can do.

::: glint
Nobody at this station has ever put that together with where that door is going, and I have been here two years. It is not a storm, Lin. It is a *season*. The sky goes wrong for weeks and then once or twice a day it tries to kill you, and you do not get a warning from an instrument, because by the time an instrument has it you are already lit.
:::

A pause.

::: glint
You are going into a shed with a welded roof and no rock over it and a skin that stops cold and does not stop light. I cannot fix your kit. I cannot smell a flare coming and neither can anybody else.
:::

She came round onto the front of the collar so Lint could actually see her.

::: glint
What I can do is count that door. Every one of us can. I counted it with you yesterday and I was within four hundredths of you and neither of us was guessing. So you will not have to wonder whether it is getting worse while you are standing in it, because I will be telling you, every time, out loud.
:::

::: glint
Nobody on Ladder has anybody who can do that.
:::

Ossuary, quietly, still holding the seals: "Lin. Six minutes for a re-draw, or now."

"Now. Of course."

---

Ossuary sealed her in forty seconds. Rill was at the rope with her sleeves up and didn't try to stop her. Calloway said nothing at all, which was permission.

She stepped up.

It set.

Not the hunting, grinding thing of the day before — this was different and worse in a way she felt immediately. It was *flat*. Solid from the first instant, the same every time, no give and no nearly.

Four attempts. She eased perfectly each time, the way four hundred people had spent a day learning to.

Nothing.

And Hesk, who had come down to the rope and should not have been inside the cordon, said it out loud before she was off the plate:

"That's not sticky. That's **full**."

Nobody in the hall knew what that meant for about two seconds, and then enough of them did. A throat cannot establish into solid matter. It was lit — so there was air in that shed. But something was sitting in the ring.

Eleven centimetres of fuel bowser, maybe.

Rill, at the rope: "Lin. Off. Now."

Fifth attempt — and Hesk was wrong, and she felt him be wrong, because it gave.

Not easily. It let her in the way a door moves when something heavy is leaning on the other side of it.

Then air.

---

Dust. Bad orange light from a single strip. Cold, dry air that tasted of rust and lamp-fed greenery.

And she came out *sideways*, shoulder-first, because the ring was not clear — there was a sheet of cut deck plate leaning across two-thirds of it, propped, deliberately, like a man holding a door with his boot.

That was what had been leaning on her.

Four seconds, and here is what they got her.

The bowser *is* in a pit. They did it. It was sitting nose-down in a hole the size of a bunkroom about four metres off, and the cut edge of the slab was raw and fresh, and the ring's apron was intact right up to a line somebody had drawn in pen two metres out.

The plate across the ring was a shield. Rock dust and grit were everywhere and they had been cutting concrete in a sealed shed, so somebody had covered the mouth to keep the dust out of it.

There were nineteen children sitting in a row against the far wall with their names written on their hands.

And about thirty people, filthy, grey-faced, mid-work, who had all stopped.

A woman with a cutting torch still in her hand said, into total silence:

"...Is it open?"

---

"Yep. I'm here, from the Nine. Looks like it's operating."

The woman with the torch put it down.

"Tace Orun. Foreman." She didn't offer a hand; both of hers were filthy. "You're Threshold. How many of you are there?"

"Four hundred and six."

Something happened in that shed. Not a cheer. Thirty people who had spent thirteen hours cutting concrete with hand tools heard a number and had to sit down.

Tace didn't sit down.

"Right. Then I want you back through that ring and I want you to go now, before anything else, because you're the only thing in this system that can tell them." She was already turning to the wall. "The children are ready. They've been ready for nine hours."

And from the row against the far wall, nineteen kids with their names on their hands were all looking at her.

On her collar, BlingBling had gone up to full gold without being asked — the only bright thing in that shed — and she was not showing off. She was lighting the ring.

---

"Who am I taking? May as well start now."

"You take Oris." Tace didn't look at the list. She hadn't needed to for two days. "Front of the row, the big one."

He stood up before she'd finished saying it. Fifteen, maybe — a head taller than the rest of them and broad across the shoulders, and the only one of the nineteen who looked too big to be carried.

Which, if you think about it for half a second, is exactly why he was first.

Tace did not explain that and would not if you asked her.

He came over and got his arms up without being told. "I know how. They made us practise on the apron." A beat. "I'm the heaviest. Sorry."

She went on his count, because Mina taught her that four days ago, and it works on a fifteen-year-old off a rock as well as it works on an eighty-one-year-old off Procyon.

Three sets. The fourth one had them.

---

The Nine.

Hot metal, nine queues, and two thousand people who had been staring at a lit ring for eleven minutes with nobody in it.

She came up out of door seven with a boy in her arms who had never been off the rock he was born on.

The hall didn't cheer. It made a noise much worse than that.

She got him down onto the deck plate and he stood up and looked at nine housings in a ring and four levels of gallery and about two thousand strangers, and he said, "...It's *warm*."

"**IT'S OPEN.**" She was shouting it over the top of him. "It works, the apron held, they dropped the bowser, there's a plate across the ring to keep the dust out — **sixty-one people and nineteen children and they're all ready and they've been ready for nine hours.**"

Calloway was already turning round.

And four hundred and six first-throughs started moving at once.
