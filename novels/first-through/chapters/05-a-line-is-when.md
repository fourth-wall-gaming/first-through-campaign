# Chapter 5 — A Line Is When

*Day one · afternoon*

She spent the afternoon doing the thing a Pathfinder does without being told to: walking a place until she could draw it. Two kilometres of cylinder, turning once every ninety seconds. Forty thousand people. Nothing grown outside it, nothing mined there, no ground within eight light years in any direction — every stone of it carried.

The decks are named for the doors, and each one smells and behaves like the place it leads. The Procyon Stair is a suburb — five light years, prams on the walkway. The Coldwater Run is red light and grain dust and the only corner of the Nine that smells of farming. The Wolf Gate queue is shift workers, the roughest line in the hall and, everyone agrees furiously, the best food on the station.

And three things she noticed that she wasn't told.

The architecture is built for five peoples, and once you see it you can't unsee it: ramps where there should be stairs, a dim corner left in every concourse, and duct grilles cut to scatter gauge on every deck — a mesh sized so a glint can pass and nothing larger can. There's a line in every budget argument about them. Somebody, centuries ago, designed this station so that the smallest people in it could go anywhere.

Second: outer decks are shuttered by default, and opening one is a small occasion. She worked out which handful are *not*.

Third, and this is the Pathfinder in her: she could now get from the hall to any deck two ways, and one of them wasn't through a concourse.

---

Which is how she ended up, near the end of it, back at the waist of the cylinder under **the Standing**.

Four metres of slate on the gallery wall above the hall, every door in human space written on it with where it currently goes. Updated whenever a courier comes in with better information. The most important object in the Causeway, and it's a *slate*, because a slate can be corrected in eleven seconds by somebody on a stool.

People stop under it without meaning to. She'd just done it.

"You're the new one. You've been walking all day. I've seen you three times."

She knew her, actually. "You were on the top rail. With the flask. You caught my last throw one-handed."

"It counted," said the girl, with enormous satisfaction, as though the matter had been in doubt all day and she had personally settled it.

She came down off the stool. Small, eleven or so, chalk to the elbow, a rag through her belt and the unmistakable bearing of someone doing a job. She wiped her hand on the rag before she offered it, which was more manners than anyone had shown Lint since she got there.

"Sennet. Everyone calls me Sen. I do the Standing on second and third watch."

She said *I do the Standing* the way an admiral says the name of a ship.

"Not the *writing*, obviously, the tallymen write. I keep it. Dust it, square the lines, chase the ones nobody's updated." A pause, slightly flinty. "I'm the one who notices when a line's gone stale. I noticed the Bell line was eleven days old and nobody else did."

She tested at nine, then. The whole family came, and she frayed like an adult, and no knack ever surfaced. That's the ordinary outcome and everybody says so beforehand and nobody quite believes it about their own. Lint didn't ask. Nobody who's been taken up ever asks.

"Do you actually know how to read it? Properly? Most of you don't. You read your own door and walk off."

"I dunno — I just try to figure it out. What's the right way, Sen?"

"Right," said Sen, and she was *delighted*, and she squared her shoulders.

"Four things on every line. People only ever read two." She tapped along the Procyon line with the flat of the chalk. "**Door. Where it goes. Who last stood it. Which watch.** That's it."

"Now. The bit nobody reads is the *fourth* one, and the fourth one is the only one that matters. Because a door tells you where it went *when somebody last looked*. That's all. The Bell line said Bell and it was eleven days stale and everybody kept reading it like it was today, and a door can stray in the time it takes you to walk down here."

She said it with real heat.

"A line isn't true. A line is *when*. If you don't read the watch you're just reading a rumour that somebody wrote up neatly."

That was better than anything the Yards taught her about the Standing.

"Huh."

"Yes, *huh*." She was extremely pleased. "Everybody says huh."

---

Then she went up two steps on the stool and reached well over to the left, to a part of the slate where the chalk had gone into the stone and the stone had gone grey around it, and she didn't touch it, and Lint got the strong impression that nobody touches it.

"And then there's that one."

> Kapteyn's — no answer this watch

"Six hundred and two," said Sen. "Spring."

She looked at it for a moment with her head tipped.

"*This watch.* That's what you write when a door doesn't answer and you expect it to answer later. It's the normal thing. I write it four or five times a year." She wiped a bit of chalk off her fingers. "Somebody wrote it that afternoon because they thought they'd be rubbing it out by the next one."

A beat.

"Nobody's ever changed it. It's the stalest line on the board by about six hundred years and it's the only one I'm not allowed to square up." She frowned at it, properly affronted, eleven years old and the keeper of this thing. "I've asked twice."

Eleven thousand people, twelve and a half light years out.

---

"Everybody shows you Kapteyn's eventually," she said, matter-of-fact, climbing back down. "The star, I mean. Up on the top gallery when the shutters are open — it's dim, you'd walk past it. They all want to be the one who shows you."

She looked up. "Has anyone done it to you yet?"

"No one. Not yet."

"You busy? Do you want to show me?"

Sen stopped with the rag halfway to her belt. "...Me?"

"You're the one who knows where it is."

There was a pause of about two seconds in which she visibly did not know what to do with her face, and then she got it under control, and then she said, far too casually:

"Well. I mean. I'd have to get Oris to sit the slate." A beat. "He's rubbish. He doesn't dust."

She was already off the stool.
