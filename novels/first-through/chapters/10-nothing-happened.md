# Chapter 10 — Nothing Happened

*Day two · still the fourth level*

"Hey Big Guy. What else? What happened to the Reach? What's a good history for that?"

Sirius took a second over it, which he does not usually do.

*That is the hardest question in the archive and I am going to tell you why before I give you anything to read, because otherwise you will think the reading is bad.*

*Nothing happened to the Reach.*

*There is no war in the record. No plague, no collapse in the accounts, no final message, no last transmission from Luna, nothing anybody can point at. The launches thinned out and then they stopped, and the reason is that there was nothing left, and the thing they had built was not going to pay out for another eighty years.*

*So the end of the greatest undertaking of the species is an **absence**, and historians hate absences, and the good ones say so and the bad ones invent a catastrophe. Be suspicious of anything that gives you a villain.*

*Read The Thinning — Ovarr, about two hundred years ago, and it is the honest one. Four hundred pages of a woman going through the launch manifests year by year, counting ships, and refusing to explain the gap she finds. The whole book is her declining to make something up. It is the best discipline you will ever see on a page.*

*And then the thing it leads to, which is the part everybody skips. Eighty-five years of nothing. They finished building and then nobody heard anything for eighty-five years — to a generation that had been told its whole life this would happen and had begun, quietly and privately, to stop believing it. Barnard's came in at a hundred and nineteen. This station at a hundred and seventy-four. Each one a lifetime apart, each one arriving to people who had only ever heard about the last one from their grandparents.*

*It is the longest-running promise anybody has ever kept, and every single person who made it was nine centuries dead before it finished being kept.*

A pause, and then the thing he volunteered, because he always does.

> *And there is one more fact in it that is not in Ovarr and is not in the speeches, and I would rather you had it from me on a bench than off somebody on a gallery in a year's time.*
>
> *Nobody is ever going to build another door.*
>
> *Not because the knowledge is lost — it is not, any engineer at the Yards can tell you how a pair is made. Because **only bodies and boxes cross a door.** No bulk, no fleet, no treasury. Twenty-eight systems can never pool what twenty-eight systems have, and one anchor pair costs more than any single system has produced in nine centuries.*
>
> *The thing that would pay for a new door cannot be gathered through the doors we already have. The network forbids its own extension, and that is not a policy anybody chose. It is the shape of the thing.*
>
> *Which means we did not build this. We inherited it, and we maintain the parts of it we can reach.*

A beat.

*Every first-through works that out in their first year. You are three days in. I would rather you heard it sitting down.*

---

"Why did they cost so much?"

*Because you cannot make one carefully.*

*An anchor pair is **entangled** — both halves made together, in the same place, at the same moment, bound at a level nothing since has been able to reach into. That is the whole of what a door is. Not a machine. A marriage.*

*And the enemy of entanglement is **everything.** Warmth. Light. Vibration. A single atom of gas touching it. Anything at all interacting with any part of that ring, at any point while it is being made, and the binding is gone and what you have is four metres of extremely expensive scrap.*

*So to make one you need a volume of the universe four metres across that is colder than anything in nature, darker than anything in nature, and absolutely still, and you need it to stay that way for the whole of the making.*

*That is the bill. Not the ring. **The silence around the ring.***

*That is what Mercury's mirrors were for. Not powering a factory — powering a refrigerator. The entire output of a planet's worth of mirrors, poured into keeping one small room quiet enough.*

*And here is the part that makes it uncollectable, which is the thing that really matters to you. You cannot build one in stages. You cannot make a third of a pair this decade and come back to it. It is one object, made in one go, and if the coherence breaks at minute nine of a two-year process you begin again with nothing.*

*So it cannot be **accumulated**. You cannot save up. There is no partial anchor anywhere in human space, because a partial anchor is a pile of metal.*

A pause, and then the number, because he always has the number.

> *They attempted four thousand, one hundred and nineteen pairs.*
>
> *Three thousand and eleven of them worked.*
>
> *A thousand and eight did not, and every one of those was a decade of somebody's whole civilisation turned into scrap, and the record of each failure is three lines long and signed, and they kept going.*
>
> *The twelfth speech is a man apologising to a hall for the four hundred and sixtieth failure. He does not resign. He asks for the money again. They give it to him.*

Sen had stopped pretending to read over Lint's arm and had her chin on her knees.

"Four hundred and sixtieth," she said. "And they *gave it to him.*"

Somewhere a long way below, the hall went on being loud about nine queues, eight of which were moving. The warm-air return ticked. BlingBling was asleep on the ledge above their heads, and second watch was still a couple of hours off.
