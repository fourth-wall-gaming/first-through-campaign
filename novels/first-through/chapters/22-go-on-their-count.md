# Chapter 22 — Go On Their Count

*Day four · the hall*

There was a line. It formed itself in about ninety seconds and it was four hundred people long and went up the service stair and round the second gallery, and Dunnel was walking it with the sheet shouting two columns at people.

Lint walked past the whole thing and got on the end of it.

Rill was about forty places ahead and saw her do it. She didn't say anything. She held up two fingers and flicked them — *let's go* — which she had definitely learned off watching Lint and BlingBling, and which she had no business knowing.

---

The nineteen took **thirty-one minutes.**

That is the thing she will remember. Eighteen more children, eighteen different first-throughs, nobody carrying twice, a glint on every third collarbone counting the sets, and Sen on her stool writing a name on the board each time one landed.

By the time the queue had moved thirty places the children were finished and she hadn't even got to the rope.

So she never got her second carry, because four hundred people got there first, and the whole hall was roaring, and Oris was sitting on a crate being handed food by strangers.

Then Dunnel's voice went flat across the hall and the noise dropped.

"That's nineteen. **Nineteen of nineteen.**"

And in the quiet after it, Calloway said the other number.

"Forty-two adults."

Nobody moved. Because every single person in that hall had done the sum by now: a child carries cheap. An adult costs the carrier a fortnight off the end of their life, and three or four in a row finishes anybody.

Forty-two.

Four hundred and six.

Calloway stepped up onto the bench inside the cordon so the whole hall could see her, and she did not dress it up.

"I am not ordering this. Anybody who wants to go home can go home and I will never raise it again."

---

Lint stood up on the bottom step of the service stair, which got her about a metre, and she could see the whole of it.

Some of them went.

Not many. Maybe thirty, out of four hundred and six — down the far stair, quietly, not looking at anybody, and nobody said a word to them and nobody ever will, because Calloway said she'd never raise it and four hundred people heard her.

Everybody else stayed where they were.

The twenty-ones came down from the upper gallery. She counted eleven of them. Every single one was somebody whose arms you could read across a hall.

Ferren shoved to the front and was told to get back twice.

Up on the wall Sen had wiped the children's column and ruled a new one and written **42** at the top of it, and she was standing on the stool rather than sitting, with the pen in her fist.

And Dunnel was at the bench with a fresh sheet, saying the same sentence over and over to a line that was not moving away:

"Name, and how many you've done today. Name, and how many you've done today."

Rill was four places from the front with her sleeves up.

---

"Lint. Four transitions. One child carry."

Dunnel didn't write it down. She looked at her.

"Two."

"...What?"

"Two today. One out, one carry." She tapped the sheet. "Yesterday was four and a bad one and a wrenched arm, and yesterday doesn't count because you slept and I'm not having the whole hall doing that sum wrong on the first night." She wrote it. *Lint — 2 — one child.*

Then, without looking up, in the same flat voice she had used on about ninety people:

"Forty-two adults. Four hundred names on this sheet. You're not going to get one, Lin."

A beat.

"Unless you want the other job."

Behind her, inside the cordon, Hesk and Vantage and the hall doctor were standing round the bench with a sheet between them, and Calloway was pointing at it.

"What's the other job?"

Dunnel nodded at the bench.

"Forty-two carries. Four hundred people who've never carried an adult, most of them doing their first one tonight, and every single one of them is going to feel it set with somebody's mother in their arms."

She finally looked up.

"Hesk says they'll average two and a half sets each. Doctor says the injuries won't come from the carrying, they'll come from the fourth second — the bit where a fifteen-year-old decides they can have it both ways."

And then she said it, and it was the thing Calloway had said to her on a deck plate four nights earlier with Mina Haral's wrist broken on the floor.

"Somebody has to stand on that plate for however long this takes and be the person who tells them to **put them down.** Out loud. Every time. And make it stick."

A beat.

"It can't be an adult. None of them would listen, and none of them know what it feels like."

Over at the bench, Calloway had stopped pointing at the sheet and was watching her.

---

"Ok. I can do that." And then, because it was obvious the moment she thought of it: "Can I ask Mina to help me? No-one's better for teaching."

Dunnel's pen stopped.

"Reader Haral can't cross."

"That's why she's perfect. You can't use her up."

---

They sent a hall child for her and she was there in six minutes, which at eighty-one with a braced wrist means somebody carried the stairs for her and she was furious about it.

Lint gave her the job in about fifteen words.

Mina Haral looked at four hundred first-throughs and a sheet with **42** on it, and did not ask a single question about the physics, or the door, or how long it would hold.

What she asked was: "How many of them have ever been carried?"

"...None of them. We're the ones who carry."

"Right." She handed her stick to the nearest teenager without looking at him. "Then not one of you has the faintest idea what it's like at the other end, and that's the whole problem, and it's been the whole problem for nine hundred years."

She got herself up onto the bench — refused help, took it anyway when Ossuary simply lifted her — and sat on it so she was at head height with the queue.

"**You.** First one. Come here and pick me up."

Rill, four places from the front, said: "Me?"

"You've got the arms for it and everybody's watching you anyway. Come on."

---

Rill picked her up and Mina was immediately, loudly, comprehensively critical.

"No. You've got me like a crate. Why have you got me like a crate?"

"Because you *are*—"

"Say that again and see what happens." She shifted deliberately against Rill's grip. "Feel that? That's me trying to help and you stopping me. I've got a hundred and sixty years of balance between us and you're fighting all of it. Let go a bit."

"If I let go you fall."

"Then I'll fall and we'll both have learned something."

Four hundred people watched the best first-through at the Nine get told off by a woman with one working wrist, and somebody up on the second gallery made a noise and got elbowed.

Rill loosened her grip about a centimetre.

"**There.** Now — you're going to go on your own count, aren't you."

"...Yeah?"

"Don't. Go on *mine*. I'm the one who can't do anything about it, so the second I'm not ready is the second it sets, and you'll never know because nobody's ever told you." She tapped Rill's shoulder with her good hand. "And talk. All the way through. Not for me — I've done forty-one of these, I'm fine. For *you*, because a frightened carrier is a stiff carrier and you need something to do with your mouth."

Rill, flatly: "I don't talk."

"Then you'd better learn in the next four minutes, hadn't you."

---

It went round the hall the way the other one had. Twenty minutes and it was a song too, and it was longer than Lint's and it was funnier, and the chorus was **GO ON THEIR COUNT**.

Dunnel's sheet said the first eight carriers were ready.

Mina settled back on the bench and jerked her chin at the plate.

"Right. You're on that side of it and I'm on this one. When one of them pushes, I'll know before you do — I'll have felt it forty-one times. You say it."

"Hey Dunnel. I want to go back. Mina's got this. She doesn't need me."

Dunnel didn't argue. She just did the thing she does, which is work out where the hole in the plan is.

"Mina's got this side. Who's got the other one?"

"...What?"

"Forty-two adults get picked *up* on an apron a hundred and ninety million kilometres from here." She tapped her sheet. "The set happens at both ends. You've got four hundred kids about to feel it with somebody's father in their arms in a shed, and the only person who can tell them to put him down is standing on a bench in *this* hall."

She wrote something.

"So yes. Go back. And do the job over there."

Then she looked up, and she said the cost out loud, because she is the one with the sheet and that is what the sheet is for.

"Mouth's been open ninety-one minutes and nobody has any idea how long it holds. If it goes while you're on that side you're on a rock with a welded roof and no shelter and the twins closing, with sixty-one people and whoever else is stuck there, and nobody's coming for a very long time."

Rill, from the front of the queue, had turned round.

"Lin."

"Ok. I've got it."

Rill didn't say anything else. She stepped out of the queue, walked her to the rope, and knocked her fist against her arm once.
