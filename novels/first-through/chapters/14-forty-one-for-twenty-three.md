# Chapter 14 — Forty-One for Twenty-Three

*Day three · morning*

Mina Haral's business took eleven minutes and was a thing of beauty.

She did not look for an office. She stood in the hall and asked the Procyon tallyman who currently keeps the residency sheet, and he told her, and she had him send a runner rather than going herself — "I am eighty-one and I have just been carried eight light years, Mr Orsel, I am not walking to the fourth level." A clerk arrived at a trot. She signed two things, declined to sign a third until it was read to her properly, and got a berth assignment out of a man who was clearly intending to say there were none.

"Right. Done." She put the pen down. "And you've been very patient and I have watched you look over at that queue about nine times. Go on. I'll find Nettie."

---

Up close it was worse than it had looked from the stair.

The queue was thirty-odd people and it was not a queue so much as a crowd that had stopped pretending. Coldwater men with crates on their shoulders. A Wolf Gate shift steward in orange talking quietly and continuously to nobody in particular. Two first-throughs in weaves, sitting on a stanchion base, waiting.

And the housing was *lit*. Four metres across, perfectly ordinary, exactly like the eight others.

What was happening was this, over and over.

A first-through steps up to the ring. Gives it her weight.

Nothing.

She comes off. Shakes her hands out. Goes again — and this time she stands there, on a lit plate, in front of thirty people, for a length of time that you could *watch*.

And then she's gone, and the queue shuffles, and the next one steps up.

That is the thing you cannot un-see once you have seen it. Yesterday the loaders said *give it a second and it has you*, and that was folklore, the kind of thing every deck says about some door. Today she was standing four metres from a ring watching a nineteen-year-old stand on a plate long enough that there was time to look at her boots.

Somebody behind her in the queue said *come on*, under their breath, to nobody.

---

The tallyman at the head of the line was a man of about forty with a sheet on a board, and he was not taking names.

He was making marks. A short stroke each time somebody stepped up, and then a longer one when they went through, and the gap between the two was a space he was leaving on the sheet, and the spaces were not all the same width.

Nobody had told him to do that. He was a tallyman. Tallying is what he does.

He had about forty of them down the page.

---

She asked the Old Man, on the pin, whether there was anything a first-through had that could feel whether a door was open and safe.

And the answer was specific, and it was no.

*The knack you are thinking of is the Listen, and you have not got it. You can tell something about the far side before you step through — pressure, heat, whether there is air, whether there is a floor, whether anybody is standing there. It is never detailed. It is the single most valuable thing a first-through can have and it is the reason any of them survive a door that has moved. There are two or three in every intake. There was one in yours and she went to Barnard's.*

A pause, and then the thing he volunteered.

*But understand what you are standing in front of. **Every human knack reads the far side.** That is what they are all for. Not one of them reads the **near** side, because for nine hundred and seventy-five years there has never been anything on the near side to know. A door is either where it is or it has strayed, and the only thing it does is open.*

*So you are standing in a hall full of first-throughs and there is nothing in any of them that can read a lit ring.*

In the queue, the two in weaves on the stanchion base were watching the plate with the flat expression of people whose turn is coming.

The tallyman made another mark.

"Big man." She said it quiet, pin only, matching him. "Get someone to seven with the Listen. Something's up."

---

*Right. Two things, and the first one is that it will not do what you think.*

Fast and quiet and straight back at her.

*The Listen reads the far side. It will not tell you why seven is sticky.*

A beat.

*It will tell you whether the far side is still the far side. Which is a live question, is not being asked by anybody in that hall, and is worth considerably more than it sounds.*

*Dray Enns. Seventeen, four years on the rota, works the Cold Doors and Procyon, has the Listen and is quiet about it the way they usually are. He is asleep in bunkroom six on the third level and came off a late. I cannot order him and I have no standing to ask him. You can ask him, and he can say no, and if he says no that is the end of it.*

*And the second thing you did not ask for.*

Of course.

*The tallyman at the head of that queue is called Pell Ivor and he has been on seven for eleven years, and he is making marks on a board. A short stroke when somebody steps up, a long one when they go through, and the gap between them left open. He has about forty of them down the page. Nobody told him to. He is a tallyman and tallying is what he does, and it is the most valuable object in that hall by a considerable margin.*

*And I cannot read it.*

A pause, and it cost him something to say.

*I have cameras in that hall and I have had them for nine centuries. He holds the board against his chest at an angle, the way everybody does, because it is his sheet. I can see that there are marks. I cannot see the spacing.*

*The only way anybody gets that sheet is for a person to walk up and ask a man for it.*

---

"Oi. Pell Ivor! How many attempts?"

He looked up like a man who had been shouted at in a hall he had worked for eleven years, which he had.

Then he looked at the Threshold flash on her weave, and the question got through to him, and something shifted.

"...Attempts."

"Yeah. Not crossings. **Attempts.**"

He turned the board round.

"Since the watch turned." He ran a thumb down the page. "**Forty-one step-ups. Twenty-three through.**"

Forty-one for twenty-three.

"Nearly two goes each."

"Nearly two goes each." He tapped a column. "And that's not the number that bothers me. Week last Tuesday I'd have had twenty-three for twenty-three and I'd not have written anything down, because it's one for one, because it's *always* one for one." He shrugged, embarrassed. "Started keeping it on the Friday. Didn't tell anybody. It's not a fault, is it. Nobody logs a sticky door."

"What's the gaps for?"

"The waiting." He said it like it was obvious, and then heard himself. "How long they stand there. I leave a space the size of it."

And he has no clock. He has been drawing the length of a pause on a sheet of paper, by feel, forty-one times, because he is a tallyman and that is what tallying is.

---

Then her pin, quietly.

*I have been timing it off the deck camera since you told me something was up, which is four minutes ago, and I have five.*

*Two point one. Two point four. Two point two. Two point six. Two point four.*

*Seconds, on the near side, standing in a lit housing before it takes them. I can measure this directly and perfectly — it is inside my hull, in front of a camera I have had for nine hundred years, and I have never had any reason to point one at a plate because a door opens instantly.*

*Mr Ivor's sheet goes back to Friday. Mine goes back four minutes.*

A pause.

*His is worth more than mine.*

Pell Ivor was still holding the board out, and he had gone slightly pale, because he had just heard half of that off her face.

"Is that — am I in trouble? For not logging it?"

Behind him somebody stepped up to the ring, and gave it her weight, and stood there.

And now that she had a number in her head she counted it, and it was a long way past two.
