# Chapter 20 — A Bowser on the Ring

*Day three · the last of it*

Four hours went.

Not quietly. The Nine turned into something she had not seen it be: Dunnel's two columns became a rota, the growing decks gave up two hundred mattresses, somebody turned the Wolf Gate mess into a receiving hall because it was nearest, and the counting song went round the gallery until a hall child started a second one about Ferren that he pretended not to hear.

Every watch, somebody who had crossed seven stood the plate and found out whether it still took. It did. Five attempts. Then six. Then six.

The sheet taped to the deck said **3.3**.

---

And at the sixth hour a Wolf Gate courier came up out of the housing with his boots in his hand and Vey's handwriting folded in his chest seal, and the hall stopped.

Calloway read it and her face did something.

Then she read it out, because four hundred people were standing there.

> *Ladder answered in twenty-two minutes. Sixty-one accounted for, all well, all told.*
>
> *They have found the shed.*
>
> *It has been their dry store for a hundred and ninety years. There is a fuel bowser parked on the apron that has not moved since before anybody living was born and has sunk eleven centimetres into the concrete.*
>
> ***The ring is underneath it.***
>
> *They have thirteen hours and forty-one people who can lift. Tell me what you want them to do.*

Four hundred first-throughs, a station director, an engineer, a fathom and a reader, and nobody said anything for about three seconds.

Then Hesk, from somewhere near the back:

"...Is there a *drain* under that apron?"

---

"Drain?" Calloway rounded on him.

"It's a survey post. They wash things down. If there's a drain run under that apron then the slab's undermined and that's *why* it's sunk eleven centimetres — it's not weight, it's a void." Hesk had his wrench out again without noticing. "And if it's a void I'm not having forty-one people lift a bowser off a ring. You don't lift a bowser. You drain it, you cut the lip of the dish it's made, and you *roll* it."

"Can you tell them that from here?"

"I can tell them that from here and it'll be wrong, because I've never seen the thing." He had gone pale. "Director, I need to know whether that's a drain or a sinkhole and whether the ring's taken the load, and the only way I find out is somebody asks them the right questions and brings the answers back."

And then everybody in that hall worked out the shape of it at the same time.

Ladder can only be reached by radio, through Vey. Twenty-two minutes each way. And Vey can only be reached by a person walking through door seven.

Which was now taking six attempts and ninety seconds and reading **3.3**.

Hesk needed three round trips. Maybe four.

---

Calloway didn't look at Lint. She looked at the four names in Dunnel's first column.

"Hesk, write your questions. Short. Numbered."

He was already writing on the back of his own hand.

Rill was pulling her sleeves down.

Six attempts. Then eight. Then she lost count, because Dunnel was counting for her and had got to eleven.

It would not have her.

She came off the plate with her arms shaking and Hesk's numbered questions still folded in her chest seal, and the sheet on the deck said 3.3 and Dunnel was writing **3.6** underneath it.

Rill had her sleeves down and was standing at the edge of the ring.

Lint stepped off and handed her the questions.

Rill took them, and didn't say anything, and didn't make it a moment.

She stepped up.

Third attempt. She eased off it once and it had her, and the hall made a noise that was mostly relief and partly something else, because everybody had seen whose arms those were.

---

Fifty-one minutes.

She came back with Vey's answer and Ladder's answer under it, and she was grey around the mouth, and she gave the sheet to Hesk and not to Calloway.

Hesk read it standing up.

Then he sat down on the bench inside the cordon, which nobody had seen him do.

"It's a drain," he said. "It's a drain and it's been leaking into that fill for a century and the slab's got a void under one corner the size of a bunk." He looked up. "They can't roll it. They roll it and it goes *through*."

Dunnel: "Then they lift it."

"Forty-one people can't lift a bowser."

Rill, sitting on the deck plate with her back against the stanchion, not looking at anybody: "They could drop it."

"Drop it." Hesk was on his feet again. "Say that again."

"The hole's under the corner. So stop fighting it." Rill didn't get up. "Cut the slab round the bowser, not under the ring. Let the corner go. It drops into its own hole and sits in a pit, and the apron the ring's in stays where it is."

Hesk stared at her.

"...How long have you been thinking about that?"

"Since you said void."

He was already writing on the bench. "Needs the cut line two metres clear of the ring minimum, needs them to know which corner, and needs somebody to tell me how deep that void is or they'll take the ring down with it." He stopped. "Three questions. One trip."

---

Dunnel, quietly: "Director. Fifth attempt failed on the last four people who tried. Including two off the list."

The sheet on the deck said **4.1**.

Calloway looked at it for a long moment.

"Then this is the last one we get. Hesk — three questions, and make them the right three, because whatever comes back is what those sixty-one people are doing without help after that."

Six hours and ten minutes.

Rill was still sitting against the stanchion and had not got her colour back. Ferren was awake and in the hall with his boots on and had crossed seven once that day. Lint had a sheet of three questions nobody else could carry.

"I go."

Ossuary sealed her. Hesk put the three questions in her chest seal himself.

Nine attempts. Eleven. Dunnel stopped counting out loud because it wasn't helping.

Twice it *nearly* had her — that half-instant where the weight goes and you're already moving — and both times it came back solid and put her on her heels.

Ossuary's hand was on her arm and he hadn't pulled yet.

Six hours four minutes. Three questions in her chest seal. Sixty-one people and a bowser on a ring.

She went again.

The thirteenth attempt was the one where it nearly had her, and she was already committed — weight gone, shoulder through where the air changes — when the slope swung and it came back **solid** against her left side.

It didn't throw her. It just stopped being soft, with her arm inside it.

She heard something before she felt it.

Ossuary had her off the plate before her knees finished going.

It wasn't much. That was the strange thing — a wrench, the sort of thing you'd get off a badly-stacked crate. She could move the fingers. It just didn't want to go above her shoulder, and it was *cold* in a way that is not like being cold.

Nine couriers on the Wolf Gate deck have had a version of this all spring and nobody wrote it up.

Hesk's three questions were still folded in her chest seal.

---

Five hours fifty. The sheet on the deck said 4.1 and nobody was going to write another number under it, because nobody was going to get a reading.

The doctor was coming down the gallery stair with a bag.

Calloway crouched down in front of her on the deck plate, which put her below her, which she had not done for anybody all day.

"Lin. Look at me. Did it take you at all? Any part of it, any instant?"

"I got nothing. Nothing at all."

Calloway closed her eyes for a second.

"Right."

She stood up. "Ferren."

---

Ferren went eleven attempts and got nothing. Rill shoved her sleeves up and went fourteen and got nothing, and had to be helped off, and was furious about being helped off.

Two more after that, off Dunnel's second column, who had never felt a set before and now had.

By the ninth hour the hall had stopped sending people up. Not on an order. People just stopped.

Hesk's three questions came out of her chest seal and went into a drawer in the ready room, unsent, and nobody said anything about it, and that was the worst thing that happened all day.

---

Three hours twelve.

The hall didn't empty. Four hundred first-throughs and about two thousand other people sat down on the deck plates and the gallery stairs and waited, because there was nothing else to do and nobody wanted to not be there.

Sen stayed on her stool.

At three hours and four minutes a Coldwater courier called Nim — nineteen, second column, who had been quietly standing the plate every twenty minutes for two hours without being asked — stepped up again.

And door seven went.

No sound. No flash. The lit ring simply stopped being lit, between one of her breaths and the next, with both her feet on the plate.

She was standing on cold metal.

For about four seconds nobody in that hall breathed, and then Nim said, in a very small voice, "...I'm fine. I'm fine, I'm — there's nothing. It's just *off*."

---

Bunk nineteen. Boots off, weave in its tray, left arm not going above the shoulder.

BlingBling came in off the rail and dimmed right down on the pillow — the sleeping colour, which she only does there.

Somewhere four levels down, four hundred people were sitting on deck plates in front of a cold ring, and most of them weren't going anywhere either.
