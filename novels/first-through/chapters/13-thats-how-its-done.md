# Chapter 13 — That's How It's Done

*Day three · dawn*

She woke on the cable trunk with a cushion under her hip and a crick in her neck that was going to be with her until lunch. The sheet had slid onto the deck. Page a hundred and ten, still.

Today was three days.

She had said three days to an eighty-one-year-old woman sitting on a Procyon deck with a broken wrist, and the woman had said *come back for me*, and Calloway had left the run on the rota without saying anything about it.

Wash. Feed the weave. Boots — the Procyon one and the Nine one, side by side, which is two stars and is nothing much yet.

The hall at that hour was the quiet end of the night shift turning over into the early one. Nine queues. Eight of them moving the way they always move.

And **door seven** had a line of about twenty people in it, which is a lot for that hour, standing about rather than moving, and a tallyman at the head of it with his arms folded.

As she came past, the older of the two Wolf Gate loaders from the day before caught her eye and tipped his head at the queue with the long-suffering cheerfulness of a man whose morning was already annoying.

"Sticky," he said.

And went back to waiting.

---

The Procyon Stair was three along, running clean, and the tallyman there knew her name and asked after Ossuary. She stepped up and it took her instantly — the way a door is supposed to, the way all thirty-four of hers had, step and you're there and the only reason you know is that the air changed.

Mina Haral was sitting up in a chair, dressed, with her left wrist in a moulded brace and a bag already packed at her feet, and she had very obviously been ready since about six.

She saw Lint in the doorway and her whole face went.

"*Three days.*"

---

She told her in about ninety seconds, as an afterthought, because to her it was one. Nobody had ever put the question to the cargo.

**Don't hold her still.** She's got a hundred and sixty years of balance between the two of them and Lint would be fighting hers. **Go on her count, not yours** — because she is the one who can't do anything about it, so the moment she isn't ready is the moment it sets. And **talk the whole way**, not to settle her, to settle *you*, because a frightened carrier is a stiff carrier, and she has spent forty-one crossings being a conversation so some child had something to do with their mouth.

Then the condition, and she was immovable about it.

"If it sets, you put me down. Not ease off. **Put me down**, on whichever side we're on, and let go, and don't spend four seconds being brave at me." She tapped the chair arm. "Say it."

Lint said it.

"Good. Right." She got her good arm up over Lint's shoulder. "The ceiling in this hall is a disgrace and has been for thirty years—"

And the air changed.

"— and the *second* motion was about the ceiling and it failed because by then everybody was bored of ceilings, which is the entire history of public works in this system—"

It didn't set. Not once. Not even the pull.

Because she moved with Lint, and she went on her own count, and she has been doing half this job since before Lint was born.

---

The Nine. Hot metal, nine queues, the smell of home.

Lint came up out of the Procyon housing with an eighty-one-year-old woman against her chest still talking about ceilings, and put her down carefully on the deck plate, and Mina stood — on her own, with her good hand on Lint's shoulder for about four seconds and then not even that.

"There," she said. "*That's* how it's done."

Then she looked round at a hall she was last standing in at twenty-two, and stopped talking for the first time in four minutes.

The tallyman on the Procyon Stair had gone very still. Two of the queue had put their bags down.

Mina Haral — who taught half the readers in human space, who was turned back in this hall at twenty-two, who has paid children forty-one times to bring her home — was standing at the Nine on her own two feet with a brace on and her bag at her side.

BlingBling came off Lint's collar and went up and hung above the two of them doing something extravagant and gold that she had no business doing in a working anchor hall, and nobody told her to stop.

Mina got her breath back, and squared her shoulders, and said:

"Right. Where do I sign? Because somebody is going to want a form and I would like to be the one who finds them rather than the other way round."

---

Over by the cordon rope at door seven, the queue had got longer.
