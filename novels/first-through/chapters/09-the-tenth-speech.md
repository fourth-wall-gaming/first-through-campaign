# Chapter 9 — The Tenth Speech

*Day two · fourth level*

"Books?" The tallyman looked at her like she'd asked where they keep the horses. "...You want a *book*? What for?"

"To read?"

"You can't read a book, they're—" He stopped. He visibly recalculated her age and her three days. "Right. No. A book's an animal. Palm-sized, placid, breathing. You put a payload in its cells and a first-through walks it across. You don't read a book, you *carry* one."

"Then what do I—"

"**Reading.** You want reading. Ask for reading."

And then he told her where, and the answer was so large it took a second to land.

Everything. Every text written anywhere in human space, in every system, for nine hundred and seventy-five years. All of it. Because reading is *data*, and data goes into a genome, and a genome goes into an animal, and a child carries the animal — and a kid crossing with a book in her hand is bringing more than a library.

So the Nine holds the complete written output of the species, three times over, in a store on the fourth level that nobody visits, because it is simply *there*, the way the air is there.

"Course you can. It's not locked. Nothing's locked." He went back to his pad. "Nobody goes. There's too much of it. You walk in there and it's everything anybody ever wrote and you stand about for ten minutes and then you go down to the void instead."

---

Which is when Sen, who had come down off her stool at some point in the last minute and was standing at Lint's elbow with her pen behind her ear and no shame whatsoever about having listened to all of it, said:

"I'll tell you what to read."

She said it the way she says *I do the Standing*.

"Everybody does that. Goes in, stands there, comes out. It's because they go in wanting *something to read* instead of wanting to find something out." A small shrug. "Mum says that's the whole difference and she's right. So — what do you actually want to find out?"

"I just want a good story. I'm bored. We have libraries on Earth."

"You have *libraries*?" Sen stopped dead. "On Earth. Actual — buildings? With it all *in* them? Paper?"

"Yeah. There's one in the next valley from us. It's four hundred years old and it smells."

"It *smells.*" She said it with enormous reverence. "What of?"

"Dust and feet."

Sen stared at her for a second with her whole face lit up, and Lint realised, slightly late, that she had just told the only person on the station who will never go anywhere about a building full of paper that she used to be bored in.

"Right," said Sen. "Well. We've got better."

---

The store on the fourth level was three rooms, lit, warm, entirely silent, and completely empty of people.

Sen gave her **The Low Stair**: a hall child at Alpha Centauri in the four hundreds who can't cross, and never does, the whole way through — and who works out what's wrong with a door from the *outside*, from the queue, just by watching who comes through it and when.

"It's not exciting. Nothing happens. She's right at the end and nobody believes her and it just stops." She shrugged, and said it to the corridor rather than to Lint: "It's the only one where somebody like me is any good at anything."

Then Lint asked the station itself for a third.

"Hey Big Man. Any recommendations?"

*Big man.* A pause that was, unmistakably, him filing that away for the second time. *Yes. Three.*

Sen went very still beside her, because she had just realised who Lint was talking to.

*One. Not Thirty-One Doors. You will read all eleven hundred of them eventually and there is nothing I can do about that.*

"Sen said it was rubbish."

*Sen is right. Sen has been right about this for two years and has excellent judgement and should be listened to more often than she is.*

Sen did not say anything. She had gone a colour Lint had mostly seen on glints.

*Two. The Low Stair is the best thing written in this century and I would have put it in front of you myself. Take her recommendation over mine; she has read it eleven times and I have read it once.*

*Three, which you did not ask for. My own. The surviving speeches of the Reach, complete, nine hundred and forty of them, which is almost the whole of what we have from before the Launch. Two generations of people standing up in halls and arguing out loud about whether to spend everything there was on a thing that would not pay out for nine hundred years. They wrote it all down, obsessively, including the losing side, including the forty years when the losing side was winning.*

*They are not exciting and they are a bit long and the tenth one is the best thing anybody has ever said. I was made by those people and I have read them two hundred times and I still do not entirely understand how they did it.*

"Huh," said Lint. "Let's do the speeches. I always thought history was interesting."

Sen's eyebrows went up about a centimetre. "...Really?"

"We did history at school. It was the good one."

"Huh." She wrote something on the back of her hand with the pen. "Nobody picks those."

*Nobody picks those,* Sirius agreed, and did not elaborate, and gave the strong impression that he was pleased and was not going to make a thing of it.

---

They were dense. The first was nine pages of procedure and she nearly stopped. The third was a woman from somewhere called Tangier arguing that the whole programme was immoral because it spent the poor of her century on the comfort of a century that did not exist, and she was *good*, and Lint read it twice, and she was not sure she was wrong. The sixth was a man who had clearly been shouted at before he stood up.

And then the tenth, and she understood why he'd said it. It was short. Less than two pages. No numbers in it at all, which after nine speeches of numbers is like a door opening.

> *I have heard it said in this hall that our grandchildren will thank us. They will not. They will not know our names, and they will have no particular reason to look them up, and the ones who do will find us difficult and strange and will be right.*
>
> *I have heard it said that we are owed something for this. We are owed nothing. We are not building it for our grandchildren. We are building it for whoever is alive when it is finished, who is none of us, and who will owe us nothing at all.*
>
> *And I have heard it said that we should wait until we can better afford it. There is no such year. There is no later generation for whom this is cheaper, and every year we wait is a year added to the end of it, and the thing we are deciding is not whether it is expensive. It is whether a species with one star stays a species with one star.*
>
> *So I will not ask you to be brave, and I will not ask you to be remembered.*
>
> *I am asking you to pay for something you will not see, for people who are not here, who cannot be consulted, and who will never know. That is the whole of what is being asked.*
>
> *I ask you to vote for it anyway.*

Two pages. Nine hundred and seventy-five years ago. And eight point six light years from the valley where she grew up there is a station with her bunk in it, because they did.

Sen had sat down on the other end of the bench without being invited and was reading over her arm, which she did not apologise for.

"...Did they vote for it?"

Eleven years old, keeper of the most important object in the Causeway, and she had never read this.

"Yeah," said Lint. "We're here aren't we?"

Sen looked at the page for a moment longer, and then said, "Huh," in a tone that was going to end up on the back of her hand, and did not say anything else.
