# Chapter 23 — Four Hundred for the Night

*Day four · the apron at Ladder*

Dust and bad orange light and the cut edge of the slab. Tace Orun had got the plate off the ring and two people with brooms keeping the grit down.

Forty-two adults on that rock, and Tace had them in an order. Thirty-four of them were in a line along the wall under the lamp halls, and the other seven were still working, and not one person was arguing about which they were.

Lint told her what she was there for in one sentence and she got it immediately, which is what two days of thinking about one problem does to a person.

"Right. You stand there." She pointed at a spot a metre from the ring where Lint could see a carrier's face and reach their shoulder. "And you say it loud and you say it to the *carrier*, not to mine. Mine'll do what they're told."

By the shed door there was a needle on the wall in a brass case, and a ledger hanging off a nail beside it on a loop of wire, swollen with damp and written in a lot of different hands.

---

Then the relay started and it went on for a very long time.

It works like this. A first-through comes up out of the ring, blinking at the orange light. Tace puts the next one in front of them. They pick them up — badly, usually, first time — and Lint is talking before they've finished: *go on their count, not yours, and if it sets you put them down.*

About a third of them set. She called four of those off the plate in the first hour.

Two of the four cried about it afterwards, and both of them went back in the queue.

Nobody was hurt. Nobody.

Eleven. Then nineteen. Sen's counting was happening eight point six light years away and Lint couldn't see any of it, and somebody told her the number every time they came through.

---

At twenty-six, Rill came up out of the ring.

And she didn't wait for Tace.

She walked past the front of the line — past a woman who had been standing there for forty minutes — and stopped in front of a man about halfway down, and picked him up.

Tace said, "That's not the order."

Rill said, "I know."

"Rill — now's not the time. Seriously."

Rill didn't put him down and didn't look away.

"Raske's paying me four hundred for this one."

That was it. That was the whole answer, and it was true, and she gave it flat in front of Tace Orun and a line of thirty-odd people and the man in her arms, who had the decency to look at the floor.

Then she changed the subject, the way she always does.

"Number's at two point nine on the Nine side. It's climbing again." She shifted her grip. "Tell Tace to start putting the heavy ones first while it's still cheap."

And she stepped onto the apron and went.

Lint caught her eye as she did it. She didn't say anything. She just held it, and shook her head once, slowly.

Rill's face did something she couldn't stop, and then she was gone, and the man went with her.

The woman at the front of the line watched the whole thing and said nothing at all.

Tace Orun said nothing.

Nobody in that shed said one word about it, and the relay did not stop, because the next first-through was already coming up out of the ring and there were people left and a number that was climbing.

Tace put the next one in front of the new arrival.

"Go on their count," Lint heard herself say. "And if it sets, you put them down."

---

Thirty-one. Thirty-three.

It got worse as it went, and not because anybody got tired. Because the number was climbing on the Nine side and the carriers were arriving having fought for it, and a kid who has just spent ninety seconds being refused by a door is a kid whose arms are already shaking when Tace puts somebody's grandmother in them.

She called five more off the plate in twenty minutes.

One of them — Wolf Gate, about sixteen, she never got her name — argued with her. Properly argued, in front of everybody, with an old man in her arms.

She said it again and the girl put him down and sat on the concrete with her head in her hands, and the old man patted her shoulder and got back in the line.

Then a long gap. Four minutes, nothing, and everybody in that shed looked at the ring.

And then a gap that went on for nine minutes, and Tace Orun came and stood next to her without saying anything, and there was one woman left in the line.

---

"How many first-throughs are still here?"

Tace had been counting. Of course she had.

"Four of you."

She nodded along the shed. Two sitting against the wall by the lamp hall doors — the Wolf Gate girl who had argued, and a boy who had set three times and hadn't tried to go back yet. One more up by the cut edge of the slab, standing, watching the ring, who had come through eleven minutes ago and had not managed to go the other way since.

And her.

"Three of yours got stuck on this side in the last half hour." Tace said it without any particular weight. "Two of them have tried going back empty. Couldn't."

BlingBling had been dark on her collar for twenty minutes, which she had not done since the night the wrist broke. Then she came up — not gold. A hard, flat white Lint had never seen on her.

---

And then the needle in the brass case by the door moved, and Tace Orun looked at it for about a second and a half.

"Front coming." She said it at a normal volume to the whole shed, which was how Lint knew it was serious. "**Pit. Now.**"

Nobody argued. Nobody asked her how she knew. Thirty-odd people who had lived under that sky their whole lives simply started moving, and the ones who didn't know where to go looked at her.

"Tace — what are your orders?"

She answered in about two seconds, because she had had two days and this is the part she is actually good at.

"The pit." She was already pointing. "Four metres of rock between you and the sky and it's the deepest hole on this rock and we dug it this morning. Round the bowser, under the lip, backs to the wall. It'll take all of us and it'll be filthy."

To her own people: "Lamp halls go dark, doors shut, *nobody* in the open. Move."

Then to Lint, flat:

"Your three come with us. They're not crossing anything in four minutes and I'm not having children stood on an apron under that."

And she did not give an order about the last one in the line.

The woman was sixty-odd. She had been standing there for an hour and ten minutes and she had not sat down, and the ring was lit two metres away, and there was nobody on that side who could carry her through it except Lint.

"Ok. Take her through." And then, to the shed: "I'm coming back."

---

She had watched thirty-three carries from four metres away for two hours. She was the best-prepared passenger on that rock and she got her arms up before she was asked.

"On my count," she said. "I've been listening."

Three sets. Four. It was flat and total and it had nobody.

Behind her Tace was shouting people down into the pit and the Wolf Gate girl was arguing about leaving her and being dragged.

The woman in her arms said, "Put me down, love."

"No," Lint told her, and went again.

Fifth set, and she said *now* on her own count, and it had them both.

---

The Nine. Noise like a wall. Two thousand people, Sen on her stool with the pen already moving, Mina Haral on the bench with both hands flat on her knees.

She put the woman down on the deck plate and she stood, and Dunnel's voice went up over everything:

"**That's the line. That's all of them out of the line.**"

The hall came apart.

And she was already turning round, because there were three first-throughs and thirty-odd people from Ladder going into a hole in the ground, and she had said she would come back.

Rill was four metres away and got to her first.

"Don't." She had her arm. "Lin, that's a flare and no shelter and your skin's cold-and-vacuum—"

"Tace is putting them in the pit. Four metres of rock. My three are down there and I said I'd come back."

Calloway, from inside the cordon, had already heard all of it.

She didn't argue. She looked at her for about a second and then stepped back off the plate, which is how she says yes.

Rill let go of her arm and swore.

Ossuary checked her seals in eleven seconds flat.

It set. Set again. Set a third time and a fourth, and BlingBling was flashing the number onto her collarbone in a hard white she could see through her own weave, and it was not getting smaller.

Rill had hold of the back of her weave and had not pulled yet.

Then her hand came off, because there was nothing left to hold.

---

Dust. Orange light. An empty shed with the lamp halls dark and the doors shut and nobody in it.

Four metres to the cut edge and then down — and the pit is not a pit from the inside, it is a raw concrete wound with a fuel bowser nose-down in the middle of it and people pressed round it under the lip with their backs to the rock.

Tace Orun's hand came up out of the dark and hauled her in by the shoulder of the weave and somebody else got her legs, and the Wolf Gate girl was shouting something that was mostly swearing.

She ended up jammed between a boy of about nine and the cold curve of the bowser with four metres of rock over her head.

BlingBling went out entirely. Dark, still, flat against her collarbone, which her own people will tell you she can do and nobody who has met her believes.

Tace, in the dark, to nobody: "Heads down. Count of forty."

Somebody started counting out loud and about ten people joined in.

---

"How many more people? I thought that was all the kids and the adults."

Tace answered between numbers, because she had the only accurate list on that rock and she had had it in her hand for two days.

"**Eleven.**"

Her, and seven of hers who had stayed back on purpose — the broom crew keeping grit out of the ring, the two who shut the lamp halls, the woman who worked the cutting torch — and Lint's three.

"Thirty-four went out of that line. I'm the sixty-first name on my own board and I'm not going till my shed's empty." She was counting with everybody else and talking in the gaps. "That's how it works. Foreman's last."

The boy of nine jammed against her shoulder said, "And me, because I hid."

Tace, flatly, without heat: "And him, because he hid, and we will be discussing it."

So the row against the far wall had been eighteen, and the hall had called nineteen of nineteen.

Twenty-two. Twenty-three.

---

Thirty-one. Thirty-four.

Nothing happened. That is the part nobody tells you about a flare from under rock — there is no bang and no light and no heat. The air in the pit goes very slightly strange and your teeth ache, and somewhere above you, through four metres of stone, something you cannot perceive at all is scouring the apron you were standing on ninety seconds ago.

Forty.

Tace kept counting past forty to sixty, because she has done this her whole life and forty is for children.

Then BlingBling came up off her collarbone — a single short green — and about two seconds later Tace said, "That's it," and eleven people started swearing and laughing at the same time.

---

She climbed out into a shed that looked exactly the same.

The ring was still lit. And here is where the sum stops working.

Eleven people. She had one crossing left in her and she knew it the way you know you're hungry — one, maybe, and the second would be the kind you don't come back from properly.

One carry gets one person out.

"Take one of the adults."

Tace shook her head before she'd finished.

"Take the boy."

He was nine and he was standing right there and he knew exactly what was being discussed.

"A child costs you minutes. One of mine costs you a fortnight off the end of your life and gets *one* person off this rock." She said it with no softening at all. "You've got one in you. Spend it on the cheap one and come back tomorrow with arms."

Then, because she is the foreman and it is her rock:

"And it isn't going to be me. My shed's not empty."

---

The boy's name turned out to be Teo and he did not argue, which told her he knew he'd lose.

"Arms round. On my count, not yours. Talk to me the whole way — doesn't matter what about."

"I hid in the bowser pit yesterday too," he said. "There's a gap under the tank."

It set. Set. Set again — and she felt the thing Mina had warned her about, which was not the door at all. It was her own hands. On the fifth set her grip was wrong and she knew it before it even went flat, and Teo said, patiently, "You've stopped talking."

Sixth crossing of the day. Nobody has ever done six.

Seventh set. He said *now* on his own count, which nobody taught him and everybody on that rock had been watching for two hours, and it had them both.

---

The Nine hit her like a wall again and this time her legs didn't love it. She put Teo down on the plate and he stood there looking at two thousand people and a ceiling and said, quietly, "It's *inside*."

Sen's pen moved.

Dunnel took the number off her sheet and said it out loud, and then said the other thing, which was that there were eleven people still on that rock and the door was reading three point six.
