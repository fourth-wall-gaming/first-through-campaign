# Storytelling and plotting in this campaign

Written after session 1 of the restart was stopped by the player with the words
*THIS IS BORING. Who is the antagonist?* The diagnosis and the fix, so that the
next session does not need to be stopped the same way.

## What went wrong, precisely

Five scenes were run: a kettle argument, a standing, a conversation with the
station mind, a quiet errand to a twelve-person hall, and a maintenance
statistic. Every one of them was competently written and the whole sequence was
inert.

**The antagonist was a maintenance schedule.** Nothing wanted anything. Nobody
in any scene was trying to do something bad, or indeed anything at all. The
player had not met a single person who posed a risk, and nothing had been taken
from him.

`TABLE.md` §2b already describes this failure and names its symptoms — a run of
scenes that each end in somebody agreeing, an antagonist who is a timetable, no
losses. The rules existed. They were read at the top of the session. They were
not applied, because a warm scene feels like good work while you are writing it.

## The three questions, before any scene is narrated

1. **Who in this scene wants something that costs the player something?** If the
   answer is nobody, it is not a scene, it is a transition. Cut it or put
   somebody in it.
2. **What is the antagonist doing right now, while this is happening?** Not what
   they will do. What they are doing today, on a clock, whether or not the player
   is looking.
3. **What does this stretch of play take away?** Not a setback. A loss that does
   not come back. If nothing leaves, nothing happened.

## Ordinary world is not the same as no pressure

Act I is designed to open light so the player loves the place before it is
threatened. That is correct. **It does not mean nothing is hunting.**

The mistake was reading "light" as "safe". The fix: the antagonists act from
the first hour, offscreen, on clocks, and the player sees the *consequences*
before they see the people. A kettle argument is fine. A kettle argument in a
house where somebody has already decided to take something from these children
is a different kettle argument, and it is the GM who knows the difference and
the GM whose job it is to make that knowledge land in the room.

## Plot with sequences, not with beats

A beat is a unit of catalog. A **sequence** is a unit of drama, and this
campaign has been short of them. Every sequence gets four lines written down
before it is run:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Question** | the dramatic question, answerable yes or no, which this sequence exists to answer |
| **Who moves first** | the antagonist, almost always, and what they do |
| **Reversal** | the thing that makes the obvious answer wrong halfway through |
| **Cost** | what the player loses whichever way it goes |

If you cannot fill in **Cost**, you are writing a transition and should cut to
the next sequence.

## The antagonist must be able to win and must act first

- **The antagonist moves before the player knows there is a game.** By the time
  the player is told anything, two things have already happened.
- **Every antagonist answers within a watch**, as triggers and not as schedule:
  *when the player does this, he does that*, fired while they are still in the
  room.
- **Violence has to be able to win.** If the armed faction has never hurt
  anybody the player cares about, nobody is frightened of it. Escalate by rungs
  and never skip one.
- **At the crisis the allies are unavailable**, for reasons established earlier.

## Build the antagonist out of the player's character sheet

This is the part that was missed hardest. An antagonist chosen for the setting
produces a plot. An antagonist chosen for the **protagonist** produces a story.

The method:

1. **Name the character's engine** — the one sentence that makes them dramatic.
   Not their skills. The contradiction they walk around with.
2. **Find the asset that engine depends on.** Usually one thing.
3. **Build somebody whose ordinary daily business destroys that asset** — not
   somebody who hates them. Somebody for whom ruining them is Tuesday.
4. **Then build a second one who can kill them**, so that the first one is not
   the only pressure and the stakes are not purely social.

The first antagonist attacks what the character *is*. The second attacks whether
the character *continues*. A campaign needs both and this one was running
neither.

See [`docs/scenarios/the-count-does-not-close.md`](../scenarios/the-count-does-not-close.md)
for this method applied to Sigurd.

## Things that are not plot and have been used as plot

- A maintenance figure, a slipped quarter, a date column. These are **clues**,
  and a clue is the key to a door and never the door.
- An NPC being nice. An NPC being helpful. An NPC having a sad backstory.
- The player learning a fact. Learning is not an event unless somebody is
  trying to stop it.
- A conversation with the station mind, which is the single most inert scene
  available in this setting, because he always answers, never wants anything,
  and cannot be hurt. Budget one per session and never two.

## The test at the end of every session

Count what the player lost. If the answer is nothing, the session did not
happen, however good the prose was.
